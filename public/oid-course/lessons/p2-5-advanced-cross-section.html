2.5 Advanced Cross-Section Econometrics

Doctoral course basis: Econometrics III: Advanced Techniques of Cross-Section Econometrics

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Fully parametric models are a bet: probit bets on normality, Tobit bets on homoskedastic normality, conditional logit bets on IIA. When the bet is wrong, the estimates are inconsistent — not just noisy. Semiparametric methods let you keep the parametric structure where it matters (the coefficients you interpret, the policy counterfactuals you need) while leaving the parts you don't trust — the error distribution, the shape of a nuisance function — unspecified. And when your model is a full structural simulation — an agent-based model of a marketplace, a queuing network with strategic users — the likelihood is intractable, and simulated method of moments is how you estimate it anyway. This lesson is about estimating serious models without making bets you can't afford.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the semiparametric efficiency bound and why relaxing distributional assumptions costs precision but buys robustness.
  • Estimate partially linear and single-index models and interpret their parameters.
  • Describe sieve estimation and how growing the sieve with the sample trades bias against variance.
  • Set up mixed logit to break IIA and allow heterogeneous tastes in discrete choice.
  • Implement simulated method of moments and indirect inference for models with intractable likelihoods.

Core content

Why semiparametric? The efficiency–robustness tradeoff

A parametric model assumes a finite-dimensional parameter plus a known distribution; a nonparametric model assumes almost nothing but converges slowly (curse of dimensionality); a semiparametric model sits between: a finite-dimensional parameter of interest θ plus an infinite-dimensional nuisance component η (an unknown function or distribution). The key theoretical result is the semiparametric efficiency bound (Newey, 1990): even with η unknown, there is a lower bound on the asymptotic variance of regular estimators of θ, achieved by the efficient score — the projection of the parametric score onto the orthocomplement of the nuisance tangent space. In plain terms: you can estimate θ at the parametric √n rate without knowing η, but you pay an efficiency cost relative to knowing the truth. That cost is the price of robustness, and it is usually worth paying: a fully parametric estimator that is inconsistent under misspecification has infinite effective cost. The practical rule: go semiparametric for the components whose misspecification would bias your answer, stay parametric where theory or evidence supports the shape.

Partially linear models

The workhorse: yi = xi′β + g(zi) + εi, with E[εi|xi, zi] = 0 and g(·) an unknown smooth function. You care about β (the effect of a config change); z is a confounder whose functional form you don't trust (time-of-day effects on latency are wiggly, not quadratic). Robinson's (1988) double-residual estimator: (1) nonparametrically regress y on z and each xj on z to get residuals ỹ = y − Ê[y|z], x̃ = x − Ê[x|z]; (2) OLS of ỹ on x̃. Intuition: partial out the nuisance nonparametrically, then run the parametric regression on what's left — a generalization of Frisch–Waugh–Lovell. β̂ is √n-consistent and asymptotically normal under mild smoothness on g, even though ĝ converges slower than √n: the slow nuisance estimation doesn't contaminate the parametric part (an early instance of the orthogonality/debiased-ML idea). For an engineer: model the effect of a routing change on latency while letting the diurnal pattern be fully flexible — no more arguing about which polynomial in hour-of-day is "right."

Single-index models

Binary choice without distributional bets: P(yi=1|xi) = G(xi′β) with G unknown (increasing). Probit assumes G = Φ; if the true link is skewed or heavy-tailed, probit is inconsistent. The single-index model keeps the index structure x′β — coefficients still summarize how covariates trade off — but estimates G nonparametrically. Identification needs a scale normalization (e.g., the coefficient on a continuous regressor fixed at 1, or ||β|| = 1), since G absorbs any rescaling. Estimators include Manski's maximum score (maximizes the number of correct sign predictions — √n-inconsistent under smoothness, converging at the cube-root rate n1/3, a famous nonstandard asymptotic), and smoothed versions (Horowitz) that recover √n rates. The lesson for practice: if your binary-outcome model will drive decisions (churn intervention, fraud flags), check the link — compare probit/logit fits against a single-index estimate, and worry if they disagree.

Sieve estimation

Sieves approximate the infinite-dimensional nuisance by a finite-dimensional space that grows with n: approximate g(z) by Σk=1Kn αkpk(z) — polynomials, splines, wavelets — with Kn → ∞ slowly. Ai and Chen (2003) developed sieve MLE and sieve minimum-distance estimators for general semiparametric problems. The bias–variance logic is transparent: small Kn can't capture g (bias), large Kn overfits (variance); the optimal Kn balances them and is typically chosen by cross-validation or information criteria. Sieves are the econometric cousin of what ML engineers do with basis expansions and wide neural nets — and the theory clarifies exactly what ML practice leaves implicit: the approximation space must grow, but slower than the sample, and inference on θ must account for the sieve approximation error. When you see "we used a flexible ML first stage," the sieve literature is the rigorous version of that sentence.

Mixed logit: breaking IIA with heterogeneous tastes

Recall conditional logit's IIA problem from Lesson 2.2: it forces proportional substitution. Mixed logit (McFadden & Train) lets coefficients vary across decision-makers: decision-maker i has βi ∼ F(β; θ), and Pi(choose j) = ∫ [exij′β/Σk exik′β] dF(β; θ). Integrating over the taste distribution breaks IIA — substitution patterns become flexible because different consumers substitute differently — and the estimated θ describes taste heterogeneity (e.g., the distribution of price sensitivity across cloud customers). The integral has no closed form, so estimation is by simulated maximum likelihood: draw R values βr from F(·; θ), approximate the integral by (1/R)Σr Lij(βr), maximize the simulated log-likelihood. Simulation noise inflates variance, so use many draws (hundreds+) and low-discrepancy sequences (Halton draws beat pseudo-random). Kenneth Train's Discrete Choice Methods with Simulation (free from the author) is the bible here. Application: modeling which instance type each customer chooses as prices and workloads vary — mixed logit captures that startups and enterprises substitute across tiers completely differently.

Simulated method of moments and indirect inference

Some models have no tractable likelihood but are easy to simulate: agent-based marketplaces, dynamic games, queuing systems with strategic users, Rust-style dynamic discrete choice (nested fixed point). The simulated method of moments (SMM) (McFadden 1989; Pakes & Pollard 1989): (1) choose moments m̂ from the real data; (2) for candidate θ, simulate S datasets from the model and compute simulated moments m̃(θ); (3) minimize [m̂ − m̃(θ)]′W[m̂ − m̃(θ)]. If the model is correct and moments identify θ, θ̂SMM is consistent and asymptotically normal; simulation adds a (1 + 1/S) variance inflation factor — simulate a lot (S ≥ 10–20) to make it negligible. Moment choice is the art: pick moments that are sensitive to the parameters you care about and robust to the parts you don't. Indirect inference generalizes: estimate an auxiliary (misspecified but easy) model on real data to get β̂, estimate the same auxiliary model on simulated data to get β̃(θ), and choose θ to match β̃(θ) ≈ β̂. When the auxiliary model is the score of a likelihood, this becomes efficient (Gallant–Tauchen EMM). The through-line: when you can simulate your system but can't write its likelihood, moment-matching on simulated data is how you do structural estimation — the same idea powers "digital twin" calibration of network simulators to measured telemetry.

Worked examples

Example 1: Partially linear model of a config change

You want the effect β of a TCP stack change on flow completion time, but completion times have a strong, wiggly diurnal pattern (z = hour of day) you don't want to model parametrically. Robinson's double-residual approach:

from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor
# partial out z nonparametrically
my = GradientBoostingRegressor().fit(Z, y).predict(Z)
mx = GradientBoostingRegressor().fit(Z, x).predict(Z)
beta = ((x-mx) @ (y-my)) / ((x-mx) @ (x-mx))   # OLS on residuals
# SEs: heteroskedasticity-robust on the residual regression

β̂ = −14 ms (SE 3 ms). A quadratic-in-hour specification had given −9 ms — the parametric diurnal model was absorbing part of the treatment effect because rollout timing correlated with time of day. Takeaway: partial out flexible confounders nonparametrically; parametric shortcuts on nuisance functions can bias the parameter you actually care about.

Example 2: Single-index check on a churn model

Your probit churn model (1 = cancelled) drives a retention campaign. You fit a single-index model with unknown link G and compare predicted probabilities: probit and single-index agree in the middle of the covariate distribution but diverge in the high-risk tail — the true link is heavier-tailed than Φ, so probit understates churn risk for the riskiest accounts, exactly the ones the campaign targets. You switch the targeting model to the semiparametric fit. Takeaway: distributional assumptions bite hardest in the tails, which is where decisions live.

Example 3: Mixed logit for cloud instance choice

50,000 customer-months choosing among standard, memory-optimized, and GPU instances with varying prices. Conditional logit: price coefficient −0.9 for everyone. Mixed logit with βprice,i ∼ N(μ, σ2): μ̂ = −1.1, σ̂ = 0.7 — substantial heterogeneity. The implied substitution: a price hike on standard instances pushes price-sensitive startups to memory-optimized, while enterprises barely move — conditional logit's IIA had forced both segments to substitute proportionally, understating startup churn. Counterfactual revenue under a 10% price increase differs by 18% between the models. Takeaway: when customers differ, mixed logit isn't a refinement — it changes the business answer.

Example 4: SMM calibration of a datacenter network simulator

You have a packet-level simulator of your datacenter fabric with parameters θ = (buffer sizes, ECN thresholds, flow-size distribution parameters) — 12 parameters, no likelihood. Real telemetry gives moments: mean and p99 flow completion times by flow-size bin, link utilization distribution, retransmission rates (28 moments). SMM: for each candidate θ, run the simulator (S = 20 replications), compute simulated moments, minimize the weighted distance. The minimized J-like criterion also serves as a specification check — a huge minimized value says the simulator can't reproduce reality at any parameter, i.e., the model itself is wrong, not just miscalibrated. Takeaway: SMM turns "we have a simulator and we have data" into parameter estimates plus a test of the simulator.

Key results to remember

  • Semiparametric models estimate a finite-dimensional θ at √n rates with an unknown nuisance function; the efficiency bound (Newey 1990) prices the robustness you buy by not specifying the nuisance.
  • Partially linear model y = x′β + g(z) + ε: Robinson's double-residual estimator partials out z nonparametrically, then OLS on residuals — nuisance flexibility without contaminating β.
  • Single-index binary choice P(y=1|x) = G(x′β) drops the probit/logit distributional bet; needs a scale normalization; Manski's maximum score converges at the nonstandard n1/3 rate.
  • Sieve estimation approximates infinite-dimensional nuisances with Kn → ∞ basis terms; choose Kn by cross-validation to balance approximation bias against variance.
  • Mixed logit integrates the logit kernel over a taste distribution, breaking IIA and capturing heterogeneous substitution; estimate by simulated MLE with many (Halton) draws.
  • SMM/indirect inference estimate models with intractable likelihoods by matching simulated moments to data moments; simulation inflates variance by (1 + 1/S); the minimized criterion doubles as a specification test.
  • Rule of thumb: be semiparametric about the components whose misspecification would bias your answer; stay parametric where theory supports the shape.

Check your understanding

  1. In a partially linear model, why doesn't the slow nonparametric convergence rate of ĝ contaminate the √n rate of β̂?
    Answer

    Because of orthogonality: the double-residual construction makes the estimation error in ĝ asymptotically uncorrelated with the score for β (the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell logic). The nuisance error averages out, leaving β̂ √n-consistent and asymptotically normal.

  2. Your mixed logit estimates show σ̂ (taste heterogeneity) near zero. What does this imply?
    Answer

    Tastes are nearly homogeneous, so the model collapses toward conditional logit — but IIA still binds exactly only at σ = 0. Practically, substitution patterns are close to proportional; you could simplify, but keep the mixed logit as a check.

  3. SMM with S = 2 simulation replications gives very noisy estimates. Why, and what's the fix?
    Answer

    Simulation noise inflates the asymptotic variance by (1 + 1/S) = 1.5×. Increase S (10–20+ replications) to drive the factor toward 1; the remaining noise is then negligible relative to sampling variation.

  4. When would you prefer a single-index model over probit for a binary outcome?
    Answer

    When the error distribution is unknown or suspected non-normal/skewed and the decision depends on tail probabilities. Probit is inconsistent under link misspecification; the single-index model keeps the interpretable index structure while estimating the link flexibly.
