7.7 Reinforcement Learning as Dynamic Programming

Bridge lesson — connecting OID core to AI, technology, and electrical systems

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Reinforcement learning is dynamic programming with the model removed: where Lesson 7.5 assumed known transitions P(s'|s,a), RL learns the Bellman fixed point from samples. Q-learning is stochastic approximation applied to the Bellman operator; policy gradients are stochastic gradient ascent on the value function; bandits are the exploration subproblem isolated. If you understood contraction mappings and Bellman equations, RL's core algorithms are one-line corollaries — and their failure modes (divergence with function approximation, exploration collapse) are exactly the places where the DP assumptions break.

Learning objectives

  • Formulate MDPs and derive Q-learning as Robbins–Monro stochastic approximation of the Bellman operator.
  • Explain why tabular Q-learning converges (contraction + martingale noise) and why function approximation can diverge.
  • State and apply the policy gradient theorem; contrast actor–critic with value-based methods.
  • Analyze bandits via UCB and connect the Gittins index to Bayesian optimal exploration.
  • Design an RL formulation for a systems-control problem (scheduling, power, routing).

Core content

MDPs: DP without the model

An MDP is Lesson 7.5's tuple (S, A, P, c, β) with the twist that P and c are unknown — the agent observes only sampled transitions (s, a, c, s'). Define the optimal action-value Q*(s,a) = c(s,a) + βE[min_{a'} Q*(s',a') | s,a]; this is the Bellman optimality equation written per state–action pair, with operator (HQ)(s,a) = c(s,a) + βE[min Q(s',·)]. H is a β-contraction in sup norm, exactly as in Lesson 7.5, so Q* is its unique fixed point. Every value-based RL algorithm is an attempt to find this fixed point from samples.

Q-learning as stochastic approximation

Q-learning (Watkins 1989) updates on each sample: Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α_t [c + β min_{a'} Q(s',a') − Q(s,a)]. Read it as Robbins–Monro stochastic approximation: θ_{t+1} = θ_t + α_t [h(θ_t) + noise_t], where h(Q) = HQ − Q is the mean Bellman residual and the noise is a martingale difference (Lesson 7.4's machinery). Convergence needs the Robbins–Monro step conditions Σα_t = ∞, Σα_t² < ∞, plus infinite visits to every (s,a) — i.e., sufficient exploration. The proof sketch: the contraction pulls the mean dynamics to Q*, while the square-summable steps kill the martingale noise (a stochastic-approximation version of Doob's convergence). This is why Q-learning "just works" in the tabular case — and pinpoints the two things that can break: non-contractive operators and unvisitable states.

The deadly triad: function approximation + bootstrapping + off-policy learning can diverge — Baird's counterexample shows linear Q-learning blowing up. The contraction argument fails because the projection onto the function class composed with H need not be a contraction. Fixes target each leg: target networks slow the bootstrapping, experience replay and importance sampling tame off-policy noise, and gradient-TD / residual methods restore contraction-like guarantees. DQN's engineering (target nets, replay) is best understood as surgery on the triad.

Policy gradients and actor–critic

Parameterize the policy π_θ(a|s) and maximize J(θ) = E[return]. The policy gradient theorem: ∇J(θ) = E_{π_θ}[Σ_t ∇log π_θ(a_t|s_t) · Q^{π_θ}(s_t,a_t)] — the gradient depends on the policy only through the score function and the action-values, not through derivatives of the stationary state distribution (the envelope-theorem-like cancellation is the whole point). REINFORCE samples this directly; actor–critic replaces the sampled return with a learned critic Q_w (baseline subtraction reduces variance without bias), giving the two-timescale stochastic approximation that powers A2C/PPO. Deterministic policy gradients (DDPG) extend this to continuous actions — the setting for power control and robotics.

Bandits: exploration isolated

The multi-armed bandit strips RL to pure exploration: K arms with unknown mean rewards, minimize cumulative regret. UCB (upper confidence bound) plays argmax_a [Q̄_a + √(2ln t / n_a)]: optimism in the face of uncertainty, with O(log T) regret matching the Lai–Robbins lower bound up to constants. Thompson sampling randomizes by posterior probability of optimality — a Bayesian UCB with excellent empirical behavior. The Gittins index (Lesson 7.5) is the exact Bayesian-optimal solution for discounted infinite-horizon bandits: compute one index per arm, always play the largest. Contextual bandits (choose arm given features x) are the workhorse of recommendation and A/B testing at scale — RL with horizon 1.

RL for control and systems

Systems problems are natural MDPs: states are queue lengths / server modes / channel states, actions are scheduling / sleep / power decisions, costs are latency + energy. Average-cost formulations (Lesson 7.5) suit perpetually running systems; constrained MDPs handle SLA constraints via Lagrangian dual methods (Lesson 7.6's duality again). The honest engineering caveats: simulators are biased models (sim-to-real gap), exploration is unsafe on live systems (use offline RL / conservative policy iteration), and sample complexity scales with the effective horizon 1/(1−β) — which is why reward shaping and hierarchical structure matter more than algorithm choice in practice.

Offline RL and the limits of learning from logs

Live exploration on production systems is often unacceptable — you cannot let a router "try" dropping all traffic to learn it is bad. Offline (batch) RL learns solely from logged data, but faces distributional shift: the learned policy visits states the logs never covered, where the critic's values are pure extrapolation. Naive off-policy evaluation then overestimates wildly. Conservative methods (CQL, behavior-regularized policy gradients) penalize deviation from the logging policy or lower-bound values on unseen actions — trading optimality for safety. The theoretical lens is concentrability: offline RL works when the data distribution covers the states the optimal policy visits. For systems engineers the takeaway is concrete: log with exploratory policies (not just the production policy), record the propensities, and treat "train on logs, deploy" as a counterfactual-inference problem (Part 4's territory) before it is a control problem.

Worked examples

Example 1: Q-learning for queue admission control

A server with buffer 10; per slot, admit or reject an arriving job (Bernoulli(0.6) arrivals, geometric service). Holding cost 1 per queued job, rejection cost 8. The optimal policy is threshold (Lesson 7.5 lattice logic). Tabular Q-learning recovers it:

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
B, beta, gamma = 10, 0.6, 0.95
Q = np.zeros((B+1, 2))  # actions: 0=reject, 1=admit
for ep in range(40000):
    s = rng.integers(0, B+1); done = False
    alpha = 0.1/(1+ep/5000)
    for _ in range(200):
        a = rng.integers(2) if rng.random() < 0.1 else int(np.argmin(Q[s]))
        arr = rng.random() < beta; dep = (s > 0) and rng.random() < 0.5
        ns = min(max(s + (a and arr) - dep, 0), B)
        c = s + (8 if (arr and not a) else 0)   # holding + rejection cost
        Q[s,a] += alpha*(c + gamma*np.min(Q[ns]) - Q[s,a])
        s = ns
policy = np.argmin(Q, axis=1)
print("admit(1)/reject(0) by queue length:", policy)
# -> admits when short, rejects when long: a threshold policy, learned

Example 2: UCB on a 3-arm bandit

Arms with true means 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 (Bernoulli). UCB with bonus √(2ln t/n_a): after t = 1000 pulls, the counts concentrate on arm 3 (n_3 ≈ 900+), cumulative regret ≈ Σ Δ_a E[n_a] grows logarithmically — roughly (0.2+0.4)×(8ln1000/0.04...) — in simulation ≈ 25–40, vs. linear regret (~200) for ε-greedy with fixed ε = 0.1. Optimism explores efficiently because the bonus shrinks exactly as 1/√n_a.

Example 3: Policy gradient for power capping

A cluster controller sets a power cap a ∈ {low, high} each 5-minute window; state s = (queue length, electricity price tier). Reward = −(latency penalty + energy cost). A softmax policy π_θ(a|s) trained with actor–critic: the critic learns V(s), the actor ascends ∇log π · (return − V(s)). After training on a week of simulated traces, the policy learns price-aware capping — low caps during price spikes unless queues are long — reproducing the threshold structure a DP would prove, without ever writing a transition model.

Key results to remember

  • Q-learning is Robbins–Monro stochastic approximation of the Bellman operator: Q ← Q + α[c + βminQ' − Q]; contraction + martingale noise + Σα=∞/Σα²<∞ ⇒ convergence (tabular).
  • The deadly triad — function approximation + bootstrapping + off-policy — can diverge; DQN's target networks and replay are fixes aimed at specific legs.
  • Policy gradient theorem: ∇J = E[Σ ∇log π(a|s)·Q^π(s,a)] — no derivative of the state distribution needed.
  • Actor–critic = policy gradient with a learned baseline/critic: lower variance, two-timescale stochastic approximation.
  • UCB achieves O(log T) regret via optimism; Gittins index is the exact Bayesian-optimal index policy for discounted bandits.
  • Average-cost and constrained MDPs are the right formulations for perpetually running systems with SLA constraints — solved via Lesson 7.5/7.6 tools.
  • Every RL algorithm inherits DP's assumptions; when it fails, check which one broke: Markovian state, sufficient exploration, contractive operator.

Check your understanding

  1. Why does tabular Q-learning converge but linear Q-learning may diverge?
    Answer

    Tabular Q-learning is stochastic approximation of a sup-norm contraction, so the mean dynamics converge and square-summable steps kill the martingale noise. With linear function approximation, the composed operator (projection ∘ Bellman) need not be a contraction — the deadly triad — so iterates can diverge (Baird's counterexample).

  2. What role does the critic play in actor–critic, and why is baseline subtraction unbiased?
    Answer

    The critic estimates Q^π or V^π to replace high-variance sampled returns. Subtracting a state-dependent baseline b(s) is unbiased because E[∇log π(a|s)·b(s)] = b(s)·∇E[1] = 0 — the score function has zero mean under π.

  3. A recommender must choose among 5 layouts per visit to maximize clicks. Which framework, and why not full RL?
    Answer

    Contextual bandit: the decision's effect is immediate (horizon 1), so there is no sequential credit assignment to gain from full RL, while bandit algorithms (UCB/Thompson) give optimal exploration–exploitation with far less sample complexity.

  4. Your Q-learning agent on a live router diverges. Name two DP assumptions to check first.
    Answer

    (1) Markovian state — if the state omits relevant history (e.g., traffic phase), the Bellman equation is wrong. (2) Sufficient exploration / step-size conditions — a greedy-only policy stops visiting state–actions, and constant α never kills the noise. Also check for the deadly triad if using function approximation.
