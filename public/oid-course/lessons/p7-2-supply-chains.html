7.2 Supply Chains and Distribution

Built from Wharton OIDD 9410: Distribution Systems Seminar

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

A GPU supply chain runs from foundry to data center through distributors, integrators, and cloud regions; cloud capacity itself flows through a chain — hardware procurement, regional pools, availability zones, on-demand instances. Multi-echelon inventory theory (Clark–Scarf) tells you where to hold safety stock across such a chain; the bullwhip effect explains why order variability explodes upstream in chip procurement; coordination contracts are the formal version of the volume commitments cloud vendors negotiate. This is inventory theory scaled to networks.

Learning objectives

  • Define echelon stock versus installation stock and state the Clark–Scarf decomposition for serial systems.
  • Explain the four causes of the bullwhip effect and how information sharing dampens it.
  • Show how double marginalization arises and how buyback and revenue-sharing contracts coordinate a chain.
  • Apply Littlewood's rule for single-leg revenue management.
  • Map multi-echelon models onto hardware and cloud-capacity chains.

Core content

Multi-echelon inventory: Clark and Scarf (1960)

Consider a serial system: stage 1 supplies stage 2, …, stage N faces customer demand; each stage has a lead time and linear holding/shortage costs. Two ways to count inventory: installation stock is the physical inventory at one stage; echelon stock is the inventory at that stage plus everything downstream (in transit and at lower echelons). Clark and Scarf's breakthrough was to decompose this coupled N-stage problem into N single-stage problems. Define the echelon inventory position at stage j and an induced penalty for starving downstream; then each echelon faces its own convex-cost newsvendor problem, and the optimal policy is an echelon base-stock policy: at each stage, order up to an echelon target S_j. Theorem (Clark–Scarf 1960): under backorders, linear costs, and nested lead times, echelon base-stock policies are optimal for the serial system.

The proof idea: work backwards from the most downstream stage. The downstream stage's optimal cost-to-go, viewed as a function of what the upstream stage delivers, induces an "echelon holding cost" that makes the upstream stage's problem separable. Convexity propagates upstream, so each echelon independently solves a newsvendor-like problem against echelon demand. Axsäter and Rosling (1993) later clarified when installation-stock policies suffice and when echelon information strictly helps: echelon policies dominate when stages can observe downstream positions — the formal justification for centralized visibility in supply chains.

Distribution systems and METRIC

Real distribution is arborescent: one depot feeds many retailers (think: a central cloud region feeding edge zones). Exact optimality is lost, but the METRIC approximation (Sherbrooke) treats each location as facing a compound-Poisson-like demand with mean and variance of resupply delay computed from the depot's backorders, then optimizes base-stock levels location by location. Guaranteed-service models offer a cleaner alternative: each stage quotes a service time to its downstream customer, decoupling stages at the cost of extra safety stock — the modeling choice behind many spare-parts networks for hardware maintenance.

The bullwhip effect

Lee, Padmanabhan, and Whang showed that order variability amplifies upstream even when consumer demand is stable. Four operational causes: (1) demand-signal processing — each stage forecasts from the orders it sees, adding its own safety stock; (2) order batching — EOQ-style batching creates lumpy orders; (3) price fluctuations — promotions pull demand forward; (4) shortage gaming — rationing during shortages induces order inflation. Cachon and Fisher (2000) quantified the value of shared information: with full demand visibility, the supplier can cut inventory substantially while holding service constant. The engineering analogue: without end-to-end telemetry, each layer of a capacity-planning stack (forecast → procure → deploy) pads its own safety margin, and the paddings compound. Shared, granular demand data is the cure in both worlds.

Coordination contracts: curing double marginalization

When a supplier sells to a retailer at wholesale price w > marginal cost c, the retailer faces a higher effective cost and orders less than the chain-optimal quantity — double marginalization. The chain leaves profit on the table because no one owns the total. Cachon and Lariviere (2005) analyze contracts that restore coordination in the newsvendor setting. A buyback contract (w, b): the supplier charges w per unit but buys back leftovers at b, shifting overage risk upstream; with the right (w, b) the retailer's critical fractile equals the chain's, so the decentralized Q* matches the centralized optimum while profits split per bargaining. A revenue-sharing contract (w, φ): the retailer keeps fraction φ of revenue and pays a low w; again, properly chosen parameters align the fractile and divide the surplus. Cachon (2004) extends this to push, pull, and advance-purchase discount contracts, showing how the allocation of inventory risk determines who bears demand uncertainty. For hardware procurement: a cloud vendor's committed-use discount is a quantity-discount contract; a take-or-pay chip agreement is a push contract with risk parked on the buyer.

Revenue management: Littlewood's rule

Revenue management allocates fixed, perishable capacity across fare classes — the same math as allocating a fixed pool of reserved instances across customer tiers. Littlewood's rule (single leg, two classes): accept a low-fare request iff its fare f_L ≥ f_H · P(D_H > remaining seats), i.e., protect seats for high-fare demand exactly when the expected marginal value of protection exceeds the low fare. EMSR heuristics extend this to many classes; network RM (Talluri and van Ryzin) handles itineraries competing for shared legs — the analogue of jobs competing for shared GPU pools with different priorities.

Worked examples

Example 1: Two-echelon Clark–Scarf for edge data centers

A central warehouse (stage 2, lead time 2 weeks) supplies an edge site (stage 1, lead time 1 week) facing Poisson demand at λ = 10 units/week. Holding cost h1 = $5, h2 = $2; backorder cost p = $50. Echelon holding costs: e1 = h1 = 5 (echelon 1), e2 = h1 + h2 = 7? No — echelon 2's incremental cost is h2 = 2, with downstream cost already accounted. Each echelon solves a newsvendor: echelon 1 faces lead-time demand over 1 week (mean 10), fractile = p/(p+e1) = 50/55 ≈ 0.909 → S1 ≈ 14 (Poisson(10) 0.909-quantile). Echelon 2 faces echelon lead-time demand over 2 weeks (mean 20) with its induced fractile → S2 computed from the echelon cost function. The policy: keep echelon inventory positions at S1, S2. The decomposition means the warehouse manager needs only the echelon position, not the full system state.

Example 2: Bullwhip in chip procurement (simulation)

import numpy as np
np.random.seed(0)
T = 200
consumer = 100 + 5*np.random.randn(T)          # stable consumer demand
# each of 3 upstream stages forecasts with moving average and batches x2
orders = consumer.copy()
for stage in range(3):
    ma = np.convolve(orders, np.ones(8)/8, mode='same')
    safety = 2*np.std(orders)
    orders = np.maximum(0, 2*(ma + safety) - np.concatenate([[0], np.cumsum(orders)[:-1]])*0 + ma)
    # simpler: order = 2x smoothed forecast
    orders = 2*ma
print("consumer std:", consumer.std().round(1), " tier-3 order std:", orders.std().round(1))
# consumer std ~5, tier-3 order std ~ 2^3 x smoothed — variance explodes upstream

Even with stable consumer demand (σ ≈ 5), three stages of forecast-and-double batching multiply order variability — the signature bullwhip pattern. Sharing the raw consumer signal (Cachon–Fisher) collapses the amplification.

Example 3: Coordinating with a buyback contract

Supplier cost c = $20/chip, retail price p = $60, demand Uniform[0, 1000]. Chain optimum: fractile = (60−20)/60 = 2/3 → Q_chain = 667. With wholesale w = $40, retailer's fractile = (60−40)/60 = 1/3 → Q = 333: double marginalization. A buyback contract (w = $45, b = $33.33): retailer's underage = 60−45 = 15, overage = 45−33.33 = 11.67, fractile = 15/26.67 = 0.5625... tune b so (p−w)/(p−b) = 2/3: (60−45)/(60−b) = 2/3 → 60−b = 22.5 → b = $37.50. Then the retailer orders 667 — coordination restored, with surplus split by w.

Key results to remember

  • Clark–Scarf (1960): echelon base-stock policies are optimal in serial systems — decompose the chain into per-echelon newsvendors.
  • Echelon stock = own inventory + everything downstream; it is the right state variable for multi-echelon control.
  • Bullwhip: order variance amplifies upstream through signal processing, batching, price fluctuations, and shortage gaming; shared demand information dampens it (Cachon–Fisher 2000).
  • Double marginalization: wholesale pricing above marginal cost shrinks orders below the chain optimum.
  • Buyback and revenue-sharing contracts (Cachon–Lariviere 2005) coordinate the chain by realigning the retailer's critical fractile with the chain's.
  • Littlewood's rule: protect capacity for high-value demand when f_L < f_H · P(D_H > x) — the basis of revenue management and priority allocation.
  • Cloud and hardware capacity chains exhibit all of these: pad-at-every-layer is bullwhip; committed-use discounts are coordination contracts.

Check your understanding

  1. Why does the Clark–Scarf decomposition use echelon stock rather than installation stock as the state variable?
    Answer

    Echelon stock internalizes everything downstream, so each stage's decision problem becomes separable: the downstream cost-to-go induces an echelon holding cost that makes the upstream stage face an independent newsvendor problem. Installation stock alone cannot capture the downstream consequences of a stockout.

  2. Name two bullwhip causes and one mitigation for each.
    Answer

    Order batching → smaller, more frequent orders (or continuous replenishment). Demand-signal processing → share point-of-sale/actual demand data downstream-to-upstream (Cachon–Fisher). Price fluctuations → everyday low pricing instead of promotions. Shortage gaming → allocate on past sales, not inflated orders.

  3. A supplier offers w = $30, buyback b = $10; retail price p = $50, cost c = $20. Does this coordinate? What b would?
    Answer

    Chain fractile = (50−20)/50 = 0.6. Retailer fractile = (50−30)/(50−10) = 0.5 ≠ 0.6 — not coordinated. Need (50−30)/(50−b) = 0.6 → 50−b = 33.33 → b = $16.67.

  4. How is Littlewood's rule analogous to spot-vs-reserved instance pricing?
    Answer

    Reserved (high-fare) demand should displace on-demand/spot (low-fare) requests exactly when the expected value of the reserved capacity exceeds the spot price — same protection-level logic, with fares replaced by margins per instance-hour.
