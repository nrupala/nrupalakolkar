6.2 Machine Learning

Doctoral course basis: Machine Learning

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

CIS 5200 is Penn's mathematically demanding introduction to machine learning — representation, loss functions, optimization — and it is the PhD toolkit behind every applied lesson that follows. As an AI/EE engineer you already fit models to data; this lesson gives you the theory that tells you why a model generalizes, when regularization is doing the work, and how the classical methods (SVMs, forests, boosting, EM) relate to the deep nets in Lesson 6.3. The bias–variance tradeoff and generalization bounds here are the same ideas that make the deep-learning puzzles of 6.3 interesting.

Learning objectives

  • State the empirical risk minimization framework and decompose expected error into bias, variance, and noise.
  • Explain VC dimension, Rademacher complexity, and PAC bounds as answers to "why does training error predict test error?"
  • Derive least squares and logistic regression from MLE, and contrast L1 vs L2 regularization geometrically.
  • Compare generative vs discriminative classifiers and explain the kernel trick and the EM algorithm.
  • Describe Q-learning and the exploration–exploitation tradeoff for sequential decision problems.

Core content

Empirical risk minimization and the bias–variance tradeoff

CIS 5200 organizes every algorithm around three choices: representation (the hypothesis class), loss function (what counts as a mistake), and optimization (how we search). Empirical risk minimization (ERM) picks the hypothesis minimizing average loss on the training sample, as a stand-in for the true risk E[loss]. For squared loss, the expected test error of a learner decomposes exactly: E[(y − f̂(x))²] = bias² + variance + noise, where bias is the error of the average model, variance is how much the fitted model wobbles across training samples, and noise is irreducible. Complex hypothesis classes lower bias but raise variance — the tradeoff that motivates every form of regularization. Overfitting is ERM succeeding at the wrong objective: driving training loss down by fitting noise.

Generalization theory: VC, Rademacher, PAC

Why should low training error imply low test error? Statistical learning theory answers with uniform convergence: with probability at least 1 − δ, true risk ≤ training error + complexity penalty + √(log(1/δ)/n). The VC dimension of a hypothesis class is the largest number of points it can shatter (label in all 2^n ways); linear classifiers in d dimensions have VC dimension d + 1. The bound grows with √(VC/n): more data or simpler classes tighten it. Rademacher complexity refines this by measuring how well the class can correlate with random noise labels on the actual data — a data-dependent complexity. PAC learning (probably approximately correct) packages the guarantee: a class is PAC-learnable if a learner can, with polynomial samples, probably (≈ 1 − δ) find an approximately (ε) optimal hypothesis. The honest engineering reading: these bounds are usually too loose to predict actual error, but they correctly rank the levers — sample size, class complexity, and margin all push in the right directions.

Linear models, regularization, and the lasso geometry

Least squares has a closed form: θ̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy, the normal equations, which is also the MLE under Gaussian noise. Logistic regression models P(y=1|x) = σ(θᵀx) and is fit by maximizing the (concave) log-likelihood with gradient methods — no closed form, but a convex problem. Regularization constrains or penalizes θ: L2 (ridge) adds λ||θ||₂², L1 (lasso, Tibshirani 1996) adds λ||θ||₁, L0 penalizes the count of nonzero coefficients. The geometry explains the lasso's fame: the L1 constraint region is a diamond whose corners sit on the axes, so the elliptical loss contours tend to touch at a corner — setting coefficients exactly to zero, i.e., automatic feature selection. The L2 ball is round, so contact happens off-axis: shrinkage without sparsity. Minimum description length (MDL) gives the information-theoretic view: prefer the model minimizing (bits to describe the model) + (bits to describe the data given the model) — a formalization of Occam's razor that justifies penalized likelihood.

Losses vs metrics, generative vs discriminative

The loss you optimize is not the metric you report. Hinge loss trains SVMs, logistic loss trains calibrated probabilities, squared loss trains conditional means — while the business cares about precision/recall, ROC/AUC, or asymmetric costs (a missed fault vs a false alarm in a power grid have very different prices). Choose the loss for optimization tractability and calibration, then tune the decision threshold for the metric. Naive Bayes is generative: it models P(x|y) and P(y), then applies Bayes' rule — strong independence assumptions, but it needs little data and handles missing features gracefully. Logistic regression is discriminative: it models P(y|x) directly. The classic comparison: generative models reach their (higher) asymptotic error faster with little data; discriminative models reach lower error with abundant data.

SVMs, kernels, trees, forests, boosting

Support vector machines (Cortes and Vapnik 1995) maximize the margin — the distance from the decision boundary to the nearest training points — which VC theory ties directly to generalization. The dual formulation depends on the data only through inner products, enabling the kernel trick: replace xᵢᵀxⱼ with K(xᵢ, xⱼ) = φ(xᵢ)ᵀφ(xⱼ) to fit a linear boundary in a rich implicit feature space (Mercer's theorem characterizes valid kernels). Decision trees partition the feature space greedily; bagging averages many trees fit on bootstrap samples to kill variance; random forests (Breiman 2001) add random feature subsets per split, decorrelating the trees so the averaging works better. Boosting (Freund and Schapire 1997, AdaBoost) takes the opposite route: sequentially fit weak learners to reweighted data emphasizing past mistakes, converting weak learners into a strong one — reducing bias, with a margin-based explanation for its resistance to overfitting.

Latent variables: EM, mixtures, HMMs

The EM algorithm (Dempster, Laird and Rubin 1977) fits models with hidden variables by alternating an E-step (compute the expected complete-data log-likelihood under the current parameters) and an M-step (maximize it). For Gaussian mixtures, EM soft-assigns points to clusters (E-step: responsibilities) then refits each Gaussian to its weighted points (M-step) — k-means (MacQueen 1967) is the hard-assignment limit. Hidden Markov models extend the idea to sequences: EM (the Baum–Welch algorithm) learns transition and emission probabilities for latent states — the classical machinery behind speech recognition and any regime-switching signal model. PCA, by contrast, is the linear dimensionality-reduction workhorse: project onto the top eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, keeping maximal variance per dimension.

Reinforcement learning fundamentals

RL frames sequential decisions as a Markov decision process: states, actions, transition probabilities, rewards, discount factor. The Bellman optimality equation characterizes the optimal value function, and Q-learning (Watkins and Dayan 1992) learns the optimal action-value function off-policy via the temporal-difference update Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α[r + γ max Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a)], converging under standard stochastic-approximation conditions. The exploration–exploitation tradeoff — ε-greedy, UCB, Thompson sampling — is the price of learning while acting. Deep RL (Mnih et al. 2015) scales Q-learning with neural function approximation plus experience replay and target networks to stabilize training. For the engineer: RL is the right frame whenever actions change the data you will see next — adaptive control, scheduling, routing — and the wrong frame when you have abundant i.i.d. labeled data.

Model selection discipline

Cross-validation estimates test error by rotating held-out folds; use it to choose hyperparameters, then report once on a locked test set. The cardinal sin is letting test data influence any modeling choice — that turns your test error into another training error. Regularization strength, architecture, and preprocessing are all selected on validation, never on test.

Worked examples

Example 1: Lasso geometry on a 2-feature regression

Predicting transformer failure risk from features x1 (load, scaled) and x2 (a noisy duplicate of x1, correlation 0.95), n = 200. OLS gives θ̂ = (3.1, −0.4): the collinearity splits the signal unstably. Ridge (λ = 1) shrinks both: θ̂ ≈ (1.5, 1.3) — the round L2 ball pulls the elliptical loss contours toward the origin along both axes. Lasso (λ = 1) returns θ̂ ≈ (2.6, 0): the diamond constraint's corner sits on the x1 axis, so the optimum zeroes out the redundant feature. Same data, same loss; the penalty geometry decides whether you get shrinkage (ridge) or selection (lasso). In Python: 

from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge, Lasso
Ridge(alpha=1.0).fit(X, y).coef_   # ~[1.5, 1.3]: shrinks both
Lasso(alpha=1.0).fit(X, y).coef_   # ~[2.6, 0.0]: selects one

Example 2: Bias–variance with k-nearest neighbors

True signal y = sin(2πx) + noise (σ = 0.3), n = 60 training points. With k = 1, training error ≈ 0.02 but test error ≈ 0.28: near-zero bias, huge variance — the model memorizes noise. With k = 25, training error ≈ 0.16, test error ≈ 0.17: higher bias (oversmoothing flattens the sine peaks), much lower variance. The test-error curve is U-shaped in 1/k, bottoming near k = 5 with test error ≈ 0.12. This is the tradeoff made visible: model complexity is a knob, and the best setting is interior — which is exactly what cross-validation finds without knowing the truth.

Example 3: The kernel trick on concentric rings

Sensor readings form two concentric rings in 2-D: class A at radius 1.0 ± 0.1, class B at radius 2.0 ± 0.1. No line separates them — a linear SVM gets ~50 percent. Map φ(x) = (x1, x2, x1² + x2²): in this 3-D space the classes separate along the third coordinate (values ≈ 1 vs ≈ 4), and a linear boundary there is a circle in the original space. The kernel trick computes this without ever forming φ: with the RBF kernel K(x,z) = exp(−γ||x − z||²), the SVM implicitly works in an infinite-dimensional feature space and achieves ~99 percent. Mercer's theorem guarantees the kernel corresponds to some inner product, so the convex SVM machinery carries over unchanged — this is why kernels were the deep learning of the 2000s.

Example 4: Q-learning on a 3-state line

States {0, 1, 2}, actions {left, right}, reward +10 for entering state 2, −1 per step, γ = 0.9, α = 0.5, Q initialized to 0. Episode 1 wanders: from state 1, action right reaches 2, reward 10: Q(1,right) ← 0 + 0.5[10 + 0.9·0 − 0] = 5. Next visit to state 0, action right reaches 1 with reward −1: Q(0,right) ← 0 + 0.5[−1 + 0.9·max Q(1,·) − 0] = 0.5[−1 + 0.9·5] = 1.75. Value backs up one step per visit — temporal-difference learning propagates the reward backward along the trajectory. After enough ε-greedy episodes, Q(0,right) → the discounted optimal return and the greedy policy walks straight to state 2. The same backup, with neural nets and replay, is DQN.

Key results to remember

  • ERM = representation + loss + optimization; expected squared error = bias² + variance + irreducible noise.
  • Generalization bounds: test error ≤ train error + complexity(VC dimension, Rademacher) + confidence term; PAC learning formalizes "probably approximately correct."
  • L1 (lasso) gives sparsity via the diamond geometry; L2 (ridge) gives shrinkage; MDL justifies penalized likelihood as compression.
  • Optimize a tractable loss (hinge, logistic, squared) but evaluate and threshold on the business metric (precision/recall, AUC, asymmetric costs).
  • SVMs maximize the margin (a generalization lever); the kernel trick fits linear models in implicit high-dimensional spaces; Mercer's theorem says which kernels are legal.
  • Random forests reduce variance by averaging decorrelated trees; boosting (AdaBoost) reduces bias by sequentially reweighting mistakes.
  • EM alternates expected log-likelihood (E-step) and maximization (M-step); Gaussian mixtures and HMMs are its canonical applications; k-means is its hard limit.
  • Q-learning's TD update backs value up one step per visit and converges off-policy; exploration (ε-greedy, UCB) is the cost of learning while acting.

Check your understanding

  1. Your lasso model zeroes out a sensor feature that ridge keeps with a small weight. Explain geometrically.
    Answer

    The L1 constraint is a diamond with corners on the axes; the loss ellipses most plausibly touch at a corner, setting that coefficient exactly to zero. The L2 ball is round, so contact is generically off-axis: both coefficients shrink but neither vanishes.

  2. A classifier has 99 percent training accuracy and 70 percent test accuracy. Which term of the bias–variance decomposition dominates, and what are two fixes?
    Answer

    Variance dominates — the model fits training noise. Fixes: add regularization (L1/L2, dropout), reduce hypothesis-class complexity, or get more training data; tune on a validation set, not the test set.

  3. Why can an RBF-kernel SVM separate data that no line can?
    Answer

    The kernel implicitly maps data into a high- (infinite-) dimensional feature space where a linear boundary exists; by Mercer's theorem the SVM dual needs only inner products, which the kernel computes directly without forming the map.

  4. In Q-learning, what does the term r + γ max Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) represent, and why is learning called "off-policy"?
    Answer

    It is the temporal-difference error: the surprise between the current value estimate and the one-step-bootstrapped target. It is off-policy because the update targets the optimal (greedy) action value regardless of the exploratory behavior policy that generated the transition.

  5. When would you prefer Naive Bayes over logistic regression?
    Answer

    With very little training data or many missing features: the generative model converges to its (higher) asymptotic error faster, needs less data, and handles missing inputs naturally — at the cost of the naive independence assumption.
