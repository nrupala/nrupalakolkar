5.6 Psychological Foundations of Management Research

Doctoral course basis: Psychological Foundations of Research in Management

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

AI systems do not operate in a vacuum — they land in teams, organizations, and professions with their own motivation structures, identities, and norms. Whether engineers adopt your tool, whether operators trust the automation, whether a postmortem produces learning or blame: these are psychological and organizational phenomena, and there is a century of theory about them. MGMT 9330 surveys the core psychology that management research borrows — motivation, attitudes, group processes, identity, culture. For an engineer, this is a toolkit for the human side of sociotechnical systems: designing jobs people stay in, teams that learn from failure, and AI deployments that fit how people actually work rather than how the org chart says they should.

Learning objectives

  • Summarize the core motivation theories (job characteristics, self-determination, social information processing) and their design implications.
  • Explain social identity theory and its predictions for team and organizational behavior.
  • Describe psychological safety and emotional contagion as group-level mechanisms.
  • Evaluate when and how to borrow a psychology theory for a technology problem without misusing it.

Core content

Motivation: designing work people will do well

Hackman and Oldham's (1976) job characteristics model says motivation flows from five core job dimensions — skill variety, task identity (doing a whole piece of work), task significance, autonomy, and feedback — through three psychological states: experienced meaningfulness, experienced responsibility, and knowledge of results. Jobs scoring high on these dimensions produce internal motivation, satisfaction, and performance. The model is a design checklist for engineering roles: a "ticket-closing" role stripped of autonomy and feedback will underperform a role with the same pay but whole-problem ownership — which is exactly what the best platform and ML teams rediscover when they organize around end-to-end ownership.

Two complements matter. Self-determination theory distinguishes intrinsic motivation (autonomy, competence, relatedness) from extrinsic rewards and warns that poorly designed incentives crowd out intrinsic motivation — paying per ticket closed can destroy the craftsmanship motive that made the team good. Relatedness deserves emphasis for distributed engineering teams: remote and hybrid work thins the informal interactions that sustain belonging, so deliberate rituals (demo days, rotating pair assignments, visible appreciation of maintenance work) are not perks but motivational infrastructure. Salancik and Pfeffer's (1978) social information processing approach adds that job attitudes are socially constructed: people infer how they feel about their work from cues around them — what peers say, what managers attend to. Attitudes toward a new AI tool are therefore not just a function of the tool; they are a function of the social signals surrounding its rollout.

Identity: who "we" are shapes what we do

Tajfel and Turner's social identity theory starts from a minimal finding: people favor their own group even when groups are arbitrary. Individuals derive self-esteem from group memberships and act to maintain a positive distinctiveness for their in-groups. Ashforth and Mael (1989) brought this into organizations: identification with a team, profession, or company predicts cooperation, citizenship behavior, and resistance to change. For technology work the predictions are concrete: engineers identify with their craft ("I am an ML engineer") and their team, and interventions framed as threats to that identity — "the AI will do your job" — trigger defensive resistance, while framings that affirm identity — "this tool makes you a 10× engineer" — recruit it. Open-source communities, incident-response teams, and platform migrations all run on identity dynamics as much as on incentives.

Group processes: safety and contagion

Edmondson (1999) showed that psychological safety — the shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking — predicts learning behavior in work teams: speaking up about errors, asking for help, experimenting. In her hospital studies, the teams with more reported errors were the better teams — they reported because they felt safe, which let them learn. The implication for blameless postmortems and AI incident reviews is direct: the review format is a psychological-safety intervention, and a review that punishes candor teaches the team to hide the next failure. Barsade (2002) adds emotional contagion: moods ripple through groups and measurably change cooperation and performance — the "ripple effect." A frustrated tech lead's affect is not private; it is a broadcast that degrades the team's decision quality, which is an argument for treating on-call load and meeting tone as performance infrastructure.

Culture and how to borrow theory well

Schein's (1985) model of organizational culture — artifacts (visible), espoused values (stated), and basic underlying assumptions (tacit) — explains why "we value reliability" on a slide deck coexists with shipping pressure that punishes caution: the basic assumption, not the espoused value, governs behavior. Changing what an on-call culture rewards requires changing the assumption, usually by changing what gets promoted and praised. Status and influence dynamics compound this: in technical teams, status accrues to those who ship visible features, while reliability and maintenance work is lower-status but higher-leverage — a status hierarchy that silently steers effort away from what the system needs most. Rebalancing requires making invisible work visible (reliability scorecards, maintenance demos) so the status system rewards it.

Finally, MGMT 9330's meta-lesson: borrowing theory is a skill with failure modes. Borrow well by (1) importing the construct definitions intact — "psychological safety" means the team's shared belief, not generic niceness; (2) respecting boundary conditions — job-characteristics effects are weaker where workers lack the needed skills; (3) testing the mechanism, not just the label — measure the psychological state, not only the outcome; and (4) avoiding construct drift, where a precise term becomes a vague synonym for "good." A theory used precisely is a scalpel; used loosely, it is a slogan. When you cite a psychology finding to justify a product or process decision, ask whether you have its definition, its boundary conditions, and its mechanism — or just its name.

Worked examples

Example 1: Redesigning an on-call role with job characteristics

Pager duty scores terribly on the Hackman–Oldham dimensions: low autonomy (alerts dictate the night), fragmented task identity (interruptions, not whole problems), and delayed feedback. Predictable result: burnout and attrition, which the model attributes to the job design, not the people. Redesign along the five dimensions: give the on-call engineer ownership of a reliability improvement project each rotation (task identity, significance), authority to page upstream service owners (autonomy), and a weekly reliability scorecard they own (feedback). Same alerts; structurally different job.

Example 2: Psychological safety in AI incident postmortems

After a model serving outage, the postmortem lead opens with: "The deploy checklist was skipped — who approved that?" Candor collapses; the next incident's contributing causes go unreported. The Edmondson-informed redesign: the facilitator states the ground rule ("we assume everyone made the best decision they could with the information they had"), separates the timeline reconstruction (no blame language) from the action items, and the manager shares their own recent mistake first. Reported near-misses rise — which is the metric going up because learning is working, the hospital-teams result replayed in production.

Example 3: Identity framing for a platform migration

You are migrating teams to a new internal ML platform. Frame A: "The old workflow is deprecated; migrate by Q3" — coded as a threat to craft identity ("our expertise is obsolete"), triggering resistance and shadow tooling. Frame B: "The platform handles the undifferentiated plumbing so your team can own the modeling that only you can do" — affirms identity (we are modelers, not YAML maintainers) and reframes migration as status-consistent. Social identity theory predicts Frame B recruits the very group pride that Frame A puts on the defensive; adoption metrics, not just messaging, should be the test.

Key results to remember

  • Job characteristics model (Hackman & Oldham): skill variety, task identity, significance, autonomy, and feedback drive motivation through meaningfulness, responsibility, and knowledge of results.
  • Extrinsic incentives can crowd out intrinsic motivation; job attitudes are socially constructed from surrounding cues (Salancik & Pfeffer).
  • Social identity theory (Tajfel & Turner; Ashforth & Mael): group memberships shape self-esteem and behavior — frame technology change as identity-consistent, not identity-threatening.
  • Psychological safety (Edmondson) predicts team learning: blameless postmortems are safety interventions, and rising near-miss reports signal learning working.
  • Emotional contagion (Barsade): affect ripples through teams and changes performance — on-call load and leadership tone are performance infrastructure.
  • Culture lives in tacit assumptions (Schein), not slide decks; change what gets rewarded to change the assumption.
  • Borrow theory precisely: import definitions intact, respect boundary conditions, test mechanisms, and resist construct drift from scalpel to slogan.

Check your understanding

  1. An ML team has high pay but high attrition; engineers describe the work as "endless ticket triage." Diagnose with the job characteristics model and propose two redesigns.
    Answer

    Low task identity (fragments, not whole problems), low autonomy, weak feedback on results → low experienced meaningfulness and responsibility → attrition despite pay. Redesigns: end-to-end ownership of a model or service (task identity), and engineer-owned quality scorecards with user-impact feedback (knowledge of results).

  2. Why did Edmondson's better hospital teams report more errors, and what is the analogue for software incident reviews?
    Answer

    Psychological safety made reporting safe, so more errors surfaced and got fixed — the reporting rate measured candor, not incompetence. The software analogue: blameless postmortems should expect reported near-misses to rise initially; suppressing them through blame teaches concealment.

  3. You want to cite "psychological safety" to justify a new code-review process. What does borrowing the theory well require?
    Answer

    Use Edmondson's definition (shared belief that interpersonal risk-taking is safe), not generic niceness; respect boundary conditions (safety enables learning behaviors — it does not replace standards); and measure the mechanism (do reviewers actually raise objections?) rather than just renaming the process.
