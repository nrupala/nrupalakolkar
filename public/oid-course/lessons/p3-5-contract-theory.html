3.5 Contract Theory and Mechanism Design

Built from Wharton BEPP 9320: Contract Theory and Applications

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Every incentive system you build is a contract: the payment rule for crowdsourced labelers, the SLA with penalties for a cloud provider, the token rewards for validators in a blockchain, the commission structure for a sales team, the loss function you train an agent against. Contract theory tells you what can and cannot be achieved when the other party knows things you don't (adverse selection) or takes actions you can't see (moral hazard) — and mechanism design tells you how to write the rules so that self-interested agents produce the outcome you want. If Lesson 3.2 was about predicting strategic behavior, this lesson is about engineering it.

Learning objectives

  • Classify a contracting friction as moral hazard, adverse selection, or holdup, and write the relevant constraints.
  • State Holmström's informativeness principle and derive its implications for performance pay.
  • Apply the revelation principle and construct screening menus under single crossing.
  • Explain Myerson's optimal-mechanism logic and the role of virtual valuations.
  • Analyze incomplete contracts: how asset ownership and renegotiation shape investment incentives.

Core content

The taxonomy: moral hazard, adverse selection, holdup

Classify the friction before writing the model — the entire analysis flows from this step. Moral hazard: hidden action after contracting. The principal hires an agent; the agent chooses unobservable effort e at cost c(e); output x is stochastic with distribution F(x|e). The principal designs a wage schedule w(x) to maximize expected surplus subject to two constraints: participation (IR) — the agent's expected utility must exceed the outside option ū — and incentive compatibility (IC) — the recommended effort must maximize the agent's expected utility given w(·). The first-order approach replaces IC with the agent's first-order condition, valid under the usual concavity assumptions (Rogerson/Jewitt conditions).

Adverse selection: hidden type before contracting. The agent knows her type θ (ability, cost, risk); the principal offers a menu of contracts {(q(θ), t(θ))} and types self-select. Signaling (Lesson 3.2) is the mirror image where the informed party moves first. Holdup: noncontractible investment with ex post bargaining. Two parties invest, then bargain over the surplus; because investment is sunk and noncontractible, each gets only a share of the marginal return and underinvests — the problem asset ownership solves.

Moral hazard: the informativeness principle

Holmström (1979) asks: which performance measures should the contract condition on? The informativeness principle: any signal that is informative about the agent's effort — i.e., that shifts the likelihood ratio f_e(x|e)/f(x|e) — should enter the contract, and any signal uninformative about effort should not. Formally, if signal y is statistically sufficient for (x, y) with respect to effort, the optimal contract conditions only on y. The intuition is pure statistics: the contract is an estimator of effort, and you use every informative signal, weighting by precision.

The practical bite is large: pay should depend on relative performance (filter out common shocks — tournament theory), on leading indicators that predict effort, and never on pure noise (which only loads risk onto a risk-averse agent, forcing a compensating risk premium). Holmström and Milgrom (1987) show when linear contracts are optimal: with exponential utility, normal noise, and continuous effort over time (or many tasks), the optimal dynamic contract aggregates to a linear payment in total output, w = α + β·x. This is the theoretical backbone of every linear commission, piece rate, and token-per-unit reward scheme — linearity isn't naive, it's optimal under those distributional conditions, and the slope β trades off incentives against risk-sharing (β = 1/(1 + r·σ²·c''), falling in risk aversion r, noise σ², and cost curvature).

Multitask moral hazard is the warning label: when the agent allocates effort across tasks and only some are measurable, strong incentives on the measurable task distort effort away from the unmeasurable one (teaching to the test; labelers optimizing throughput while quality collapses). The fix is muted incentives, subjective evaluation, or better measurement — never just "more β."

Adverse selection and mechanism design: the revelation principle

The revelation principle is mechanism design's great simplification: for any mechanism and any equilibrium, there exists a direct revelation mechanism — "report your type, I'll implement the equilibrium outcome for that report" — that is incentive compatible (truth-telling is optimal) and achieves the same outcome. So the designer can restrict attention to direct truthful mechanisms without loss of generality. The problem becomes: choose allocation rule q(θ) and transfer rule t(θ) to maximize the principal's objective subject to IC (each type prefers its own bundle) and IR (each type prefers participating).

With single crossing (higher types value the allocation more at the margin — the Spence–Mirrlees condition), IC reduces to monotonicity of q(θ) plus an envelope condition pinning down transfers: t(θ) = t(θ_low) + ∫ marginal surplus. The principal distorts the allocation downward for all but the top type to reduce the information rents paid to higher types — "no distortion at the top, distortion below." Rothschild and Stiglitz (1976) apply this to insurance: competitive insurers offer a menu {(coverage, premium)}; high-risk types choose full coverage at high premium, low-risk types choose partial coverage (a deductible) at low premium — the deductible screens, exactly as in Lesson 3.2's screening example.

Myerson (1981) is the revelation principle's most famous application: the optimal auction (Lesson 3.2) is derived by choosing the allocation and payment rules to maximize revenue subject to IC and IR, yielding allocation by virtual valuation ψ(v) = v − (1−F(v))/f(v). The term (1−F(v))/f(v) is the information rent — what the seller must leave to type v to keep higher types honest. Every mechanism-design problem has this structure: efficiency wants the surplus-maximizing allocation; IC forces you to pay information rents; the optimum trades them off, distorting the allocation to cut rents.

Incomplete contracts and holdup: Hart and Moore

Sometimes the friction isn't hidden information but noncontractibility: the parties cannot write enforceable contracts on the relevant variables (quality of innovation, effort in a partnership, future states too complex to describe). Grossman–Hart–Moore (Hart and Moore 1988) model this: parties make relationship-specific investments, then bargain ex post over the surplus (Nash bargaining, 50-50 split of gains from trade). Anticipating that half the marginal return will be expropriated, each underinvests — holdup.

The remedy is asset ownership: the owner of a physical (or digital) asset holds residual control rights — the right to decide all uses not specified in the contract — which improves her ex post bargaining position and hence her ex ante investment incentive. The optimal ownership structure allocates each asset to the party whose investment matters most. This is the theory of the firm: firms exist (as ownership structures) because contracts are incomplete. For technology: who should own the training data, the model weights, the platform API? The Hart–Moore answer: allocate residual control to whoever's noncontractible investment is most critical — the party you most need to incentivize. Data-ownership disputes, platform–developer power struggles, and "who owns the fine-tuned model" are holdup problems wearing modern clothes.

Applications: regulation, insurance, price discrimination, financial contracting

The machinery is portable. Regulation: the regulator (principal) sets price/quality menus for a utility with unknown costs (adverse selection à la Laffont–Tirole) — cost-plus contracts insure but dull incentives; fixed-price contracts sharpen incentives but load risk. Insurance: deductibles and copays screen risk types and tame moral hazard simultaneously. Price discrimination: the monopolist's menu {(quantity, price)} is a screening mechanism — the "damaged goods" version (slower shipping, ad-supported tier) exists to separate high- from low-willingness-to-pay customers, not because it's cheaper to produce. Financial contracting: debt vs. equity as securities that allocate control and cash-flow rights under moral hazard (the entrepreneur's risk-shifting) — convertible notes and vesting schedules are contracts engineered around IC constraints. In each case, write IR and IC first; the economics follows.

Worked examples

Example 1: Paying crowdsourced labelers (moral hazard)

You pay labelers per image; effort (carefulness) is unobservable; you observe labels, and can audit a sample against gold-standard items. The informativeness principle says: condition pay on the audit score (informative about effort), and also on relative performance vs. other labelers on the same batch (filters common shock — ambiguous images hurt everyone). Don't condition on raw throughput alone — that's the multitask trap: labelers will race through images and quality collapses. A Holmström–Milgrom-style linear contract: w = α + β·(audit accuracy) − γ·(peer-relative error rate). Set β from the risk–incentive tradeoff: labelers are risk-averse over income variance, so β < 1; noisier audits → smaller β → more base pay α. And the audit sample size is a design variable: more gold items = more precise signal = stronger optimal incentives, at the cost of gold-labeling expense — a measurement-investment problem the theory prices exactly.

Example 2: Screening cloud customers with a menu

A cloud provider faces two customer types: startups (low willingness to pay, elastic demand) and enterprises (high willingness to pay, need SLAs). It cannot observe type, so it screens with a menu: (Basic: no SLA, $0.10/GB) and (Pro: 99.99% SLA with penalties, $0.25/GB). Single crossing holds: enterprises value the SLA more at the margin. IC requires the enterprise prefers Pro: v_E(SLA) − 0.25 ≥ v_E(no SLA) − 0.10, and the startup prefers Basic. The provider distorts Basic downward (throttled support, no SLA — "no distortion at the top," since Pro is efficient for enterprises; distortion below, to make Basic unattractive to enterprises and cut their information rent). The ad-supported / free tier of every SaaS product is this menu: the degraded version exists to protect the premium price, not to serve the low type efficiently. Check monotonicity (higher type gets weakly more allocation) before claiming optimality — if it fails, iron (pool types).

Example 3: The informativeness principle in ML monitoring

A principal (you) deploys an ML contractor (a vendor, or an RL agent) to maximize a business metric; you observe the metric plus auxiliary signals. The vendor's effort is unobservable. Informativeness says: include in the payment/performance review every signal informative about effort — e.g., input-data quality logs (did the vendor feed clean data?), latency traces, ablation results — and exclude pure noise (e.g., a market-wide demand shock that moves the metric but not effort). Concretely, regress the metric on observable shocks and contract on the residual: w = α + β·(metric − E[metric | shocks]). This is relative performance evaluation, and it's why good ML contracts (and good employee reviews) benchmark against baselines and control for confounders rather than rewarding raw outcomes. The failure mode to avoid: conditioning on a signal the agent can game (Goodhart) — informativeness assumes the signal distribution given effort is fixed; if the agent can shift the signal without effort, it's not informative, it's a vulnerability.

Example 4: Who owns the fine-tuned model? (holdup)

A startup fine-tunes a foundation-model provider's base model for medical imaging. Both invest noncontractibly: the provider improves the base model; the startup curates rare labeled data and clinical validation. Model quality — and whose effort produced it — can't be contracted on ex ante. Ex post they bargain over the medical product's surplus. If the provider owns the fine-tuned weights (residual control), it can walk away and the startup's data investment is held up → the startup underinvests in curation. If the startup owns them, the provider underinvests in base-model improvements for this domain. Hart–Moore: give ownership to the party whose marginal investment matters more. If curated clinical data is the bottleneck (it usually is — base models are general, medical labels are scarce), the startup should own the fine-tune, possibly with the provider holding a revenue share or a buyback option. Every "platform vs. developer" fight over API access, app-store ownership, and model weights is this bargaining game — structure ownership ex ante to protect the investment you can't contract on.

Key results to remember

  • Classify first: hidden action = moral hazard (IR + IC constraints), hidden type = adverse selection (screening menus), noncontractible investment + ex post bargaining = holdup.
  • Holmström (1979) informativeness principle: condition the contract on every signal informative about effort (likelihood-ratio test); exclude pure noise; use relative performance to filter common shocks.
  • Holmström–Milgrom (1987): linear contracts are optimal under exponential utility + normal noise + continuous effort; slope β = 1/(1 + rσ²c′′) trades incentives against risk.
  • Multitask warning: strong incentives on measurable tasks distort effort away from unmeasurable ones — mute incentives or improve measurement.
  • Revelation principle: restrict to direct truthful mechanisms w.l.o.g.; with single crossing, IC = monotonicity + envelope condition; optimal screening distorts all but the top type ("no distortion at the top").
  • Myerson (1981): optimal auction allocates by virtual valuation ψ(v) = v − (1−F(v))/f(v) — the information-rent logic generalizes to all mechanism design.
  • Hart–Moore (1988): with incomplete contracts, allocate residual control (ownership) to the party whose noncontractible investment matters most; this is the theory of the firm.
  • Applications share one template: write IR and IC, then optimize — regulation menus, insurance deductibles, SaaS tiering, debt/equity design.

Check your understanding

  1. A DAO wants to pay contributors in tokens for code review. Reviews vary in difficulty (hidden type) and reviewers choose how carefully to read (hidden action). Which frictions are present, and what two contract features address each?
    Answer

    Both adverse selection (difficulty/type hidden before contracting) and moral hazard (carefulness hidden after). For adverse selection: a screening menu — e.g., contributors choose between (easy reviews, low per-review pay) and (hard reviews, high per-review pay with quality bonus), with single-crossing (skilled reviewers value the quality bonus more). For moral hazard: condition pay on informative signals — spot-audits against known-bug test cases (informativeness principle), peer-relative scoring to filter common difficulty shocks; keep the variable component moderate since token income is risky (risk–incentive tradeoff).

  2. Your CEO says "let's put salespeople on 100% commission — maximum incentives!" Use Holmström–Milgrom to explain why the optimal commission rate is below 100%, and name the multitask concern.
    Answer

    β = 1/(1 + rσ²c′′) < 1 whenever agents are risk-averse (r > 0) and sales are noisy (σ² > 0): 100% commission loads all demand risk onto the salesperson, who demands a compensating risk premium — the firm pays more in expectation than it gains in effort. Multitask concern: pure revenue commission distorts effort toward closing deals and away from unmeasurable tasks (mentoring, accurate forecasting, customer success) — teaching to the test.

  3. A platform offers a free tier (1,000 API calls/month, no support) and a pro tier (unlimited, SLA, $500/month). A naive analyst says the free tier is charity. What is its mechanism-design function?
    Answer

    It's a screening menu: the degraded free tier separates low-willingness-to-pay users from enterprises, protecting the $500 price. The distortion (rate limits, no SLA — "distortion below the top") reduces the information rent the platform must leave to high types: without a deliberately worse free option, enterprises would demand lower pro prices. It's price discrimination via self-selection, à la Rothschild–Stiglitz.

  4. Two cofounders dispute who should own the IP of their joint project. One writes the core algorithm (hard to contract on quality); the other brings customers (also hard to contract on). How does Hart–Moore frame the decision?
    Answer

    Both make noncontractible relationship-specific investments subject to ex post holdup. Ownership (residual control) should go to the cofounder whose marginal investment is more important to total surplus and more vulnerable to holdup — the one you most need to incentivize. If the algorithm is the bottleneck asset that makes the venture viable, the technical founder owns the IP (possibly with vesting, which is itself a dynamic contracting solution to the same holdup problem); if distribution is the bottleneck, flip it. Split ownership is worst — it invites bilateral holdup.
