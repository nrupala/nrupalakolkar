3.1 Microeconomic Theory

Doctoral course basis: Microeconomic Theory

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Microeconomic theory is, at its mathematical core, the theory of optimization under constraints with prices playing the role of Lagrange multipliers. Every constrained resource allocation you meet in engineering — power allocation across subcarriers, compute scheduling across GPUs, bandwidth pricing in a network — is an instance of the problems ECON 6100 studies. Consumer theory's duality (utility maximization ↔ expenditure minimization) is the same duality you know from convex optimization; the welfare theorems tell you when a decentralized, price-guided system is efficient and when it is not, which is the design question behind any market you build into software. General equilibrium is fixed-point theory applied to markets, and the machinery of choice under uncertainty (expected utility, risk aversion) is the language of decision under uncertainty you already use, formalized.

Learning objectives

  • Formulate consumer and producer problems as constrained optimization problems and derive Marshallian, Hicksian, factor, and supply functions.
  • State and use duality: recover one decision problem's solution from another's, and interpret prices as shadow values.
  • Apply the von Neumann–Morgenstern expected utility theorem and Arrow–Pratt measures of risk aversion.
  • State the two fundamental theorems of welfare economics and explain where they fail (externalities, public goods).
  • Use Edgeworth-box and general-equilibrium reasoning to analyze whether a decentralized allocation is efficient.

Core content

Consumer theory: choice, utility, and duality

The primitive is a preference relation ≿ on a consumption set X (a subset of R^n), assumed complete, transitive, continuous, and usually convex. Under mild conditions, preferences admit a utility representation: there exists u: X → R such that x ≿ y if and only if u(x) ≥ u(y). This is an ordinal statement — any strictly increasing transformation of u represents the same preferences, so cardinal magnitudes of utility are meaningless. What matters is the shape of indifference curves (convexity encodes diminishing marginal rates of substitution) and how choices respond to prices.

The consumer's primal problem is utility maximization: max u(x) subject to p·x ≤ w, where p is the price vector and w is wealth. Its solution is the Marshallian (uncompensated) demand x(p, w). But ECON 6100's deep insight is duality: the same preferences generate the expenditure minimization problem, min p·x subject to u(x) ≥ ū, whose solution is the Hicksian (compensated) demand h(p, ū). The expenditure function e(p, ū) = min{p·x : u(x) ≥ ū} is concave in prices, and by Shephard's lemma, h(p, ū) = ∇_p e(p, ū) whenever differentiable. Dually, the indirect utility function v(p, w) = max{u(x) : p·x ≤ w} and Roy's identity gives Marshallian demand: x(p, w) = −(∇_p v)/(∂v/∂w).

Engineers will recognize this as convex duality. The consumer's budget constraint binds like a power constraint; prices are the multipliers that decentralize the allocation; the expenditure function is the value function of the dual problem. The same structural relationship (primal value ↔ dual value ↔ envelope theorems) recurs in every optimization you do.

The Slutsky equation decomposes how demand responds to a price change: ∂x_i/∂p_j = ∂h_i/∂p_j − x_j · ∂x_i/∂w. The first term is the substitution effect — always nonpositive on the diagonal (the Slutsky matrix of compensated price effects is symmetric and negative semidefinite, a testable restriction on demand data). The second term is the income effect. Revealed preference (WARP, SARP) gives the theory its empirical bite: observed choices either satisfy these axioms or the model is rejected.

Choice under uncertainty: expected utility and risk

When outcomes are lotteries, the von Neumann–Morgenstern (1944) theorem says: if preferences over lotteries satisfy completeness, transitivity, continuity, and the independence axiom, they are represented by expected utility, U(L) = Σ π_i u(x_i), where π_i are objective probabilities. Savage (1954) extended this to subjective probabilities. This is the normative benchmark against which Lesson 3.4's behavioral deviations are measured.

Risk aversion is curvature of u: a concave u means the agent prefers the expected value of a lottery to the lottery itself (Jensen's inequality). The Arrow–Pratt coefficient of absolute risk aversion, A(x) = −u''(x)/u'(x), and relative risk aversion, R(x) = −x u''(x)/u'(x), quantify this locally. Constant relative risk aversion (CRRA), u(x) = x^(1−γ)/(1−γ), is the workhorse of finance and macro. Stochastic dominance gives partial orders over lotteries without committing to a specific u: first-order stochastic dominance (everyone with increasing utility agrees) and second-order (everyone risk-averse agrees).

Producer theory: technology and duality again

The firm has a technology set Y (input-output combinations), assumed closed, convex, with free disposal. Profit maximization, max p·y over y in Y, yields supply y(p) and factor demands; Hotelling's lemma recovers them as the gradient of the profit function π(p) = max_{y in Y} p·y. Cost minimization, min w·x subject to producing at least q, yields the cost function c(w, q); Shephard's lemma gives conditional factor demands x(w, q) = ∇_w c(w, q). The cost function is concave in factor prices and increasing in output. Duality means: know the cost function and you know the technology (up to convexification), because c(w, q) encodes the production set. Returns to scale (constant, increasing, decreasing) are read off the cost function's shape, and long-run average cost curves are the engineer's cost-per-unit curves made rigorous.

General equilibrium: Edgeworth box and the welfare theorems

Partial equilibrium studies one market holding the rest fixed. General equilibrium asks: does there exist a price vector at which all markets clear simultaneously? In the Edgeworth box for two consumers and two goods, the contract curve is the locus of Pareto-efficient allocations (where indifference curves are tangent, so marginal rates of substitution are equal). A competitive equilibrium is a price vector p* and allocation x* such that each consumer maximizes utility at p* and markets clear.

The First Welfare Theorem: under local nonsatiation, every competitive equilibrium allocation is Pareto efficient. No planner is needed; the price system decentralizes efficiency. The Second Welfare Theorem: under convexity, every Pareto-efficient allocation can be supported as a competitive equilibrium after lump-sum redistribution of endowments. Together they are the benchmark: prices can coordinate decentralized agents perfectly, provided markets are complete and competitive and there are no externalities.

Existence is nontrivial and uses fixed-point theorems (Brouwer/Kakutani applied to an excess-demand correspondence): Arrow and Debreu (1954) showed equilibrium exists under continuity, convexity, and interiority conditions. The core — allocations no coalition can improve upon by re-trading among itself — shrinks to the competitive equilibrium as the economy is replicated (core convergence), a limit theorem connecting cooperative and noncooperative solution concepts.

Market failures: externalities and public goods

The welfare theorems fail when the assumptions fail. An externality exists when one agent's action directly affects another's utility or production possibilities outside the price system (pollution, congestion, network effects). The classic fix is Pigouvian taxation: set a tax equal to marginal external damage so the private decision internalizes the social cost — a control-theoretic correction of a misaligned incentive. Equivalently, with well-defined property rights and no transaction costs, bargaining reaches efficiency (Coase).

Public goods (nonrival, nonexcludable) break the price mechanism more fundamentally: voluntary contribution leads to free-riding and underprovision, because each agent's dominant strategy is to let others pay. The Lindahl equilibrium constructs personalized prices at which everyone voluntarily demands the efficient quantity — elegant, but informationally demanding (it requires the planner to know preferences, which is exactly the mechanism-design problem of Lesson 3.5). Externalities and public goods are why markets need design, regulation, or mechanism design — the subject of the next four lessons.

Worked examples

Example 1: Water-filling as expenditure minimization

A wireless engineer allocates power p_i across N subcarriers with channel gains g_i to achieve target rates at minimum total power — the classic water-filling solution. This is exactly the expenditure minimization dual: min Σ p_i subject to Σ log(1 + g_i p_i) ≥ R (a "utility" constraint). The Lagrangian multiplier on the rate constraint is the shadow price of rate; the first-order conditions give p_i = max(0, μ − 1/g_i), and μ is the marginal "price" of rate. Now flip it: the primal, max Σ log(1 + g_i p_i) subject to Σ p_i ≤ P, is utility maximization under a budget. Duality tells you the two problems share the solution structure, and the multiplier μ is the engineer's indifference-curve slope. Consumer theory's duality is not an analogy here — it is the same mathematics.

Example 2: Slutsky decomposition in cloud pricing

A cloud provider raises the price of GPU instances from $2.00 to $2.50/hour. A startup's demand falls from 100 to 80 instance-hours. The Slutsky equation separates two forces: the substitution effect (at constant "utility" — constant compute output — the startup substitutes toward cheaper CPU instances) and the income effect (the price hike reduces its real budget, cutting total compute). If compensated demand (the Hicksian part, computable from the expenditure function) falls from 100 to 85, then substitution explains 15 hours and income explains 5. The negative semidefiniteness of the Slutsky matrix is a testable prediction: estimated demand systems from cloud billing data must satisfy it, or the rational-choice model is rejected — a specification check you can run on real usage logs.

Example 3: Risk aversion and the certainty equivalent

An engineer with wealth $100,000 and CRRA utility u(x) = √x (relative risk aversion 1/2) is offered a project paying $120,000 with probability 1/2 and $80,000 with probability 1/2 (expected value $100,000). Expected utility is (1/2)√120000 + (1/2)√80000 ≈ 314.6, so the certainty equivalent is 314.6² ≈ $98,994 — she would pay up to about $1,006 to avoid the risk. The Arrow–Pratt approximation says the risk premium is ≈ (1/2)·A·σ² where A = 1/(2·100000) = 5×10⁻⁶ and σ² = 4×10⁸, giving ≈ $1,000, matching the exact calculation. This is the same risk-premium logic behind insurance pricing, the equity premium, and any expected-utility-based controller that penalizes variance.

Example 4: Pigouvian pricing for network congestion

Two data centers share a link. Each chooses traffic x_i; latency cost is c(x_1 + x_2) = (x_1 + x_2)² borne by both (a negative externality — each ignores the delay it imposes on the other). Privately each sets marginal benefit equal to its own marginal cost, overusing the link. The social planner's first-order condition equates each user's marginal benefit to the full marginal social cost 2(x_1 + x_2). A Pigouvian toll t = marginal external damage = 2x_j charged per unit restores efficiency — this is precisely how congestion pricing on roads and how ECN (explicit congestion notification) marking in TCP work: a price signal that internalizes the externality. If instead the link were a public good (adding capacity benefits everyone, nonexcludable), voluntary contribution underprovides — the free-rider problem behind underinvestment in shared infrastructure.

Key results to remember

  • Duality: utility maximization ↔ expenditure minimization (consumer), profit maximization ↔ cost minimization (producer); prices are the Lagrange multipliers that link primal and dual.
  • Shephard's lemma, Roy's identity, and Hotelling's lemma recover demands and supplies as gradients of value functions (expenditure, indirect utility, profit).
  • The Slutsky equation splits price effects into substitution (always ≤ 0 on the diagonal) and income effects; the Slutsky matrix is symmetric and negative semidefinite.
  • Von Neumann–Morgenstern (1944): completeness, transitivity, continuity, and independence imply expected-utility representation; Arrow–Pratt measures quantify risk aversion via curvature.
  • First Welfare Theorem: competitive equilibria are Pareto efficient. Second Welfare Theorem: any efficient allocation can be decentralized with lump-sum transfers (under convexity).
  • Arrow–Debreu (1954): competitive equilibrium exists (fixed-point argument); the core converges to competitive equilibrium under replication.
  • Externalities → Pigouvian taxes (or Coasian bargaining); public goods → free-riding and underprovision, with Lindahl pricing as the informationally demanding efficient benchmark.

Check your understanding

  1. An engineer's resource allocation problem is max f(x) subject to a budget Σ p_i x_i ≤ B with f concave. State the dual problem, and explain what the Lagrange multiplier on the budget constraint corresponds to in consumer theory.
    Answer

    The dual is expenditure minimization: min Σ p_i x_i subject to f(x) ≥ ū. The multiplier is the marginal utility of wealth (the derivative of the indirect utility function) — the shadow price of the budget, i.e., how much the objective improves per extra dollar of budget.

  2. Why does the Slutsky substitution effect have to be nonpositive on the diagonal, and what would a violation in estimated demand data tell you?
    Answer

    The compensated (Hicksian) demand comes from minimizing expenditure to hold utility fixed; raising p_i while compensating income can never increase Hicksian demand for good i — that would contradict cost minimization. A violation (positive own-price substitution effect) rejects the rational-choice model: preferences are not well-behaved, or the data/estimation are misspecified.

  3. A startup founder says: "Competitive markets are always efficient, so our platform needs no intervention." Which welfare-theorem assumptions is she implicitly invoking, and give one platform-relevant failure.
    Answer

    She invokes the First Welfare Theorem: complete markets, price-taking behavior, no externalities, convex technology/preferences, local nonsatiation. A platform-relevant failure: network externalities and two-sided-market effects (each user's participation changes others' payoffs outside the price system), or market power from data scale economies — either breaks efficiency.

  4. Using u(x) = ln x, compute the Arrow–Pratt absolute and relative risk aversion. An agent with this utility is offered a fair gamble; will she take it?
    Answer

    A(x) = −u''/u' = (1/x²)/(1/x) = 1/x; R(x) = x·A(x) = 1 (CRRA with γ = 1). She will not take a fair gamble: ln is strictly concave, so by Jensen's inequality expected utility of the gamble is below utility of its expected value — she is strictly risk-averse.
