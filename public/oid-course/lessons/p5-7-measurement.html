5.7 Measurement and Data Analysis

Built from Wharton MKTG 9400: Measurement and Data Analysis in Marketing — Part A / MGMT 9570: Applied Research Methods and Data Analysis in Organizational Behavior

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Engineers measure latent things constantly: "trust in the copilot," "alert fatigue," "code quality," "user satisfaction." A single survey question or a hand-rolled score is not a measurement — it is a hope. Psychometrics, the discipline behind MKTG 9400 and MGMT 9570, tells you how to build measures that actually capture the construct, how to tell reliability apart from validity, and how to check whether your five "trust" questions measure one thing or three. The same toolkit — factor analysis, reliability coefficients, common-method-bias guards — applies whenever you score human judgments of AI systems, evaluate annotator agreement, or analyze messy survey and behavioral data with the right outcome model instead of forcing everything into OLS.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish construct definition from operationalization and reliability from validity.
  • Follow the Churchill scale-development paradigm from item pool to validated scale.
  • Compute and interpret Cronbach's alpha and diagnose what it does and does not guarantee.
  • Use exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis, including the Fornell–Larcker discriminant-validity check.
  • Guard against common method bias with procedural and statistical remedies (Podsakoff et al.).

Core content

Constructs, operationalizations, and the two separate claims

Cronbach and Meehl (1955) drew the founding distinction: the construct is the theoretical concept ("trust in automation"), the operationalization is how you measure it (five Likert items, a behavioral reliance rate). Construct validity is the argument that the operationalization captures the construct — established through the construct's nomological network: the pattern of relationships the construct should and should not have with other constructs. Two claims must then be kept separate. Reliability is consistency: does the measure give the same answer on repetition (test–retest) or across its items (internal consistency)? Validity is accuracy: does it measure the right thing — content validity (coverage of the domain), convergent validity (correlates with other measures of the same construct), discriminant validity (does not correlate with measures of different constructs), and criterion/nomological validity (behaves as theory predicts)? A measure can be perfectly reliable and perfectly invalid — a bathroom scale that always reads 5 kg high is reliable but not valid. In AI evaluation this bites when teams report high annotator agreement (reliability) as if it established that the annotation guidelines capture the right concept (validity).

The scale-development paradigm

Churchill (1979), updated by Gerbing and Anderson (1988), gives the field's standard pipeline. (1) Specify the construct's domain precisely — what is included and excluded. (2) Generate a large item pool, deliberately over-inclusive. (3) Collect pilot data and purify: drop items with low item–total correlations. (4) Collect fresh data and assess dimensionality with factor analysis, retaining items that load cleanly on the intended factor(s). (5) Assess reliability. (6) Assess validity — convergent, discriminant, nomological — on new data. Netemeyer's Scaling Procedures is the handbook reference. The non-negotiable principle: develop on one sample, validate on another. Purifying and "validating" on the same data is the measurement analogue of training and testing on the same set — it overfits, and your scale will disappoint in the field exactly the way an overfit model does.

Reliability: Cronbach's alpha and its limits

Cronbach's alpha (α) is the workhorse internal-consistency coefficient: for k items, α = [k/(k−1)]·[1 − (Σσᵢ²/σₜₒₜ²)], the ratio of shared to total variance, ranging 0–1, with .70 a common adequacy threshold and .80+ preferred for applied decisions. But alpha is widely misread. It assumes (tau-)equivalence of items and is inflated by sheer item count — twenty mediocre items can outscore five excellent ones. High alpha does not prove unidimensionality: a scale mixing two distinct constructs can still show α = .85. So report alpha, but establish dimensionality with factor analysis and report test–retest reliability separately when stability over time matters (e.g., a "trust in AI" trait measure should be stable week to week; a "trust in this session" state measure should not be).

Factor analysis: EFA, CFA, and discriminant validity

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) discovers how many latent dimensions underlie a set of items: items that covary share a factor. Use it during scale development. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) tests a pre-specified structure: each item loads on its intended factor, factors may correlate, and fit indices (CFI, RMSEA, SRMR) judge whether the data accept the theory. CFA is where discriminant validity gets teeth via the Fornell and Larcker (1981) criterion: a construct's average variance extracted (AVE — the mean squared loading of its items) must exceed its squared correlations with every other construct. Intuitively, a construct must share more variance with its own items than with any other construct — otherwise your "trust" and "satisfaction" scales are measuring the same thing under two names. Structural equation modeling then embeds the measurement model (CFA) inside a structural model of construct relationships, estimating both with measurement error accounted for rather than wished away.

Common method bias and messy outcomes

When the same respondent provides both predictor and outcome in one survey — "rate your team's AI tool use" and "rate your productivity" — common method bias inflates their correlation: mood, acquiescence, and implicit theories create shared variance that is method, not substance (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Procedural remedies come first: separate measurement occasions, different response formats, protect anonymity, and — best — separate sources (self-reported tool use plus system-logged productivity). Statistical checks (Harman's single-factor test, marker variables, CFA method factors) are backstops, not cures. Finally, the MKTG 9400 "funny Y's and messy X's" lesson: match the outcome model to the dependent variable — binary (logit/probit), ordered (ordered logit), choice (multinomial/conditional logit), counts (Poisson/negative binomial), truncated/censored (tobit, survival models), durations (hazard models) — and treat causal identification with non-experimental data as a design problem (Part 2's econometrics spine), not a control-variable problem.

Worked examples

Example 1: Building a "trust in the copilot" scale

Domain specification: trust = willingness to rely on the copilot's suggestions under uncertainty (excluding general tech optimism). Item pool: 14 candidate statements ("I double-check every copilot suggestion" [reverse], "I accept copilot completions without reading them fully," …). Pilot with 200 engineers; drop 4 items with item–total r < .30. Fresh sample of 300: EFA reveals two factors — "reliance behavior" and "perceived competence" — not one. You now have two subscales, not the single "trust" score you assumed; shipping the single score would have mixed distinct constructs. Validate both subscales' nomological network: reliance should predict actual acceptance rates in telemetry (criterion validity) but not correlate with general risk tolerance (discriminant).

Example 2: Computing alpha — and what it hides

import numpy as np

def cronbach_alpha(X):
    # X: n_respondents x k_items
    k = X.shape[1]
    item_vars = X.var(axis=0, ddof=1).sum()
    total_var = X.sum(axis=1).var(ddof=1)
    return k / (k - 1) * (1 - item_vars / total_var)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# two distinct 3-item constructs, correlated only within-construct
c1 = rng.normal(size=(300, 1)) + rng.normal(scale=0.5, size=(300, 3))
c2 = rng.normal(size=(300, 1)) + rng.normal(scale=0.5, size=(300, 3))
print("alpha, all 6 items:", round(cronbach_alpha(np.hstack([c1, c2])), 3))
print("alpha, construct 1:", round(cronbach_alpha(c1), 3))

The six-item alpha prints around .55–.65 (two dimensions dilute it), while each three-item subscale scores ~.85. The moral cuts both ways: a "good" alpha on a mixed item set can mask multidimensionality, and a "low" alpha can simply mean you pooled two good scales. Always pair alpha with a factor analysis.

Example 3: Fornell–Larcker check on "trust" vs "satisfaction"

Your CFA gives trust items loadings averaging .75 (AVE = .56) and satisfaction items averaging .80 (AVE = .64); the trust–satisfaction correlation is .82, so the squared correlation is .67. Fornell–Larcker fails: .67 > .56 — trust shares more variance with satisfaction than with its own items. Conclusion: respondents are not distinguishing the constructs; either rewrite items to sharpen the conceptual boundary (trust = reliance under uncertainty; satisfaction = affective evaluation) or model a single higher-order factor. Do not publish them as two separate predictors.

Example 4: Procedural fix for common method bias

You survey engineers on "AI assistant usage" (predictor) and "self-rated productivity" (outcome) in one questionnaire — a textbook common-method design. Redesign: measure usage from IDE telemetry (separate source), collect productivity self-ratings two weeks later (temporal separation), and add a theoretically unrelated marker variable (e.g., attitude toward office lighting) to gauge shared method variance statistically. The correlation that survives across sources and time is the one worth reporting to leadership.

Key results to remember

  • Construct (the concept) ≠ operationalization (the measure); construct validity is argued through the nomological network of predicted relationships.
  • Reliability (consistency) and validity (accuracy) are separate claims — a measure can be perfectly reliable and invalid.
  • Churchill's paradigm: specify domain → large item pool → purify → factor-analyze → assess reliability → validate on fresh data; never validate on the development sample.
  • Cronbach's α = [k/(k−1)]·[1 − Σσᵢ²/σₜₒₜ²]; .70+ adequate, .80+ preferred — but alpha is inflated by item count and does not prove unidimensionality.
  • EFA discovers dimensionality; CFA tests a pre-specified structure; Fornell–Larcker requires AVE > squared inter-construct correlations for discriminant validity.
  • Common method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003): separate sources, occasions, and formats procedurally; statistical checks are backstops, not cures.
  • Match the model to the outcome — binary, ordered, choice, count, censored, duration models ("funny Y's") — instead of forcing everything into OLS.

Check your understanding

  1. Your annotation guideline yields 92% inter-annotator agreement. Does that establish the labels are valid? Explain.
    Answer

    No — agreement is reliability (consistency), not validity (accuracy). Annotators can agree reliably on the wrong interpretation of an ambiguous guideline. Validity requires showing the labels capture the intended construct, e.g., through predicted relationships with downstream outcomes.

  2. A 12-item "developer experience" scale has α = .88. Give two reasons this might still be a bad scale.
    Answer

    Alpha is inflated by item count, so 12 mediocre items can clear .88; and high alpha does not establish unidimensionality — the items could span two or three distinct constructs (tooling, process, culture) that should be scored separately, as only a factor analysis would reveal.

  3. Trust AVE = .50, satisfaction AVE = .60, correlation between them = .78. Does discriminant validity hold? What do you do?
    Answer

    No: squared correlation .78² ≈ .61 exceeds trust's AVE of .50 (Fornell–Larcker fails). Rewrite items to sharpen the conceptual boundary or model one higher-order factor; do not use both as separate predictors.

  4. Name three procedural remedies for common method bias in a survey of engineers about AI tool impact.
    Answer

    Temporal separation (predictor and outcome measured weeks apart), source separation (tool usage from telemetry rather than self-report), and format/anonymity protections (different scale formats, anonymous responses) — plus a marker variable as a statistical check.
