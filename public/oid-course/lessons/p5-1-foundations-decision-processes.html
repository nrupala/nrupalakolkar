5.1 Foundations of Decision Processes

Built from Wharton OIDD 9000: Foundations of Decision Processes

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Engineers decide under uncertainty constantly: whether to ship a model that is 2% better on a benchmark, how much redundancy to buy for a power grid, whether a chip tape-out is worth the mask cost. Decision analysis — the normative theory of choice under uncertainty — gives you a precise grammar for those calls: coherent beliefs, coherent preferences, and a dollar value on information. It turns "trust your gut" into an auditable calculation, and it tells you exactly when an experiment or pilot is worth paying for. It also sets the baseline that the next two lessons will show humans systematically violating, which is why explainable AI, recommender design, and human-in-the-loop systems all have to be built for real decision makers rather than idealized ones.

Learning objectives

  • State the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and explain how they yield the expected-utility representation of preferences.
  • Distinguish expected value from expected utility and explain risk aversion through concave utility, the certainty equivalent, and the risk premium.
  • Apply Savage's subjective expected utility to decisions where objective probabilities are unavailable.
  • Compute the expected value of perfect information and use it to price pilots, tests, and data-collection efforts.
  • Build and solve a multi-stage decision tree by backward induction (foldback).

Core content

The normative benchmark: expected utility theory

Decision research starts with a normative question: how should a rational agent choose among uncertain prospects? The answer, due to von Neumann and Morgenstern (1944), is expected utility theory. A "prospect" or lottery is a list of outcomes with associated probabilities, written L = (p₁, x₁; p₂, x₂; …; pₙ, xₙ). The theory says a decision maker whose preferences satisfy four axioms — completeness (any two lotteries can be compared), transitivity (if A is preferred to B and B to C, then A is preferred to C), continuity (no infinitely good or bad outcome dominates everything), and independence (mixing both lotteries with a third lottery in the same proportion preserves the preference order) — behaves as if maximizing the expectation of some utility function u. Formally, L is preferred to M if and only if Σᵢ pᵢ·u(xᵢ) > Σⱼ qⱼ·u(yⱼ).

The axioms are deliberately spare; the representation theorem is what makes them powerful. It does not claim people actually compute these sums — it says any coherent set of preferences can be represented this way. The classic motivation is the St. Petersburg paradox: a gamble paying 2ⁿ dollars where n is the number of coin flips until the first heads has infinite expected monetary value, yet nobody would pay more than a few dollars to play. Bernoulli's 1738 resolution, which the theory formalizes, is that people maximize expected utility, and marginal utility diminishes — u is concave. A concave utility function encodes risk aversion: the decision maker prefers the expected value of a gamble for sure over the gamble itself. Two derived quantities make this operational. The certainty equivalent is the sure amount that leaves the agent indifferent to the gamble; the risk premium is the gap between the gamble's expected value and its certainty equivalent — literally the price of risk, which is why it matters when you decide how much redundancy to buy or what insurance premium is fair.

Subjective probability: deciding when the odds are unknown

Real engineering decisions rarely come with known probabilities. Nobody hands you the true failure probability of a novel battery chemistry or the true lift of a recommender change. Savage (1954) extended expected utility to this case with subjective expected utility. Instead of lotteries with given probabilities, the primitives are states of the world, acts (mappings from states to consequences), and consequences. Savage showed that if preferences over acts satisfy his axioms, the decision maker behaves as if holding a subjective probability distribution P over states and a utility function u over consequences, choosing the act that maximizes Σₛ P(s)·u(consequence of act in state s).

Two things follow. First, "probability" becomes a property of beliefs, not of coins — coherent beliefs must obey the probability calculus, which is the normative justification for Bayesian updating in engineering practice. Second, the theory separates the decision into three auditable pieces: beliefs (what you think will happen), preferences (what you value), and the decision rule (maximize expected utility). When a design review goes wrong, this decomposition tells you where to look: were the beliefs miscalibrated, were the objectives misspecified, or was the choice rule incoherent?

Decision trees and backward induction

The decision tree is the working engineer's rendering of the theory. Squares are decision nodes (your choices), circles are chance nodes (uncertainty), and branches carry probabilities and payoffs. You solve the tree by foldback or backward induction: starting at the rightmost nodes, replace each chance node with its expected utility and each decision node with the best available branch, then roll the values leftward to the root. The path that survives is the optimal contingent plan.

Foldback enforces a discipline that ad-hoc reasoning skips: it forces you to specify what you would do after each possible test result before you decide whether the test is worth running. In system design this is the logic behind pilot deployments, canary releases, and staged tape-outs — each is a decision node placed before a big commitment, and its value comes entirely from the chance node that follows it.

The value of information

Information has value because it can change your decision. The expected value of perfect information (EVPI) is the difference between the expected utility of deciding with perfect foresight about the uncertain state and the expected utility of deciding now. Concretely: compute the best action's expected utility without information; then, for each possible state, compute the utility of the action you would take if you knew that state, and average over your prior. EVPI is the difference. It is an upper bound: no real test, pilot, or dataset can be worth more than perfect information, so if a vendor quotes you a price above EVPI, walk away without further analysis.

The expected value of a specific, imperfect test — sample information — is computed the same way but with the test's possible outcomes and their likelihoods. This is the formal backbone of experimental design in tech: the value of an A/B test, a labeling pilot, or a simulation study is the expected improvement in the downstream decision it enables. It is also the logic of active learning and Bayesian optimization in ML: query the point whose answer most improves the expected decision, not the point with the highest uncertainty for its own sake.

Normative, descriptive, prescriptive: the three faces of decision research

OIDD 9000 organizes the field around three stances. The normative stance asks how an ideal agent should decide — expected utility, Bayesian updating. The descriptive stance asks how people actually decide — the subject of lessons 5.2 and 5.3, where heuristics, biases, and prospect theory live. The prescriptive stance asks how to help real, boundedly rational people decide better: decision structuring, debiasing, multi-attribute utility analysis (Keeney & Raiffa), and choice architecture. Simon's (1955) bounded rationality — satisficing under limited information processing — is the bridge: people cannot optimize the full tree, so they satisfice, and prescriptive tools should fit the decision maker rather than assuming the normative ideal. For engineers, the practical moral is that you will use the normative machinery to design systems, and you will need the descriptive findings to design for the humans who use them.

Worked examples

Example 1: Expected value versus expected utility in a launch decision

Your team can launch a new edge-inference chip now (a certain $4M profit) or run a risky redesign: 50% chance of $10M profit, 50% chance of $0. Expected monetary value favors the redesign ($5M > $4M). But with a concave utility function u(x) = √x (in $M), the expected utility of the redesign is 0.5·√10 + 0.5·√0 ≈ 1.58, while the sure launch gives √4 = 2.0. The coherent risk-averse decision is the sure launch: its certainty equivalent is 1.58² ≈ $2.5M, so the risk premium on the redesign is $5M − $2.5M = $2.5M. A risk-neutral firm maximizes expected value; a cash-constrained startup maximizing expected utility rationally plays it safe — same math, different objective.

Example 2: Decision tree for a recommender pilot

You must choose: deploy recommender v2 fleet-wide now, or run a 2-week pilot ($200K) and then decide. Prior belief: 60% chance v2 lifts revenue by $5M, 40% chance it loses $3M (net of everything, risk-neutral so utility = dollars). Deploy now: 0.6·5 + 0.4·(−3) = $1.8M. With a pilot, assume the pilot perfectly reveals the outcome: if good, deploy (+$5M − $0.2M); if bad, keep v1 ($0 − $0.2M). Pilot expected value: 0.6·4.8 + 0.4·(−0.2) = $2.8M. Foldback says run the pilot — its value over deploying now is $1.0M, well above its $0.2M cost. Note the pilot's value comes entirely from the bad-outcome branch, where it lets you avoid a $3M mistake.

Example 3: Pricing a dataset with EVPI

A vendor offers a labeled dataset for $150K that would resolve uncertainty about which of two perception models to ship. Your prior: model A wins (worth $1M) with probability 0.5; otherwise model B wins (worth $1M) and A is worth $0. Without information you pick either, expected value $0.5M. With perfect information you always pick the winner: expected value $1M. EVPI = $1M − $0.5M = $0.5M. Since $150K < $0.5M, the dataset is worth serious consideration — and because the vendor's data is imperfect, its true value (expected value of sample information) is strictly less than $0.5M, so you now know the range in which to negotiate.

Example 4: Computing expected utility in Python

import math

def expected_utility(outcomes, probs, u):
    return sum(p * u(x) for p, x in zip(probs, outcomes))

u_log = lambda x: math.log(x)          # concave: risk averse
u_lin = lambda x: x                     # risk neutral

designs = {
    "conservative": ([4.0], [1.0]),
    "aggressive":   ([10.0, 0.5], [0.5, 0.5]),
}
for name, (xs, ps) in designs.items():
    print(name,
          "EU_log=%.3f" % expected_utility(xs, ps, u_log),
          "EV=%.2f" % expected_utility(xs, ps, u_lin))

The log-utility agent prefers the conservative design even though the aggressive design has higher expected value — the code makes the preference's source (the curvature of u) explicit and auditable.

Key results to remember

  • The von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence) imply preferences over lotteries are representable as maximization of Σ pᵢ·u(xᵢ).
  • Risk aversion is concave utility; the risk premium (expected value minus certainty equivalent) is the price of risk.
  • Savage's subjective expected utility extends the theory to unknown probabilities: coherent beliefs behave like probabilities and are updated by Bayes' rule.
  • Decision trees are solved by foldback — replace chance nodes with expectations and decision nodes with the best branch, rolling values back to the root.
  • The expected value of perfect information bounds what any test, pilot, or dataset can be worth; compare it against the price before deeper analysis.
  • Information is valuable only insofar as it can change the downstream decision — the logic behind A/B tests, canary releases, active learning, and Bayesian optimization.
  • Decision research has three stances — normative (how one should decide), descriptive (how people do decide), prescriptive (how to help them decide better) — and bounded rationality (Simon, satisficing) connects them.

Check your understanding

  1. Why does the independence axiom matter — what goes wrong in the expected-utility representation if it is dropped?
    Answer

    Independence says mixing two lotteries with a common third lottery in the same proportion cannot reverse their ranking. Without it, preferences cannot be represented as a probability-weighted sum of outcome utilities, because the weight attached to an outcome would depend on what it is mixed with. Violations (e.g., the Allais paradox) are precisely what motivated prospect theory's probability weighting.

  2. A gamble pays $100 with probability 0.5 and $0 otherwise. With u(x) = √x, what is its certainty equivalent and risk premium?
    Answer

    Expected utility = 0.5·√100 + 0.5·√0 = 5. Certainty equivalent CE solves √CE = 5, so CE = $25. Expected value is $50, so the risk premium is $50 − $25 = $25.

  3. A pilot test costs $50K and, if run, lets you avoid a $400K loss that occurs with prior probability 0.2. What is the most the pilot could be worth, and should you run it?
    Answer

    Perfect information about the loss would let you avoid it exactly when it would occur, worth 0.2·$400K = $80K in expectation. EVPI = $80K > $50K, so the pilot is worth considering; its true value depends on how informative the pilot actually is (EVSI ≤ EVPI).

  4. Give an engineering example where the normative, descriptive, and prescriptive stances each say something different.
    Answer

    Normative: choose the grid redundancy level maximizing expected utility over outage scenarios. Descriptive: operators overweight recent blackouts (availability) and anchor on last year's budget. Prescriptive: restructure the planning review with explicit base rates, pre-committed decision rules, and a checklist that debiases the anchoring.
