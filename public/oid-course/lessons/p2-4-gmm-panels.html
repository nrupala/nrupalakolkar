2.4 GMM, M-Estimation, and Panel Methods

Doctoral course basis: Econometrics II: Methods & Models

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Modern estimation problems rarely hand you a tidy likelihood. Your model of a recommender system gives you moment conditions — "these residuals should be uncorrelated with those features" — but no full probability distribution. GMM turns any set of moment conditions into an efficient estimator, and its overidentification test is the closest econometrics gets to asking the data "are my assumptions mutually consistent?" M-estimation generalizes the extremum theory from Lesson 2.3 to robust estimators. And the panel methods here — dynamic panels, sample selection, quantile regression — are the tools for the two hardest realities of system data: persistence over time, nonrandom missingness (you only observe the servers that didn't crash), and effects that differ across the distribution (a config change that helps the median but hurts the tail).

Learning objectives

  • Construct the GMM estimator from moment conditions, derive the optimal weighting matrix, and interpret the J test of overidentifying restrictions.
  • Apply Newey–McFadden M-estimation asymptotics to robust estimators like LAD and Huber.
  • Estimate dynamic panels with Arellano–Bond and test the identifying assumptions.
  • Correct for sample selection with the Heckman model and defend the required exclusion restriction.
  • Estimate and interpret quantile regression, contrasting it with mean regression.

Core content

GMM: Hansen's framework

Suppose economic or engineering theory gives you moment conditions E[g(wi, θ0)] = 0, an m-vector of functions with E[g] = 0 only at the true θ0. With more moments than parameters (m > k, overidentification), no θ sets all sample moments ḡ(θ) = (1/n)Σg(wi, θ) exactly to zero, so Hansen (1982) proposed minimizing a quadratic form: θ̂GMM = argminθ ḡ(θ)′Wnḡ(θ), for a positive-definite weighting matrix Wn. The estimator is consistent for any Wn →p W, and its asymptotic variance is (G′WG)−1G′WΩWG(G′WG)−1, where G = E[∇θg] and Ω = Var(g(wi, θ0)) — another sandwich. The optimal weighting matrix is W = Ω−1, the inverse of the moment covariance: it downweights noisy, imprecise moments and upweights informative ones, exactly like GLS downweights noisy observations. With W = Ω−1, the asymptotic variance collapses to (G′Ω−1G)−1, the efficiency bound for moment-based estimation.

In practice: two-step GMM first estimates with W = I (or another simple matrix) to get a consistent θ̃, estimates Ω̂ from the residuals' moment outer products, then re-estimates with W = Ω̂−1. Continuously updated GMM (CUE) minimizes ḡ(θ)′Ω̂(θ)−1ḡ(θ) jointly over θ, updating the weighting matrix as θ moves — more stable in small samples when the two-step weighting is erratic, and invariant to normalization. Report both when they diverge and investigate why. Note that 2SLS is just GMM with moments E[ziεi] = 0 and a particular weighting matrix; GMM is the parent, IV the special case.

Testing overidentifying restrictions

When m > k, the model makes more claims than needed to identify θ — and those extra claims are testable. Hansen's J test (Sargan's test is the homoskedastic/2SLS special case): J = n·ḡ(θ̂)′Ω̂−1ḡ(θ̂) →d χ2m−k under the null that all moment conditions hold. Intuition: if every moment is truly zero at θ0, the minimized criterion should be small; a large J says the moments cannot be simultaneously satisfied — at least one assumption is wrong. But the test has a critical blind spot: it tests the joint validity of all moments, and it has no power against violations that all moments share. If every instrument is invalid in the same way, J will not catch it. So a non-rejection is not a certificate of validity — it only says the moments are mutually consistent. When J rejects, don't just drop moments until it passes (that is specification search); investigate which assumption fails, because the rejection is the data telling you your theory is incomplete.

M-estimation and robust estimators

M-estimators maximize (or minimize) a sum of criterion functions, θ̂ = argmaxθ Σi m(wi, θ) — MLE (m = log-likelihood), nonlinear least squares, and also robust estimators. The Newey–McFadden theory from Lesson 2.3 applies directly: consistency from uniform convergence plus identification, normality from the first-order-condition expansion. The payoff is robustness: least absolute deviations (LAD), θ̂ = argminθ Σ|yi − xi′θ|, estimates the conditional median and resists outliers that would drag OLS — one corrupted sensor reading can't move it much. Huber's estimator blends quadratic loss near zero with linear loss in the tails, keeping efficiency under normality while bounding influence. The asymptotic variance follows the sandwich with H0 = E[∇2m] and Ω0 = Var(∇m). For an engineer drowning in heavy-tailed telemetry — latency spikes, bursty errors — M-estimators are the principled alternative to ad-hoc outlier trimming: they keep all the data but limit each observation's influence.

Dynamic panels: Arellano–Bond (deeper cut)

Building on Lesson 2.2: the model yit = ρyi,t−1 + xit′β + αi + εit with small T. First-difference to kill αi: Δyit = ρΔyi,t−1 + Δxit′β + Δεit. The regressor Δyi,t−1 correlates with Δεit (both contain εi,t−1), but lagged levels yi,t−2, yi,t−3, … are valid instruments: E[yi,t−sΔεit] = 0 for s ≥ 2 when εit is serially uncorrelated. The instrument set grows with t (at time t you can use all lags back to period 1), giving many moment conditions — estimate by GMM with the optimal weighting matrix. Three practical disciplines: (1) test for AR(2) in the differenced residuals — AR(1) is mechanical, but AR(2) means εit itself is serially correlated and the instruments are invalid; (2) report the Hansen/Sargan overidentification test on the stacked moments; (3) control instrument proliferation — with long T the instrument count can exceed n, overfitting the first stage; collapse the instrument matrix or cap lag depth. The system-GMM extension (Arellano–Bover/Blundell–Bond) adds level equations instrumented with lagged differences, gaining efficiency when series are highly persistent — the usual case for throughput, utilization, and other system metrics.

Sample selection: Heckman

You observe wages only for the employed, latency only for requests that complete, failure diagnostics only for machines that report back. If selection into the observed sample correlates with the outcome error, OLS on the observed sample is biased. Heckman's (1979) model: selection si = 1{zi′γ + ui > 0}, outcome yi = xi′β + εi observed only when si = 1, with (ui, εi) bivariate normal, corr = ρ. Then E[yi|xi, si=1] = xi′β + ρσελ(zi′γ), where λ(·) = φ(·)/Φ(·) is the inverse Mills ratio. The two-step estimator: (1) probit for selection, compute λ̂i; (2) OLS of y on x and λ̂i — the Mills ratio absorbs the selection bias, and its coefficient estimates ρσε (a test of whether selection bias exists at all). Full-information MLE is more efficient. The Achilles' heel is the exclusion restriction: z needs a variable affecting selection but not the outcome — without it, identification rests purely on the nonlinearity of λ(·), which is fragile (λ is nearly linear over much of its range, causing severe multicollinearity). Defend the exclusion with institutional knowledge, or treat the estimates with deep skepticism. For engineers: modeling "time to failure" only on machines that failed, or "user satisfaction" only among survey respondents, are textbook selection problems.

Quantile regression

Mean regression answers "how does x shift the average?" Quantile regression (Koenker & Bassett, 1978) answers "how does x shift the τ-th quantile?" — the median, the 90th percentile, the tail. The estimator minimizes the asymmetric absolute loss: β̂(τ) = argminβ Σi ρτ(yi − xi′β), with check function ρτ(u) = u(τ − 1{u<0}). It is an M-estimator (non-differentiable at zero, but the asymptotics go through with care), computed by linear programming. Why it matters for systems: SLAs are written on quantiles (p99 latency ≤ 200 ms), and treatment effects are routinely heterogeneous — a load-balancing change might cut median latency while increasing tail latency by rerouting stragglers. Plotting β̂(τ) across τ ∈ {0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9} with confidence bands reveals the full distributional effect that mean regression flattens into a single number. Quantile regression is also inherently robust: like the median, it shrugs off outliers. Its limitation: quantiles don't aggregate — the quantile of a sum isn't the sum of quantiles — so it's a descriptive tool for distributions, not a substitute for structural modeling.

Worked examples

Example 1: GMM estimation of a network demand model

You model link demand: throughput yi depends on price pi (endogenous — prices respond to congestion) with instruments zi = (fiber-construction cost shifters, regulatory dummies). Moments: E[zi(yi − α − βpi)] = 0, three instruments, two parameters — overidentified by one degree. Two-step GMM:

from linearmodels.iv import IVGMM
res = IVGMM.from_formula('thrpt ~ 1 + [price ~ cost_shifter + reg_a + reg_b]',
                         data=df).fit(cov_type='robust')
print(res.summary)   # J-test p-value reported

The J statistic is 1.9 on 1 df (p = 0.17) — the moments are mutually consistent, no evidence against the instruments as a set. But you remember the blind spot: this doesn't prove validity, only consistency. You additionally argue exclusion from the institutional fact that construction costs shift supply, not demand. Takeaway: GMM + J test is the workflow for overidentified models; the J test checks consistency, your domain knowledge defends validity.

Example 2: LAD vs. OLS on latency with outliers

Regressing request latency on payload size across 10,000 requests, OLS gives a slope of 0.9 ms/KB — but 40 requests hit a pathological retry storm with latencies 100× normal, and they dominate the squared-error fit. LAD (median regression) gives 0.31 ms/KB, and dropping the 40 outliers then running OLS gives 0.30 — LAD found the right answer without any ad-hoc trimming. You report LAD with its sandwich standard errors as the headline and OLS-with-trimming as a robustness check. Takeaway: M-estimators like LAD handle contamination gracefully; they dominate manual outlier deletion because the influence bound is built into the estimator.

Example 3: Arellano–Bond for persistent server utilization

You model hourly CPU utilization for 300 servers over 48 hours with a lagged dependent variable and a cooling-intervention dummy. Fixed effects would suffer Nickell bias (T = 48 helps, but the bias is still material and the intervention is staggered). Arellano–Bond difference GMM with lags 2–4 as instruments: ρ̂ = 0.82 (highly persistent, as expected), intervention effect −3.1 points (SE 0.8). Diagnostics: AR(1) test rejects (expected mechanically), AR(2) test p = 0.34 (good — no second-order serial correlation), Hansen J p = 0.22 (moments consistent), 12 instruments for 300 units (no proliferation). Takeaway: report the full diagnostic battery — AR(2), J test, instrument count — or the dynamic-panel estimate isn't credible.

Example 4: Quantile regression reveals heterogeneous load-balancer effects

A new load-balancing policy is evaluated on request latency. Mean regression: −8 ms (SE 3). Quantile regression tells the real story: τ = 0.5: −12 ms; τ = 0.9: −4 ms; τ = 0.99: +25 ms (SE 9). The policy helps typical requests but hurts the extreme tail — rerouting occasionally strands requests on slow paths. The SLA-relevant p99 effect has the opposite sign of the mean effect. You recommend against rollout despite the attractive mean. Takeaway: when the loss function lives in the tail, mean regression can recommend exactly the wrong policy; always check the quantiles.

Key results to remember

  • GMM: θ̂ = argmin ḡ(θ)′Wḡ(θ); the optimal weighting matrix is Ω−1, giving asymptotic variance (G′Ω−1G)−1. Two-step GMM is standard; CUE is the small-sample-robust alternative.
  • Hansen's J test: n·ḡ(θ̂)′Ω̂−1ḡ(θ̂) ∼ χ2m−k tests mutual consistency of overidentifying moments — not their validity. Investigate rejections; don't specification-search them away.
  • M-estimators (LAD, Huber) fit the Newey–McFadden extremum framework with sandwich asymptotics; they bound outlier influence by construction, dominating ad-hoc trimming.
  • Arellano–Bond: difference the dynamic panel, instrument Δyi,t−1 with lagged levels yi,t−2,…, estimate by GMM; mandatory diagnostics are the AR(2) test, the J test, and the instrument count.
  • Heckman selection: add the inverse Mills ratio λ(z′γ̂) from a first-stage probit to absorb selection bias — but credible identification needs an exclusion restriction in the selection equation, not just the nonlinearity of λ.
  • Quantile regression minimizes the check function ρτ; plot β̂(τ) across τ with bands to see distributional effects. Tail effects can reverse mean effects — check before shipping.

Check your understanding

  1. Your GMM J test fails to reject. Can you now claim your instruments are valid?
    Answer

    No. The J test checks mutual consistency of the moments, not validity: if all instruments are invalid in the same way, the test has no power. Validity still rests on the economic/engineering argument for exclusion.

  2. Why is the optimal GMM weighting matrix the inverse of the moment covariance?
    Answer

    Because it minimizes the asymptotic variance: weighting by Ω−1 downweights noisy moments and upweights precise ones, collapsing the sandwich to (G′Ω−1G)−1, the efficiency bound for moment-based estimation. It's the GMM analogue of GLS.

  3. In Arellano–Bond, the AR(2) test rejects. What does this mean and what do you do?
    Answer

    It means the differenced residuals have second-order serial correlation, so εit is serially correlated and the lagged-level instruments (which require E[yi,t−2Δεit] = 0) are invalid. Use deeper lags as instruments (t−3 and earlier) if AR(2)-only correlation holds, or reconsider the dynamic specification.

  4. You run Heckman without any variable in the selection equation that's excluded from the outcome. Why is this fragile?
    Answer

    Identification then rests solely on the nonlinearity of the inverse Mills ratio, which is approximately linear over much of its range — producing severe multicollinearity between λ̂ and x and wildly unstable estimates. A genuine exclusion restriction is needed for credibility.
