3.4 Behavioral Economics and Policy

Doctoral course basis: Behavioral Economics and Policy Analysis

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

The users of your systems — and often the agents inside them — are not the expected-utility maximizers of Lesson 3.1. They overweight losses, procrastinate, follow defaults, and care about fairness. If you design recommender systems, pricing pages, energy dashboards, or human-in-the-loop AI without modeling these deviations, your mechanism will misfire: the "optimal" contract from Lesson 3.5 can be undone by present bias, and the "efficient" default can be the one nobody switches away from. Behavioral economics gives you portable, empirically grounded models of these deviations — and the welfare framework to decide when to nudge and when to leave people alone.

Learning objectives

  • State prospect theory's three components and compute reference-dependent valuations.
  • Model present bias with quasi-hyperbolic discounting and distinguish naïfs from sophisticates.
  • Describe leading models of social preferences and limited attention.
  • Design a nudge (default, framing, reminder) and predict its mechanism.
  • Explain the behavioral-welfare problem: how to do policy analysis when revealed preference fails.

Core content

Prospect theory: reference dependence, loss aversion, probability weighting

Kahneman and Tversky (1979) built the leading descriptive alternative to expected utility from observed choices. Prospect theory has three components. First, reference dependence: people evaluate outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point (the status quo, expectations, a goal), not as final wealth levels — the carrier of value is the change, v(x − r), not u(w + x). Second, loss aversion: the value function is steeper for losses than for gains, v(−x) > v(x) in magnitude, with the loss-aversion coefficient λ typically estimated around 2 — losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains please. The value function is concave over gains (risk aversion) and convex over losses (risk seeking), producing the famous reflection effect: people gamble to avoid a sure loss but take the sure thing to lock in a gain.

Third, probability weighting: people transform probabilities with a weighting function π(p) that overweights small probabilities (lottery tickets, plane crashes, rare model failures) and underweights moderate-to-large ones. Combined with loss aversion, this explains the simultaneous demand for insurance (overweighting small loss probabilities) and lottery tickets (overweighting small gain probabilities) — a combination expected utility cannot produce. For engineers: probability weighting is why users overreact to rare AI errors and underreact to common small ones; any trust calibration or risk communication must be designed against π(p), not p.

Time preferences: present bias

Exponential discounting, U = Σ δ^t u(c_t), implies time consistency: a plan optimal today stays optimal tomorrow. Empirically, people reverse preferences as rewards approach — choosing $100 today over $110 tomorrow but $110 in 31 days over $100 in 30 days. Laibson (1997) models this with quasi-hyperbolic (β–δ) discounting: U_t = u(c_t) + β Σ_{τ>t} δ^{τ−t} u(c_τ), with β < 1 capturing present bias — a discrete extra discount on everything not immediate.

The β–δ model generates procrastination (a task worth doing eventually is never worth doing now), undersaving, and gym-membership puzzles. Crucially, it distinguishes naïfs, who believe their future selves will follow through (β̂ = 1 > β), from sophisticates, who know they won't (β̂ = β) and demand commitment devices — deadlines, automatic enrollment, locked savings. O'Donoghue and Rabin (1999) show the welfare economics of the distinction: naïfs are hurt by flexibility (easy cancellation, payday loans), sophisticates value commitment, and policy must decide whose preferences count — the present self's or the long-run self's. For AI systems: a present-biased user will keep clicking "remind me tomorrow" on a security update; the design fix is a commitment default (auto-update), not a better argument.

Social preferences: fairness and reciprocity

Lesson 3.3's ultimatum-game rejections need a model. Fehr and Schmidt (1999) inequity aversion: U_i = x_i − α max(x_j − x_i, 0) − γ max(x_i − x_j, 0), where α captures envy (disadvantageous inequality hurts) and γ captures guilt. Charness and Rabin (2002) nest inequity aversion with efficiency concerns and reciprocity — people reward kind intentions and punish unkind ones, not just unequal outcomes. These models explain conditional cooperation in public-goods games, gift-exchange in labor markets (above-market wages reciprocated with effort), and why "unfair" algorithmic decisions provoke backlash beyond their material cost. If your system allocates resources across users (bandwidth, compute quotas, moderation), perceived fairness is a first-order design constraint, and these models let you predict which allocations will be accepted and which will be sabotaged.

Limited attention and salience

Attention is scarce and allocated strategically. Chetty, Looney, and Kroft (2009) show consumers underreact to taxes not included in posted prices (salience): a tax added at the register reduces demand far less than an equivalent tax in the sticker price, violating the fungibility that Lesson 3.1 assumes. The general principle: shrouded attributes (add-on fees, data collection, fine print) are underweighted; salient ones are overweighted. Firms exploit this with drip pricing and partitioned tariffs — and regulators respond with salience mandates (all-in pricing). For technology: every dark pattern is applied limited-attention theory; every "simplify the bill" intervention is salience policy. Mullainathan and Shafir's scarcity work adds that poverty itself taxes attention (bandwidth), with implications for how you design interfaces for stressed users.

Behavioral welfare and policy: nudges and choice architecture

Thaler and Sunstein's nudge program: redesign choice architecture — defaults, framing, reminders, simplification — to steer behavior toward people's own long-run goals without restricting choice (libertarian paternalism). The empirical wins are real: automatic enrollment in retirement savings raises participation from ~50% to ~90% (Madrian and Shea); default green-energy tariffs shift consumption; timely reminders cut missed appointments and late fees.

But BEPP 9150's guiding focus is the harder question: welfare analysis when revealed preference fails. If choices reflect bias (present bias, inattention, probability weighting), then "what people choose" no longer reveals "what is best for them," and standard welfare economics breaks. Chetty (2015) develops the sufficient-statistics approach to behavioral public finance: you don't need the full psychological model — you need the behavioral elasticity (how much choices move) and the bias (how far choices are from the unbiased optimum) to sign and size optimal policy. Example: with underreaction to a tax (salience), the optimal tax formula adjusts by the attention parameter; with present bias, sin taxes can be welfare-improving even absent externalities, because they correct an internality — a harm the present self imposes on the future self. The internality concept extends Pigouvian logic (Lesson 3.1) from externalities to self-control problems: the optimal "tax" on sugary drinks or doomscrolling has the same structure as the optimal pollution tax, with the future self as the victim.

The honest limits: nudges are small relative to prices and bans; their effects decay; sludge (friction that harms) is the mirror image; and behavioral welfare requires taking a stand on true preferences — usually the "as-judged-by-themselves" criterion (what the person would choose if informed and attentive). Heterogeneity matters: a default that helps sophisticates can trap naïfs, and vice versa.

Worked examples

Example 1: Prospect theory and the backup decision

An IT admin with reference point "no data loss" chooses between (A) a certain small cost: 1 hour of backup maintenance weekly, and (B) a 0.1% weekly chance of a catastrophic 200-hour outage. Expected-value logic says (B) costs 0.2 hours/week — take the gamble, skip backups. But probability weighting overweights 0.1% → π(0.001) ≈ 0.02 (typical estimates), and loss aversion (λ ≈ 2) amplifies the dreaded loss: the weighted prospect value of (B) is roughly −2 × 0.02 × v(200) vs. −v(1) for (A). With v(x) = x^0.88 (standard curvature), v(200) ≈ 101, v(1) = 1: (B) evaluates to about −4 vs. −1 for (A) — the admin backs up, and prospect theory says that's the descriptively correct prediction. Design implication: security dashboards should present risks in prospect-theoretic terms (salient loss frames, concrete reference points), because expected-value presentations systematically undersell low-probability catastrophes to exactly the people who need to act.

Example 2: Present bias and the energy retrofit

A homeowner can install a smart thermostat for $200, saving $25/month. Exponential discounting at δ = 0.99/month says install (payback in 8 months, NPV positive). With β = 0.7 present bias, today's self compares −200 + 0.7×(discounted savings stream): the immediate $200 looms 1/0.7 ≈ 1.43× larger than the future savings feel, and installation keeps slipping to "next month" — the classic energy-efficiency gap. A naïf keeps believing she'll do it next month; a sophisticate signs an on-bill financing contract (the $200 amortized into the bill, commitment via the utility). Policy: an opt-out (default) enrollment in the financing plan, with free opt-out, converts naïf procrastination into action — the mechanism is the default effect plus present bias working for, not against, adoption. This is a nudge whose welfare case rests on the internality: the future self wants the savings.

Example 3: Defaults in software — the update nudge

An OS vendor switches security updates from opt-in ("click to install") to opt-out (auto-install with easy deferral). Update compliance jumps from 40% to 92%. Three behavioral mechanisms: (a) present bias — clicking "install now" has immediate hassle cost vs. diffuse future security benefit; the default removes the action; (b) inattention — most users never visit the update screen; the default decides for the inattentive; (c) implied endorsement — users infer the default is recommended. Welfare analysis (Chetty-style): the bias is the gap between the 40% who update under opt-in and the informed preference (surveys show ~90% want auto-updates once informed); the nudge closes most of that gap at near-zero cost, dominating both mandates (which anger the 8% with legitimate reasons to defer — developers, metered connections) and information campaigns (which move the needle ~2pp). Keep the opt-out easy: that's what makes it libertarian paternalism rather than a mandate, and it protects the heterogeneous minority.

Example 4: Fairness in algorithmic allocation

A cloud scheduler must split 100 GPUs between two research teams. Team A contributed 80% of the cluster budget; Team B 20%. The "efficient" allocation (all 100 to whoever values them most this week — say Team A) triggers a Fehr–Schmidt backlash: Team B's utility is x_B − α(x_A − x_B), and with α = 0.5, giving B zero yields U_B = −50 — active sabotage territory (complaints, shadow IT, political escalation). A max-min fair split (50/50) wastes budget-proportionality; a proportional split (80/20) gives U_B = 20 − 0.5(60) = −10, still negative but tolerable, and U_A = 80 − γ(60) with small guilt γ ≈ 0.1 → 74. The behavioral prediction: proportional allocation is the stable one — it matches the equity norm both teams' reference points encode. Lesson: when you design multi-tenant schedulers, optimize perceived-fairness-constrained efficiency, not raw efficiency; the constraint is in the users' utility functions, so it's real.

Key results to remember

  • Prospect theory: reference dependence + loss aversion (λ ≈ 2, concave gains / convex losses) + probability weighting (overweight small p, underweight large p).
  • Quasi-hyperbolic (β–δ) discounting models present bias; naïfs (β̂ = 1) procrastinate and are hurt by flexibility, sophisticates (β̂ = β) demand commitment devices.
  • Social preferences (Fehr–Schmidt inequity aversion; Charness–Rabin reciprocity) explain ultimatum rejections, conditional cooperation, and fairness backlash — fairness is a design constraint.
  • Limited attention/salience (Chetty–Looney–Kroft): shrouded attributes are underweighted; salience is a policy lever and a dark-pattern toolkit.
  • Nudges (defaults, framing, reminders) work through these biases; automatic enrollment is the canonical large-effect example.
  • Behavioral welfare: when revealed preference fails, use sufficient statistics (behavioral elasticity + bias) — Chetty (2015); internalities extend Pigouvian logic to self-control problems.
  • Welfare criterion under bias: "as judged by themselves" (the informed, attentive self); heterogeneity means one nudge rarely fits all — keep opt-outs easy.

Check your understanding

  1. An employee must choose between a sure $500 bonus and a 50% chance of $1,200 (nothing otherwise). Compute what expected utility with u(x) = √x recommends, then explain what prospect theory adds beyond that prediction.
    Answer

    EU: 0.5×√1200 + 0.5×√0 ≈ 0.5×34.64 = 17.32 < √500 ≈ 22.36, so EU recommends the sure $500 (risk aversion from concave utility). Prospect theory adds distinctive predictions EU forbids: with reference point $0, the gamble's upside is a gain (concave, risk-averse — same qualitative call here), but PT predicts risk-seeking to avoid a sure loss (the reflection effect) and overweighting of small probabilities — e.g., the employee might prefer a 1% chance of $60,000 to the sure $500, which concave EU rules out.

  2. Your app wants users to enable two-factor authentication. Design two nudges using two different behavioral mechanisms, and state how you'd test which works.
    Answer

    Nudge 1 (default): make 2FA opt-out during onboarding — mechanism: present bias + inattention; the default decides for the passive. Nudge 2 (loss-framed reminder): "Your account is currently unprotected — 3 of your contacts enabled protection this week" — mechanisms: loss aversion (unprotected = loss frame) + social norms. Test: three-arm RCT (control, default, reminder), pre-registered primary outcome = 2FA enablement within 14 days; compare ITT effects; watch for heterogeneity (sophisticated users may resent the default).

  3. A soda tax is proposed where there are no externalities (no secondhand effects). Standard welfare economics says it's pure deadweight loss. Give the behavioral-welfare counterargument in one paragraph.
    Answer

    With present bias, the present self overconsumes relative to the long-run self's informed preference — an internality. The soda tax corrects this internality the way a Pigouvian tax corrects an externality: if the marginal internality (in dollar terms) equals t*, the tax moves consumption to the as-judged-by-themselves optimum and raises welfare. Chetty's sufficient-statistics formula needs only the demand elasticity and the bias (gap between chosen and normative consumption) to size t* — no full structural model of self-control required.

  4. Why might a well-meaning default (e.g., auto-enrollment in a data-sharing program) be welfare-reducing even if most people stick with it?
    Answer

    Stickiness confounds preference with inertia: inattention and present bias keep people in defaults they would reject if attentive. If the informed majority would actually opt out (privacy preferences), the default traps them — revealed preference fails, so "90% stayed" is not evidence of welfare. Heterogeneity compounds it: the default helps the inattentive majority only if it matches their informed preference; otherwise it's sludge with good branding. The fix: active choice, or check informed preferences via survey/experiment before setting the default.
