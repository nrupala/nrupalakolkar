4.1 Program Evaluation and Empirical Public Policy

Built from Wharton BEPP 9110: Empirical Public Policy

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Every product decision in a technology company is a policy decision: roll out the new recommender, change the pricing page, subsidize EV chargers in a city, or adjust the feed ranking. Engineers measure what happened after the change; program evaluation asks what happened because of the change. The difference between the two is the difference between a dashboard metric and a causal claim. BEPP 9110 is a PhD course about research designs with observational data — how to learn causal effects when you cannot run a perfect experiment. Its toolkit (randomized trials, instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, matching) is exactly the toolkit behind geo experiments, staged rollouts, eligibility thresholds, and quota-based treatments in modern tech firms. If you can run a randomized trial you are done; the rest of this lesson is for all the times you cannot.

Learning objectives

  • Write a causal question in the potential-outcomes framework and name the selection mechanism that blocks naive comparisons.
  • Design and interpret a randomized experiment, reporting both intent-to-treat and the local average treatment effect when compliance is imperfect.
  • Evaluate an instrumental variable: state the exclusion restriction, test instrument strength, and interpret the estimate as a LATE over compliers.
  • Implement difference-in-differences correctly: defend parallel trends with event studies, cluster inference properly, and avoid the staggered-adoption pitfalls of plain two-way fixed effects.
  • Recognize sharp and fuzzy regression discontinuity designs and check their credibility with density and balance tests.

Core content

The potential-outcomes starting point

Begin every causal question by writing the estimand before choosing a method. For unit i, define potential outcomes Y_i(1) under treatment and Y_i(0) under control; the causal effect is Y_i(1) − Y_i(0). We observe only Y_i = D_i · Y_i(1) + (1 − D_i) · Y_i(0), where D_i is the treatment indicator. The fundamental problem: the missing counterfactual. A naive comparison E[Y|D=1] − E[Y|D=0] equals the average treatment effect on the treated plus selection bias, E[Y(0)|D=1] − E[Y(0)|D=0] — the treated would have differed even without treatment. LaLonde (1986) famously showed that standard econometric estimators could not recover experimental benchmarks for job-training programs, launching the modern credibility movement: every design below is a strategy for making that selection-bias term vanish or at least argue it away transparently.

Randomized experiments: the gold standard, used honestly

Randomization makes treatment independent of potential outcomes, so E[Y_i(0)|D_i=1] = E[Y_i(0)|D_i=0] and the simple difference in means is unbiased for the average treatment effect (ATE). Publish the randomization protocol, report attrition and compliance, and pre-specify outcomes. In practice — especially in tech rollouts — compliance is imperfect: some assigned users never see the feature. Then the experiment identifies two things. The intent-to-treat (ITT), the effect of assignment, is policy-relevant ("what happens if we ship this to the treated group?"). If assignment Z only affects outcomes through actual receipt D (the exclusion restriction) and assignment moves take-up monotonically, the ratio ITT / (first stage) identifies the local average treatment effect (LATE) for compliers — units induced into treatment by the assignment (Imbens & Angrist 1994). Report both. An A/B test where only 30% of assigned users actually encounter the change has an ITT diluted by the other 70%; the LATE rescales to the exposed population but generalizes only to compliers.

Instrumental variables: good stories, strong first stages

When treatment is endogenous — units choose it based on things correlated with outcomes — an instrument Z supplies exogenous variation. The IV recipe: (1) relevance, Z shifts D, verifiable in the first stage; (2) exclusion, Z affects Y only through D, which is a story, not a statistic — defend it with economics, not tests; (3) independence, Z is as-good-as-random; (4) monotonicity, Z pushes everyone in the same direction. Under these, two-stage least squares (2SLS) estimates the LATE for compliers: β̂_IV = Cov(Z,Y)/Cov(Z,D). Weak instruments (first-stage F below about 10) bias 2SLS toward OLS and break standard inference — report first-stage F statistics and, when weak, prefer reduced-form evidence or robust (Anderson–Rubin) confidence sets over a fragile point estimate. The Angrist & Krueger (1991) quarter-of-birth instrument for schooling is the canonical example; the tech analogue is encouragement designs — randomly assigned nudges, notifications, or eligibility lotteries that shift adoption of a feature you cannot force.

Difference-in-differences: parallel trends or nothing

With a treated and a control group observed before and after a policy, DiD estimates β̂ = (Ȳ_treated,post − Ȳ_treated,pre) − (Ȳ_control,post − Ȳ_control,pre). The identifying assumption is parallel trends: absent treatment, the two groups would have evolved the same way. Levels may differ; trends must not. Two disciplines are mandatory. First, plot an event study: estimate separate coefficients for each period relative to treatment and show pre-treatment coefficients hovering around zero — visible pre-trends are disqualifying, and their absence is necessary but not sufficient. Second, get inference right: Bertrand, Duflo & Mullainathan (2004) showed that with serially correlated outcomes, conventional standard errors massively over-reject; cluster at the level of treatment assignment (usually the group, e.g., state or market), and with few clusters use wild bootstrap. The modern complication is staggered adoption: when units adopt treatment at different times, plain two-way fixed effects (TWFE) regressions combine clean 2×2 comparisons with "forbidden" comparisons that use already-treated units as controls, and with heterogeneous effects the estimate can even take the wrong sign. Use the modern estimators (Callaway & Sant'Anna; Sun & Abraham; Borusyak et al.) that aggregate only clean not-yet-treated comparisons. If parallel trends fails, switch to synthetic control or a matched comparison group rather than forcing DiD.

Regression discontinuity: the cutoff is the experiment

When treatment is assigned by a running variable X crossing a cutoff c — a scholarship for test scores above 80, a feature enabled for accounts with 10,000+ followers, a city getting a pilot if its population exceeds a threshold — units just above and just below are nearly identical, and the jump in outcomes at c identifies a causal effect (Hahn, Todd & Van der Klaauw 2001; Lee & Lemieux 2010). In a sharp RDD the cutoff fully determines treatment; in a fuzzy RDD it only shifts the probability of treatment, so the design becomes an IV with the cutoff indicator as instrument, and the estimand is a LATE for compliers at the cutoff. Credibility checks: test the density of X for bunching at the cutoff (the McCrary test) — manipulation by units destroys the design; show covariates are smooth through the cutoff; choose the bandwidth transparently and show robustness across bandwidths; and never control for the running variable with a high-degree global polynomial — use local linear regression within the bandwidth. The estimand is local to the cutoff, so external validity is limited: the effect at the margin is not the effect of the program everywhere.

Matching: selection on observables, stated honestly

Matching and propensity-score methods (Rosenbaum & Rubin 1983) compare treated and control units with similar observed covariates, invoking selection on observables (conditional independence): conditional on X, treatment is as good as random. This is the strongest assumption of the lesson — it is untestable, since the worry is precisely about unobservables. Never present matching as causal without arguing why the observables plausibly capture the selection mechanism. Always check covariate balance before and after matching, report the matched sample's representativeness, and run sensitivity analyses for hidden bias (Rosenbaum bounds). Matching is most credible as a preprocessing step that makes treatment and control groups comparable before applying another design, not as a standalone identification strategy.

Worked examples

Example 1: Staggered city rollout of an EV-charging subsidy

A utility subsidizes home EV chargers, rolling out across 40 cities in 3 waves (months 4, 9, 14) as crews become available. Naive TWFE of charger installations on a subsidy dummy mixes comparisons of late-treated cities against already-treated early cities. With heterogeneous effects (early-adopter cities respond more), the TWFE coefficient can be attenuated or sign-flipped. Correct approach: event-study plot with not-yet-treated cities as controls; estimate group-time average treatment effects à la Callaway–Sant'Anna and aggregate. Suppose the aggregate ATT is +180 installations per 100,000 households per quarter, with pre-period event-study coefficients of −4, +7, −2 (statistically zero) — parallel trends is plausible, and clustering standard errors by city gives a 95% interval of [+95, +265]. The policy conclusion: the subsidy works, and the TWFE number (say +60) would have understated it.

Example 2: Encouragement design for a new ML feature

A platform ships an AI coding assistant but cannot force adoption. It randomly assigns half of 200,000 eligible developers a prominent onboarding nudge (Z). Adoption rises from 12% (control) to 34% (nudged): first-stage effect +0.22, F ≈ 8,500 — a strong instrument. Weekly commits rise by 0.9 on average in the nudged group (ITT). Exclusion requires the nudge to affect commits only through adoption — check: the nudge is a one-time banner with no other content, and placebo metrics (meeting hours) are flat. Monotonicity: no one is discouraged from adopting by the nudge (defiers implausible). LATE = 0.9 / 0.22 ≈ 4.1 extra commits per week for compliers — developers induced to try the assistant. This does not generalize to always-takers (power users who adopted anyway) or never-takers. Python sketch:

import statsmodels.api as sm
# Z: nudge assignment, D: adopted assistant, Y: weekly commits
first = sm.OLS(D, sm.add_constant(Z)).fit()          # first stage
print("F-stat:", first.fvalue)                        # want >> 10
# 2SLS manually (Wald): ITT / first stage
itt = sm.OLS(Y, sm.add_constant(Z)).fit().params[1]
late = itt / first.params[1]                          # ≈ 4.1 commits/week
# In practice: use linearmodels.IV2SLS for correct SEs
from linearmodels.iv import IV2SLS
res = IV2SLS(Y, sm.add_constant(D), None, Z).fit(cov_type="robust")
print(res.summary)

Example 3: Fuzzy RDD at a usage threshold

A cloud provider grants premium support to accounts whose trailing-30-day spend crosses $10,000. Assignment is fuzzy: 78% of accounts just above the cutoff take up support, 6% just below do (some get it via sales exceptions). The cutoff indicator Z instruments actual support D. Running variable: spend. Check: density of spend shows no bunching at $10,000 (McCrary p = 0.42); pre-cutoff covariates (account age, industry mix) are smooth through the cutoff. Bandwidth ±$1,500, local linear. First stage: +0.72 take-up jump. Reduced form: churn falls 1.8 percentage points at the cutoff. Fuzzy RDD estimate: −1.8/0.72 = −2.5 pp churn effect for compliers at the margin — accounts near $10k spend. Note the limitation: this says nothing about the effect on $100k enterprise accounts, who are far from the cutoff.

Example 4: DiD of a pricing-page redesign (a cautionary tale)

A SaaS company redesigns its pricing page for EU visitors in March but not the US. A naive DiD on conversion rates finds +2.1 pp. Event-study coefficients for the 6 pre-months: +0.2, +0.9, +1.4, +1.1, +1.8, +2.0 — a clear upward pre-trend in the EU relative to the US (perhaps EU demand was already growing). Parallel trends fails; the +2.1 pp is mostly trend. The analyst switches to a synthetic control: a weighted combination of non-EU regions matching the EU's pre-period path. The synthetic-control estimate is +0.4 pp, with placebo tests (applying the method to untreated regions) showing +0.4 pp is inside the placebo distribution — no credible effect. Lesson: plot the event study before believing the regression table.

Key results to remember

  • Write the estimand in potential outcomes first; name the selection mechanism that makes treatment endogenous.
  • Randomization identifies the ATE; with imperfect compliance, report ITT and LATE (Imbens & Angrist 1994) together and say which compliers the LATE covers.
  • An IV is only as good as its exclusion restriction (a story, not a test); check relevance with first-stage F and prefer reduced-form or robust inference when instruments are weak.
  • DiD stands or falls on parallel trends: plot event studies, cluster at the treatment-assignment level (Bertrand, Duflo & Mullainathan 2004), and use modern estimators — not plain TWFE — under staggered adoption.
  • Sharp RDD identifies the effect at the cutoff; fuzzy RDD is IV with the cutoff as instrument; test the running-variable density for manipulation and use local linear regression, not global polynomials.
  • Matching requires selection on observables — untestable, so argue it, check balance, and run sensitivity analyses; never present matching alone as causal identification.
  • Match the design to the variation you actually have: encouragement designs and eligibility cutoffs are the tech industry's natural instruments and discontinuities.

Check your understanding

  1. Only 40% of users assigned to a new feature actually use it. The ITT on revenue is +$0.80 per assigned user. What is the LATE for compliers, and what assumption beyond random assignment does it need?
    Answer

    LATE = 0.80 / 0.40 = $2.00 per complier. Beyond random assignment it needs the exclusion restriction (assignment affects revenue only through feature use) and monotonicity (no defiers).

  2. Your DiD event study shows treated and control groups diverging two periods before treatment begins. What do you conclude?
    Answer

    Parallel trends is violated (or anticipation effects exist). Do not report the DiD estimate as causal; investigate anticipation, or switch to synthetic control or a matched design.

  3. In a fuzzy RDD, why do you need a first stage, and what does the ratio of reduced form to first stage estimate?
    Answer

    Because the cutoff shifts but does not determine treatment, the cutoff indicator is an instrument. The ratio (Wald estimator) estimates the LATE for compliers at the cutoff — units whose treatment status flips at the threshold.

  4. You have a first-stage F of 4.2 for your instrument. Your 2SLS estimate is significant at 5%. Should you trust it?
    Answer

    No. With F well below ~10 the instrument is weak: 2SLS is biased toward OLS and conventional standard errors are unreliable. Report the reduced form, use Anderson–Rubin confidence sets, or find a stronger instrument.

  5. Give one reason a matched comparison can still be biased even with perfect covariate balance.
    Answer

    Selection on unobservables: if treatment choice depends on variables you did not measure (e.g., motivation, private information), balance on observables does not remove the bias. Matching assumes conditional independence, which is untestable.
