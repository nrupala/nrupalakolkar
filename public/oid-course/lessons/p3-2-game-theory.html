3.2 Game Theory and Applications

Built from Wharton ECON 6110: Game Theory and Applications

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Multi-agent AI systems, ad auctions, spectrum allocation, platform competition, and even adversarial training are strategic interactions: each agent's best action depends on what the others do. Game theory is the mathematical language for predicting the outcomes of such interactions and for designing the rules so the outcome is the one you want (mechanism design). If you build recommender systems that compete for attention, autonomous agents that negotiate, or markets that allocate compute, you are doing applied game theory whether you name it or not — and the equilibrium concepts below are the fixed points your systems will converge to, or fail to converge to, in production.

Learning objectives

  • Define a game (players, actions, payoffs, timing, information) and match the equilibrium concept to the game form.
  • Compute Nash, subgame perfect, and Bayesian Nash equilibria in canonical models, including mixed strategies.
  • State the folk theorem and explain how repeated interaction sustains cooperation.
  • Analyze auctions: derive revenue equivalence and describe optimal auction design.
  • Model signaling and screening with asymmetric information and apply them to tech-market examples.

Core content

Static games of complete information: Nash equilibrium

A game in strategic form lists players, each player's action set, and payoff functions mapping action profiles to payoffs. A Nash equilibrium (Nash 1950, 1951) is a profile of strategies, one per player, such that each player's strategy is a best response to the others' — no one can profitably deviate unilaterally. It is the fixed point of the best-response correspondence, and existence in finite games follows from the Brouwer or Kakutani fixed-point theorem applied to that correspondence: every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium, possibly in mixed strategies.

Mixed strategies matter: in matching pennies or rock-paper-scissors there is no pure-strategy equilibrium, but each player randomizing 50-50 (or 1/3 each) makes the opponent indifferent, which is exactly the indifference condition that characterizes mixed equilibria — a player mixes only over actions that yield equal expected payoff given others' strategies. Dominance (strictly dominated strategies are never played) and rationalizability (iterated elimination of never-best-responses) are weaker solution concepts that refine predictions without imposing equilibrium beliefs.

The canonical applications are oligopoly: Cournot competition (firms choose quantities; price clears the market) yields the Cournot-Nash equilibrium with price above marginal cost but below the monopoly price, converging to perfect competition as the number of firms grows. Bertrand competition (firms choose prices for homogeneous goods) yields the Bertrand paradox — price equals marginal cost with just two firms — which resolves once you add capacity constraints, differentiation, or dynamics. For an engineer: Cournot is quantity-setting (bandwidth provision, inventory), Bertrand is price-setting (cloud spot pricing, ad bids).

Dynamic games of complete information: subgame perfection

When moves are sequential, Nash equilibrium admits noncredible threats — strategies that threaten punishments the threatener would not want to carry out. Subgame perfect equilibrium (Selten) requires the strategy profile to induce a Nash equilibrium in every subgame: solve by backward induction from the end of the game tree. In the Stackelberg model, the leader commits to a quantity anticipating the follower's reaction function, and earns more than in Cournot — commitment has value, a theme that recurs in mechanism design (Lesson 3.5).

Repeated games change everything. In the infinitely repeated prisoner's dilemma with discount factor δ, cooperation can be sustained as a subgame perfect equilibrium using trigger strategies (cooperate until anyone defects, then punish forever) provided δ is large enough: the one-shot gain from defecting must not exceed the discounted value of lost future cooperation, δ ≥ (deviation gain)/(punishment loss). The folk theorem (Fudenberg and Maskin 1986) generalizes: with sufficiently patient players, any feasible, individually rational payoff profile can be sustained as a subgame perfect equilibrium. This is the theoretical foundation of reputation systems, tit-for-tat in peer-to-peer protocols (BitTorrent's choking algorithm is a folk-theorem mechanism), and collusion in oligopoly. It also warns you: repeated interaction multiplies equilibria, so predicting which one emerges requires extra structure (renegotiation-proofness, learning, evolutionary stability).

Bargaining gets its canonical treatment in Rubinstein (1982): alternating offers with discounting yield a unique subgame perfect equilibrium splitting the surplus according to patience — the more patient player gets more. The Nash bargaining solution axiomatizes the same split. Any negotiation protocol you design for autonomous agents (contract nets, automated procurement) implicitly chooses a bargaining game; Rubinstein tells you what division to expect.

Games of incomplete information: Bayesian Nash equilibrium

Harsanyi (1967–68) showed how to model private information: each player has a type (private valuation, private cost) drawn from a commonly known distribution, and strategies map types to actions. A Bayesian Nash equilibrium is a profile of type-contingent strategies where each type maximizes interim expected payoff given beliefs about others' types. Auctions are the flagship application.

Consider the standard symmetric independent-private-values model: n bidders, values v_i drawn i.i.d. from distribution F. In a second-price (Vickrey) auction, bidding your true value is a weakly dominant strategy — the price you pay is the second-highest bid, independent of your own bid, so shading can only make you lose a profitable win or win at a loss. In a first-price auction, bidders shade: the symmetric equilibrium bid is b(v) = E[Y | Y < v], the expected highest competing value conditional on winning, where Y is the max of others' values. With values uniform on [0,1], b(v) = ((n−1)/n)·v.

The revenue equivalence theorem: under independent private values, risk neutrality, and symmetry, any auction that (a) always awards the good to the highest-value bidder and (b) gives zero expected surplus to the lowest type raises the same expected revenue — first-price, second-price, English, and Dutch auctions are all equivalent. The proof is the envelope theorem applied to the bidder's interim payoff: the allocation rule pins down the payment rule. This is why Google could switch ad-auction formats without (in theory) changing revenue — and why deviations from the assumptions (affiliation, asymmetry, risk aversion, common values) are where real auction design lives. Milgrom and Weber (1982) extend the analysis to affiliated (positively correlated) values, where the English auction's information revelation raises revenue — the linkage principle.

Myerson (1981) solves optimal auction design: the revenue-maximizing mechanism allocates to the bidder with the highest virtual valuation ψ(v) = v − (1−F(v))/f(v), provided ψ is increasing (regularity), and excludes bidders with negative virtual values via a reserve price r* solving ψ(r*) = 0. The optimal auction is a Vickrey auction with a reserve price. This is mechanism design's crown jewel and the direct ancestor of the sponsored-search and spectrum auctions in Lesson 3.7.

Dynamic games of incomplete information: signaling and screening

When privately informed players move sequentially, beliefs must be updated by Bayes' rule on the equilibrium path: perfect Bayesian equilibrium adds belief-consistency to sequential rationality. The two canonical information-revelation models are mirror images. In Spence (1973) job-market signaling, the informed party moves first: high-ability workers get costly education to separate from low-ability workers, and education has value purely as a signal even if it teaches nothing — the single-crossing property (education is cheaper, in utility terms, for high types) makes separation possible. In screening (Rothschild and Stiglitz 1976), the uninformed party moves first, offering a menu of contracts that induces types to self-select — the insurance-market model where deductibles screen risk types. Both models live at the heart of Lesson 3.5's contract theory; both exhibit equilibrium multiplicity (pooling vs. separating), disciplined by refinements like the intuitive criterion.

Worked examples

Example 1: Spectrum sharing as a prisoner's dilemma

Two wireless operators share unlicensed spectrum. Each can Cooperate (limit transmit power, coordinate channels) or Defect (blast at full power). Payoffs: (C,C) → (3,3); (D,C) → (5,0); (C,D) → (0,5); (D,D) → (1,1). The unique Nash equilibrium is (D,D) — mutual interference — though both prefer (C,C). Now suppose they interact repeatedly with discount factor δ per period (the value of continued coexistence). A grim-trigger strategy sustains (C,C) if 3/(1−δ) ≥ 5 + δ·1/(1−δ), i.e., δ ≥ 1/2. If operators value the future enough (stable market, long licenses), cooperation is an equilibrium; with short horizons or high churn, the folk theorem's condition fails and interference is the prediction. This is why spectrum regulators mandate etiquette protocols (listen-before-talk): they change the stage game so cooperation needs no patience.

Example 2: First-price cloud spot auction

Three startups bid for a spare GPU cluster; values are i.i.d. uniform on [0, 100] dollars/hour. In a first-price sealed-bid auction the symmetric equilibrium bid is b(v) = ((n−1)/n)·v = (2/3)v. A startup valuing the cluster at $90 bids $60. Expected revenue is E[second-highest value] = $50 (the expected second order statistic of 3 uniforms on [0,100] is (n−1)/(n+1)·100 = 50). In a second-price auction everyone bids truthfully and the winner pays the second-highest value — same $50 expected revenue, illustrating revenue equivalence. Now add a reserve price r: Myerson's optimal reserve solves ψ(r) = r − (100−r)/1 = 2r − 100 = 0, so r* = $50. Setting the reserve at $50 excludes low-value outcomes where the virtual valuation is negative and raises expected revenue — the single most useful formula in practical auction design.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n_sim, n = 200_000, 3
vals = rng.uniform(0, 100, size=(n_sim, n))
# First-price equilibrium bids
bids = (n - 1) / n * vals
fp_rev = bids.max(axis=1).mean()
# Second-price revenue = second-highest value
sp_rev = np.sort(vals, axis=1)[:, -2].mean()
# Optimal reserve r*=50 in a second-price auction
r = 50
above = vals.max(axis=1) >= r
sp_res_rev = np.where(above, np.maximum(np.sort(vals, axis=1)[:, -2], r), 0).mean()
print(f"First-price revenue:  {fp_rev:.2f}")
print(f"Second-price revenue: {sp_rev:.2f}")
print(f"Second-price w/ reserve 50: {sp_res_rev:.2f}")

Example 3: Signaling in a hiring market for ML engineers

Candidates are high-ability (productivity 100) or low-ability (productivity 40), equally likely; employers cannot observe ability. A Kaggle-style certification costs high types 10 units of effort and low types 30 (single crossing). A separating equilibrium: high types certify, low types don't; employers pay 100 to certified and 40 to uncertified. Incentive compatibility: high types get 100 − 10 = 90 ≥ 40 (no certify); low types get 40 ≥ 100 − 30 = 70 (certify). Both hold, so separation is an equilibrium — and the certification is socially wasteful (it doesn't raise productivity) yet privately valuable. There is also a pooling equilibrium (nobody certifies, wage 70) sustained by pessimistic off-path beliefs. The intuitive criterion selects separation here: if an employer saw a certification, the only type that could plausibly benefit is the high type. Lesson: credentials in tech hiring can be pure signals; designing cheaper, more informative assessments is a mechanism-design problem.

Example 4: Bertrand competition in cloud storage pricing

Two cloud providers sell undifferentiated object storage with marginal cost $0.02/GB-month. In the one-shot Bertrand game, each undercuts the other until price equals marginal cost: the unique Nash equilibrium is p₁ = p₂ = $0.02, zero profit — the Bertrand paradox. Now add realism: (a) capacity constraints (each can serve only half the market) soften competition — the Edgeworth resolution; (b) differentiation (egress fees, ecosystem lock-in) makes demand less than perfectly elastic; (c) repeated interaction lets the folk theorem sustain p = $0.05 with trigger punishments, i.e., tacit collusion. An engineer building a price-comparison or auto-migration tool should know which regime the market is in: under (c), the tool's entry can destroy the collusive equilibrium and trigger a price war — the tool is itself a move in the game.

Key results to remember

  • Nash equilibrium: mutual best responses; every finite game has one (Nash 1950/51, fixed-point argument); mixed equilibria are characterized by indifference.
  • Subgame perfection (Selten) eliminates noncredible threats via backward induction; Stackelberg leadership shows commitment has value.
  • Folk theorem: with patient players, repeated interaction sustains any feasible individually rational payoff — cooperation, reputation, and collusion all live here.
  • Rubinstein (1982): alternating-offer bargaining splits surplus by patience; the protocol determines the division.
  • Bayesian Nash equilibrium handles private types; in second-price auctions truthful bidding is dominant; in first-price auctions bidders shade to E[highest rival value | winning].
  • Revenue equivalence: under IPV, symmetry, and risk neutrality, all standard auctions that award to the highest value raise equal expected revenue.
  • Myerson (1981): the optimal auction allocates by virtual valuation ψ(v) = v − (1−F(v))/f(v) with reserve ψ(r*) = 0 — a Vickrey auction with reserve.
  • Spence signaling vs. Rothschild–Stiglitz screening: who moves first determines whether information is signaled or screened; single crossing enables separation.

Check your understanding

  1. In the spectrum-sharing prisoner's dilemma above, suppose a new entrant arrives each period so each operator expects the interaction to end with probability 1/3 per period. Can (C,C) be sustained? What does this say about unlicensed spectrum with high churn?
    Answer

    The effective discount factor is δ = 2/3 (continuation probability). Sustaining cooperation needs δ ≥ 1/2, and 2/3 ≥ 1/2, so yes — grim trigger still works. But if churn rose so the continuation probability fell below 1/2, cooperation would collapse to (D,D). High churn destroys the folk-theorem logic: with strangers you meet once, defect.

  2. Two bidders, values uniform on [0,1]. What is the equilibrium bid in a first-price auction for a bidder with value 0.8, and what is the seller's expected revenue? Verify revenue equivalence against the second-price format.
    Answer

    Equilibrium bid: b(v) = ((n−1)/n)v = v/2, so b(0.8) = 0.4. Expected revenue in either format equals E[second-highest value] = E[min of two uniforms] = 1/3. In the first-price auction the winner pays b(max value) = max/2, whose expectation is E[max]/2 = (2/3)/2 = 1/3. ✓

  3. A platform switches its ad auction from second-price to first-price. A naive analyst predicts revenue will rise because "winners pay their full bid now." What does revenue equivalence say, and under which violated assumption could the analyst actually be right?
    Answer

    Under IPV, symmetry, and risk neutrality, expected revenue is identical — the analyst's intuition ignores bid shading, which exactly offsets the payment rule. The analyst could be right if bidders are risk-averse (risk aversion raises first-price revenue, since bidders shade less to insure against losing) or values are affiliated (then second-price/English does better by the linkage principle).

  4. In the hiring-signal example, the certification cost low types 30. Suppose an online proctored exam cuts both types' costs in half (high: 5, low: 15). Does separation survive? Is that good or bad for employers?
    Answer

    Check IC: high gets 100 − 5 = 95 ≥ 40 ✓; low gets 40 ≥ 100 − 15 = 85? No — 40 < 85, so low types now certify too; separation collapses to pooling at wage 70. Employers are worse off (they lose the information), though total wasteful signaling cost falls. Cheaper signals can destroy informativeness — a key screening-design tradeoff.
