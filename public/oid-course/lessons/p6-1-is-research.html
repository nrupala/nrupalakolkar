6.1 Information Systems Research

Built from Wharton OIDD 9550: Research Seminar in Information Systems

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

OIDD 9550 is the Wharton OID doctoral seminar that defines Information Systems as a research field: the economics of IT, platforms, and digitization. If you build AI systems, communications infrastructure, or platform products, you are working inside the very phenomena this literature studies — why IT investments sometimes fail to show up in productivity statistics, how two-sided platforms price access to both sides of a market, and how digitization reshapes the industries your technology enters. This lesson gives you the canon: the results a serious researcher in the economics of technology is expected to know cold.

Learning objectives

  • State the productivity paradox debate and the firm-level evidence that resolved its empirical core.
  • Explain organizational complements as the mechanism linking IT investment to business value.
  • Apply two-sided market pricing logic (Rochet and Tirole) to platform design decisions.
  • Use the Goldfarb and Tucker digital-economics framework to analyze a digitization question.
  • Describe how bundling of information goods raises profit, and when it does not.

Core content

The productivity paradox and IT business value

In 1987 Robert Solow quipped that "you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics." Erik Brynjolfsson's 1993 paper, "The Productivity Paradox of Information Technology," organized the explanations into four groups: mismeasurement (output and inputs, especially quality change and intangible value, are badly measured), lags (payoffs arrive years after investment, as with electrification), redistribution (IT helps individual firms while merely shifting rents around the economy), and mismanagement (firms invest poorly). The IS research program spent the next two decades testing these. The firm-level evidence — Brynjolfsson and Hitt (2000) in the Journal of Economic Perspectives, and especially Brynjolfsson and Hitt (2003) in the Review of Economics and Statistics — found that IT capital does contribute to output and productivity, with estimated gross returns well above those of other capital, but with large variance across firms. That variance is the research question. Meta-analyses (Kohli and Devaraj 2003, Information Systems Research) and integrative models (Melville, Kraemer and Gurbaxani 2004, MIS Quarterly) map IT resources through business processes to organizational performance, conditioning on complementary resources and competitive environment. The famous provocation — Carr's 2003 Harvard Business Review essay "IT Doesn't Matter," arguing IT is infrastructural and commoditized — met its rebuttal in McAfee and Brynjolfsson (2008): IT matters precisely when it is coupled with novel, hard-to-copy business processes, which is when it becomes a source of competitive difference rather than parity.

Organizational complements: why the same servers yield different returns

The central mechanism the literature converged on is complementarity. Bresnahan, Brynjolfsson and Hitt (2002, Quarterly Journal of Economics) showed with firm-level data that IT adoption is associated with increased demand for skilled labor, greater delegation of decision rights, and broader spans of control. The finding is not that computers replace workers; it is that the firms earning high returns on IT reorganize work around it — decentralized decision-making, skilled teams, new processes — and these organizational investments are the true co-investment, often several times the hardware and software spend. For an engineer this reframes the problem: deploying a model or a network is the cheap part; the returns live in the workflow redesign, the retraining, and the decision rights that surround it. An AI rollout evaluated only on model accuracy misses where the value is created and destroyed.

Network effects, compatibility, and two-sided markets

Katz and Shapiro (1985, American Economic Review) founded the modern analysis of network externalities: when a product's value rises with the number of users, competition turns on expectations (which network will win), compatibility choices (adapters, standards), and installed bases. Their framework explains standards wars in communications technology — VHS versus Betamax, or modern protocol battles — as equilibrium selection problems where the technology with the best expectations, not necessarily the best engineering, wins.

Rochet and Tirole (2003, Journal of the European Economic Association) extended this to two-sided markets: platforms that bring together two distinct groups (cardholders and merchants, riders and drivers, developers and users) with cross-side externalities. The central result is that the price structure matters, not just the price level: it is optimal to subsidize the side that is more price-elastic or that creates more value for the other side, and to recover margin from the other. A platform can rationally price below cost — even at zero or negative prices — on one side. Armstrong (2006) added the multi-homing refinement: the side that multi-homes (uses several platforms) gets competed for and pays little; the side that single-homes is where platforms extract rent, because it is the bottleneck through which the other side must pass. Parker and Van Alstyne (2005, Management Science) showed how a platform firm can profitably give away an information product to seed one side and monetize the other — the economic logic behind free developer tools, free consumer apps, and open interfaces that look like charity and are strategy.

Bundling information goods

Bakos and Brynjolfsson (1999, Management Science) showed why bundling is the characteristic pricing strategy of information goods. The logic is statistical: when a seller bundles many goods with near-zero marginal cost, the law of large numbers compresses the distribution of buyers' valuations for the bundle relative to any single good. Valuations that are heterogeneous and not too positively correlated average out, so a single bundle price captures far more surplus than separate prices could. This is why software suites, streaming catalogs, and API bundles dominate: the bundle monetizes the long tail of weak preferences. The result fails when valuations are strongly positively correlated (then bundling adds nothing) or when marginal costs are substantial. Shapiro and Varian's Information Rules (1999) is the book-length treatment of these pricing strategies — versioning, bundling, and lock-in — for information goods.

Digitization: five forces and the empirics

Goldfarb and Tucker's "Digital Economics" (Journal of Economic Literature, 2019) is the modern survey and the standard framing: digitization lowers five costs — search costs, reproduction costs (marginal cost near zero), transportation costs, tracking costs, and verification costs. Every digitization question can be organized as: which of the five costs fell, who benefits, and what new costs (fraud, attention scarcity, privacy loss) rise in response. The empirical canon tests these mechanisms. Brynjolfsson and Smith (2000, Management Science) asked whether the internet delivers "frictionless commerce": online book and CD retailers had lower menu costs and smaller price changes, and lower prices on average — but substantial price dispersion persisted, driven by heterogeneity in awareness, trust, and service, not just search frictions. Brynjolfsson, Hu and Simester (2011, Management Science) showed the long-tail effect: lower search costs shift the sales distribution toward niche products — "goodbye Pareto principle, hello long tail" — which is the demand-side twin of the bundling logic above and the reason recommendation systems carry economic weight.

The decision-technologies side: adoption and success

The IDT half of the seminar studies how information technologies get used. Venkatesh et al. (2003, MIS Quarterly) unified eight acceptance models into UTAUT: use is driven by performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions, moderated by experience, voluntariness, age, and gender. DeLone and McLean's (2003, Journal of Management Information Systems) ten-year update of their IS success model traces system quality, information quality, and service quality through use and user satisfaction to net benefits — the standard causal chain for evaluating any deployed system. Finally, Benbasat and Zmud (2003, MIS Quarterly) forced the field's identity debate: IS is defined by the IT artifact and its nomological net, they argued, and research that drifts too far from the artifact loses the discipline's distinctiveness. For the engineer, the takeaway is methodological: IS research pairs economic theory with empirical identification — econometrics, quasi-experiments, and increasingly experiments on digital platforms — and judges a contribution by whether it explains an economic mechanism, not by whether the technology is novel.

Worked examples

Example 1: Mismeasurement and the paradox, in numbers

A telecom operator spends $40M on a network-operations AI that cuts mean time to repair by 30 percent. Measured output (billed revenue) does not move: fewer outages mean fewer truck rolls, and customers pay flat rates. A naive productivity regression finds zero return. But consumer surplus rose — say 2M subscribers each value avoided outages at $6/year, or $12M/year in unmeasured welfare — and the firm's avoided capex on spare capacity is $8M/year. Brynjolfsson's mismeasurement hypothesis in one ledger: when IT output is quality, variety, or avoided cost rather than counted revenue, the paradox is a measurement artifact, not an economic fact. The Bresnahan–Brynjolfsson–Hitt complementarity story adds the second ledger: the same $40M with reorganized dispatch workflows and retrained technicians might show up as $25M/year of measured gain, because the organizational co-investment converts latent capability into counted output.

Example 2: Two-sided pricing for an EV charging network

You run a charging platform with two sides: drivers (D) and charge-point hosts (H). Drivers value the network at $2 per additional host; hosts value it at $0.50 per additional driver; each side's standalone value is near zero. Per Rochet and Tirole, subsidize the side whose participation creates more cross-side value and is more price-elastic. Suppose acquiring a driver costs $5 in subsidy but each driver attracts hosts worth $0.50 × (drivers per host). With 10,000 drivers and 500 hosts (20 drivers per host), one more driver adds $0.50 × 20 = $10 of value to the host side through denser utilization — but only if hosts single-home. If drivers multi-home across three networks while hosts single-home (one plug standard), Armstrong's logic says the hosts are the bottleneck: charge hosts the access fee, and compete drivers down to zero or negative prices (free charging credits). The engineering instinct says price the scarce physical asset (the charger); the two-sided logic says price the single-homing side, whatever it is.

Example 3: Bundling arithmetic for two software features

Two enterprise features, A and B, each cost you ~$0 to reproduce. 100 customers value A uniformly on [$0, $100] and B uniformly on [$0, $100], independently. Sold separately at the optimal $50 each, each feature sells to 50 customers: revenue 2 × 50 × $50 = $5,000. Now bundle at $100: a customer buys if vA + vB ≥ 100. With independent uniforms, P(vA + vB ≥ 100) = 0.5, so 50 customers buy the bundle: revenue = 50 × $100 = $5,000 — no gain yet, because with two goods the variance reduction is small. Add a third, fourth, …, twentieth feature and the bundle valuation concentrates tightly around its mean ($1,000 for 20 features); pricing the 20-feature bundle at $900 captures nearly all buyers and nearly all surplus: revenue approaches 100 × $900 = $90,000 versus 20 × $2,500 = $50,000 sold separately. This is Bakos and Brynjolfsson's law of large numbers at work — the economic reason your SaaS vendor sells the suite, not the feature.

Key results to remember

  • The productivity paradox (Brynjolfsson 1993) had four candidate explanations — mismeasurement, lags, redistribution, mismanagement — and firm-level evidence (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 2000, 2003) showed IT does raise productivity, unevenly.
  • Organizational complements (Bresnahan, Brynjolfsson and Hitt 2002): IT returns come bundled with skilled labor, decentralized decision rights, and redesigned processes — the co-investment often exceeds the technology spend.
  • Carr (2003) argued IT is commoditized infrastructure; McAfee and Brynjolfsson (2008) replied that IT creates competitive difference exactly when paired with hard-to-copy process innovation.
  • Two-sided pricing (Rochet and Tirole 2003): the price structure matters, not just the level — subsidize the elastic, value-creating side; monetize the single-homing bottleneck (Armstrong 2006).
  • Bundling information goods (Bakos and Brynjolfsson 1999): with near-zero marginal cost and weakly correlated valuations, bundling compresses valuation heterogeneity and captures more surplus — the logic of suites and streaming catalogs.
  • Goldfarb and Tucker (2019): digitization lowers search, reproduction, transportation, tracking, and verification costs; analyze any digital market by asking which cost fell.
  • Online markets are more efficient but not frictionless (Brynjolfsson and Smith 2000): price dispersion persists through awareness, trust, and service heterogeneity; lower search costs fatten the long tail (Brynjolfsson, Hu and Simester 2011).
  • IS success is evaluated through use: UTAUT (Venkatesh et al. 2003) for adoption, DeLone and McLean (2003) for the quality → use → satisfaction → net benefits chain.

Check your understanding

  1. Why can two firms buy identical AI systems and report very different productivity gains, in the Bresnahan–Brynjolfsson–Hitt account?
    Answer

    Returns depend on organizational complements: skilled labor, delegated decision rights, and redesigned workflows. The firm that reorganizes around the technology converts capability into measured output; the firm that drops it into unchanged processes does not.

  2. A platform connects freelance engineers (who use five platforms at once) with enterprise clients (who standardize on one). Which side should bear the platform fee, and why?
    Answer

    The clients. Engineers multi-home, so platforms compete them down to low prices; clients single-home, making them the bottleneck side through which engineers must pass — Armstrong's logic says extract margin from the single-homing side.

  3. When does bundling information goods fail to raise profit over separate sales?
    Answer

    When buyers' valuations are strongly positively correlated (bundling adds no variance reduction), when marginal costs are substantial rather than near zero, or when there are too few goods for the law of large numbers to concentrate bundle valuations.

  4. An online retailer cuts search costs sharply but price dispersion barely moves. Name two mechanisms from Brynjolfsson and Smith (2000) that explain this.
    Answer

    Heterogeneity in consumer awareness (not all shoppers find the low price) and in trust/service (brand, reliability, and returns policies sustain premia); menu-cost and retailer-heterogeneity effects also keep dispersion alive.
