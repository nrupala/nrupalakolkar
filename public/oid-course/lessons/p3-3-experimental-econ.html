3.3 Experimental Economics

Doctoral course basis: Experimental Economics

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

You already run experiments: every A/B test on a product, every bandit algorithm allocating traffic, every pilot rollout of a pricing change is an experiment. Experimental economics gives you the discipline to run them well — randomization done right, validity threats named and checked, and the humility to distinguish what your test actually identified from what you hoped it did. It also supplies the empirical counterpart to Lessons 3.2 and 3.4: lab markets test whether game-theoretic predictions survive contact with human subjects, and field experiments test whether behavioral insights survive contact with real stakes.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish lab, field, and natural experiments and choose the design that fits the research question.
  • Define internal and external validity and name the main threats to each.
  • Design a randomized trial: randomization unit, treatment arms, pre-registration, and minimum detectable effect.
  • Interpret intent-to-treat and local average treatment effects under imperfect compliance.
  • Summarize what experiments in markets and games have taught us about equilibrium predictions.

Core content

Lab vs. field: the control–realism tradeoff

The laboratory experiment, pioneered in economics by Smith (1962), creates an artificial market with induced values: subjects trade in a double auction with experimenter-assigned costs and valuations, and the question is whether the competitive equilibrium of Lesson 3.1 emerges. It does — with striking reliability, even with few traders and no one knowing the equilibrium price in advance. The lab buys internal validity (tight control of the environment, exact replication of the institution) at the price of artificiality: student subjects, small stakes, scrutiny effects.

Field experiments (Harrison and List 2004) move the randomization into naturally occurring environments with real stakes — job training programs, auction platforms, electricity pricing pilots. They buy realism at the price of control: the experimenter cannot hold everything else fixed, attrition and spillovers threaten the design, and each field setting is in some way unique. The modern consensus is a portfolio: use the lab to test mechanisms (does the institution produce the predicted outcome when the theory's assumptions hold?) and the field to test policy effects (does the intervention move the outcome where it matters?). A result that survives both is far more credible than one that survives either alone.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are the workhorse: randomly assign units to treatment and control, and the difference in means identifies the average treatment effect, because randomization makes treatment independent of potential outcomes. The development-economics RCT literature (Banerjee and Duflo and coauthors) scaled this to policy evaluation worldwide. For tech: your A/B test is an RCT, and everything below applies to it.

Design: pre-registration, power, and the randomization unit

Good design starts before data collection. Pre-register the hypothesis, the primary outcome, and the analysis plan: this disciplines the forking paths of analysis (which subgroup? which specification? which outcome?) that turn noise into "findings." Choose the randomization unit to match the interference structure — randomize at the user level when treatments don't spill over, at the market or cluster level when they do (randomizing electricity prices household-by-household fails if neighbors talk; randomize feeders instead). More arms and finer randomization buy precision but cost sample; the minimum detectable effect (MDE) translates your sample size, variance, and power target into the smallest effect you could reliably see. An underpowered experiment cannot distinguish "no effect" from "couldn't see the effect" — null results must be read through power, not through prior belief.

Internal validity: is the comparison clean?

Internal validity asks whether the estimated difference is the causal effect of the treatment. Check balance: randomization should make treatment and control look alike on pre-treatment covariates — test it, and worry if it fails (it can fail by chance, especially in small samples; stratification and re-randomization help). Watch attrition: if treated subjects drop out differentially (a demanding new interface drives users away), the survivors are a selected sample and the comparison is biased. Watch spillovers (SUTVA violations): in network products, treating one user changes her friends' outcomes, contaminating the control group — cluster randomization or explicit interference modeling is the fix. Watch experimenter demand effects: subjects who know they are studied may behave to please the experimenter, a particular hazard in lab settings and in any pilot where participants know management is watching.

External validity: does it travel?

External validity asks whether the effect generalizes — to other populations, other settings, other scales. State the population your sample represents before claiming anything beyond it: undergraduates in a lab are not gig drivers; a pilot in one city is not a national rollout. Scale-up changes behavior (general-equilibrium effects: treating everyone moves prices), and site-selection bias means the places that agree to experiments differ from those that don't. The honest move is to name the transport assumptions explicitly: the result travels to settings where the mechanism operates the same way, and you should say which features of the setting those are.

Imperfect compliance: ITT and LATE

In field settings, assignment is not receipt: some assigned to treatment don't take it up, some controls get treated anyway. The intent-to-treat (ITT) effect — the effect of being assigned — is always identified and is the policy-relevant quantity (a policymaker controls assignment, not compliance). But ITT dilutes the effect of the treatment itself. Under the Imbens and Angrist (1996) assumptions (random assignment, exclusion, monotonicity — no defiers), instrumental-variables estimation using assignment as an instrument for receipt identifies the local average treatment effect (LATE): the effect on compliers, those who take treatment if and only if assigned. LATE is not the average effect on everyone — it is local to compliers — and reporting both ITT and LATE, with the compliance rate, is the standard.

What experiments taught us about markets and games

Four findings anchor the field. First, competitive market equilibrium is robust: double-auction experiments converge to the predicted price and quantity with remarkable speed (Smith 1962) — the welfare theorems of Lesson 3.1 survive the lab. Second, asset bubbles happen anyway: in experimental asset markets with known fundamental value, prices reliably bubble and crash (Smith, Suchanek, and Williams) — common knowledge of rationality is not enough to prevent them, a caution for any market mechanism you design. Third, public-goods games show conditional cooperation: many subjects contribute if others do, and contributions decay without punishment — but peer punishment sustains them (Fehr and Gächter), grounding the folk-theorem logic of Lesson 3.2 in behavior. Fourth, bargaining experiments systematically deviate from subgame-perfect predictions: ultimatum-game responders reject unfair offers, revealing the social preferences (fairness, reciprocity) that Lesson 3.4 formalizes. The lab thus both confirms the price mechanism and disciplines the game theory: equilibrium concepts survive where institutions aggregate, and fail where psychology intrudes.

Worked examples

Example 1: Designing an A/B test for a new ranking model

You want to test a new recommendation ranker on click-through rate. Design: randomize at the user level (treatments don't spill across users much), two arms (control: current ranker; treatment: new ranker), primary outcome pre-registered as CTR per session over 14 days. With baseline CTR 5%, standard deviation ≈ 21.8% per session, and 100,000 sessions per arm, the MDE at 80% power is roughly 0.13 percentage points — you can detect a 2.6% relative lift. Threats: (a) novelty effects — users click new layouts out of curiosity, inflating early treatment effects (run long enough to wash out); (b) interference — if the ranker changes what content creators produce, control users see a different content pool (SUTVA violation; consider cluster randomization by content cohort); (c) peeking — checking significance daily and stopping when p < 0.05 inflates false positives (pre-commit to the runtime or use sequential testing). This is BEPP 9040's design checklist applied to your deploy pipeline.

Example 2: A field experiment on time-of-use electricity pricing

A utility wants to know whether time-of-use pricing cuts peak demand. Randomize 5,000 households: control stays on flat rates, treatment gets peak/off-peak pricing with in-home displays. Randomization unit: the household, but check spillovers — neighbors on different rates talk, and peer effects could contaminate controls; the design stratifies by neighborhood and tests for spillover by comparing control households near many treated ones versus isolated controls. Compliance is imperfect: 20% of treated households never look at the display (never-takers). ITT = −3% peak demand (effect of being assigned). With 80% compliance, LATE = ITT/0.8 = −3.75% (effect on compliers). External validity: the volunteer sample is more engaged than average, and a national rollout would move wholesale prices (general-equilibrium effect) — so the −3.75% is an upper bound on system-wide savings. Every caveat here has a named validity concept behind it.

Example 3: Replicating the double auction in a classroom market

Twelve students get buyer cards (values $10, $9, …, down) and seller cards (costs $2, $3, …); they trade in a double auction (anyone can bid or ask at any time). The competitive equilibrium price is $6 with 5 units traded. Run it: transaction prices start scattered ($8, $4, $7…) and converge to $6 within three or four rounds; total surplus approaches the maximum. This is Smith (1962) in miniature, and it teaches two things no theorem can: (1) equilibrium emerges from decentralized haggling with zero common knowledge of supply and demand — the price system aggregates dispersed information; (2) it takes a few rounds — convergence is a dynamic process, which is why market-design details (tick size, clearing rules) matter for speed. If you ever build a matching or trading mechanism, run this experiment on it before trusting the theory.

Key results to remember

  • Lab experiments (Smith 1962) test mechanisms with tight control; field experiments (Harrison and List 2004) test policy effects with real stakes; use both, for different questions.
  • Pre-register hypotheses, primary outcomes, and analysis plans; choose the randomization unit to match the interference structure; compute the minimum detectable effect before running.
  • Internal validity threats: imbalance, attrition, spillovers (SUTVA), experimenter demand effects. Check each by name.
  • External validity: state the population your sample represents; name the transport assumptions before generalizing; scale-up changes behavior.
  • With imperfect compliance, report intent-to-treat (effect of assignment, always identified) and LATE via Imbens–Angrist IV (effect on compliers).
  • Read null results through statistical power, not prior belief; an underpowered null is uninformative.
  • Substantive lessons: double auctions converge to competitive equilibrium; asset bubbles occur despite known fundamentals; public-goods cooperation needs punishment; ultimatum rejections reveal social preferences.

Check your understanding

  1. Your team A/B tests a new checkout flow, randomizing by session. A user often has multiple sessions, sometimes in treatment and sometimes in control. Name two validity problems and how you would fix them.
    Answer

    (1) Interference/carryover: a user's treated session changes behavior in later control sessions (learning, annoyance) — violates SUTVA. Fix: randomize at the user level with sticky assignment. (2) The effective sample size is overstated if sessions are treated as independent — cluster standard errors at the user level. Bonus: novelty effects argue for a longer runtime.

  2. A job-training RCT finds ITT = +$800/year earnings with 50% compliance. A policymaker asks "what is the effect of the training?" Give the careful answer.
    Answer

    The effect of being offered training (ITT) is +$800 — the policy-relevant number since the policymaker controls offers, not attendance. Under Imbens–Angrist assumptions, the effect on compliers (LATE) is $800/0.5 = $1,600. Neither is "the" effect on everyone: never-takers and always-takers may differ from compliers, so don't extrapolate LATE to the full population.

  3. An asset-bubble experiment shows prices exceeding fundamental value even though every trader can compute fundamentals. Does this refute the First Welfare Theorem? Explain.
    Answer

    No. The First Welfare Theorem is about competitive equilibrium allocations being Pareto efficient given the equilibrium concept — it doesn't claim markets instantly reach equilibrium or that expectations are rational. Bubbles are a failure of common knowledge / backward induction in a dynamic game, not a counterexample to static efficiency. They do warn that equilibrium selection and dynamics matter for market design.
