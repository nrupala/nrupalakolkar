4.4 Experimentation at Scale: A/B Testing for Tech Platforms

Bridge lesson — connecting OID core to AI, technology, and electrical systems

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Lesson 4.1 taught randomized experiments as the gold standard of causal inference. This lesson is what happens when the gold standard meets a production system serving hundreds of millions of users: experiments run continuously, dozens overlap, users interfere with each other through networks and marketplaces, product managers peek at dashboards daily, and the question is often not "which variant wins" but "how fast can we learn while losing as little as possible." Online controlled experimentation is the engineering discipline built on top of the statistics — randomization infrastructure, variance reduction, sequential decision-making, and interference-aware design. For an AI/EE engineer it is the highest-leverage empirical skill in the whole part: every model you ship, every ranking change, every pricing page should pass through it.

Learning objectives

  • Choose the randomization unit for an online experiment and diagnose SUTVA violations from interference.
  • Apply variance-reduction techniques (CUPED) to shrink experiment runtime without changing the design.
  • Explain why naive peeking inflates false positives and name the fixes: alpha spending, group-sequential designs, and always-valid inference.
  • Design around network and marketplace interference with cluster randomization, switchbacks, and egocentric designs.
  • Decide between A/B testing and multi-armed bandits based on whether the goal is learning or earning.

Core content

Randomization units and the SUTVA assumption

The textbook experiment randomizes users; production reality is messier. The unit of randomization (user, session, pageview, device, geo) should match the unit at which the treatment acts and the metric is computed. Randomizing pageviews when the treatment is a logged-in feature creates cross-contamination (one user sees both variants); randomizing users when the metric is per-session is fine but noisier. The deep assumption is SUTVA — the stable unit treatment value assumption: one user's outcome must not depend on another's assignment. SUTVA fails constantly in tech: a messaging feature's value depends on how many of your contacts have it (network effects), a pricing experiment on a marketplace shifts supply and demand for everyone, and a feed-ranking change alters the content pool all users draw from. Before running the experiment, write down the interference channels; if they are first-order, a naive user-randomized A/B does not estimate any well-defined causal effect — it estimates a mush of direct and spillover effects. Diagnose with a holdout or by comparing cluster-randomized and user-randomized estimates; a gap between them is the signature of interference.

Variance reduction: CUPED and friends

Experiment runtime is set by variance: to detect an effect of size δ with metric variance σ² you need n ∝ σ²/δ² users. CUPED (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data) shrinks σ² without touching the design: regress the outcome Y on pre-experiment covariates X (e.g., each user's pre-period metric value) and analyze the residual Y − θ̂X. Because X is measured before randomization, it is independent of treatment, so the adjusted estimator stays unbiased while its variance falls by a factor of (1 − ρ²), where ρ is the pre-post correlation. With ρ = 0.7 — typical for engagement metrics — variance halves, and the experiment needs half the traffic or half the time. The same idea generalizes: any pre-treatment covariate (device type, signup cohort, historical spend) can enter the adjustment, and stratification or post-stratification on strong predictors buys similar gains. The discipline: covariates must be pre-treatment (never adjust for post-treatment variables — that is a bad control), and the adjustment model should be simple and pre-specified to avoid overfitting the experiment itself.

Peeking, sequential testing, and always-valid inference

Product teams watch dashboards daily and stop experiments when the p-value dips below 0.05. This peeking destroys the nominal error rate: with continuous monitoring, the chance of ever seeing p < 0.05 under the null approaches 1 as the experiment runs longer — you are essentially guaranteed a "significant" result eventually. Three principled fixes. Group-sequential designs: pre-specify a small number of interim looks and spend the α budget across them (O'Brien–Fleming boundaries are conservative early, lenient late). Alpha spending functions: generalize to arbitrary look times while controlling the cumulative Type I error. Always-valid p-values and confidence sequences: construct intervals that are valid at every stopping time, so teams can peek freely and stop whenever — the modern standard at large experimentation platforms, built on martingale / mixture sequential-probability-ratio-test theory. The practical rule: either fix the sample size in advance and do not look, or use a sequential method designed for looking — never a fixed-horizon test with a wandering eye.

Interference: cluster randomization, switchbacks, egocentric designs

When SUTVA fails, change the design, not the wish. Cluster randomization randomizes groups (cities, schools, network communities) so that most interference stays inside the cluster; the estimand becomes the total effect including within-cluster spillovers, at the cost of fewer effective units and the need to cluster standard errors (the Petersen lesson from 4.3, again). Switchback (time-split) experiments alternate treatment on and off over time for the whole system — the standard for marketplaces and pricing where user-level randomization is meaningless because prices are market-level; carryover effects are the threat, so include washout periods and model temporal correlation. Egocentric randomization treats a focal user and measures outcomes on their network neighbors, isolating spillover effects directly. For two-sided platforms, two-sided randomization (e.g., randomize both listings and searchers in Airbnb-style experiments) separates direct effects from equilibrium effects. The honest analysis reports both the naive and the interference-aware estimate and interprets their difference.

Bandits vs. A/B: learning versus earning

A/B testing is optimized for learning: unbiased estimation of the treatment effect with controlled error rates. Multi-armed bandits optimize for earning: maximizing cumulative reward while learning, formalized as minimizing regret — the gap between the reward earned and the reward of always playing the best arm. Thompson sampling and UCB algorithms shift traffic toward winners during the experiment, which is ideal when the cost of showing the inferior variant is high (e.g., checkout flows, ad creative) and the decision is reversible. But bandit data are adaptively collected: naive sample means are biased, standard inference is invalid, and you cannot easily answer "by how much did B beat A?" — the learning goal. The decision rule: use A/B (possibly sequential) when you need a defensible causal estimate to justify a permanent launch or a publication-grade claim; use bandits when the priority is cumulative performance during the test and the winner will simply be kept. Hybrids exist — run a bandit for optimization, then a confirmatory A/B for the finalists — but do not compute p-values on bandit data and call it an experiment.

Running the platform: ramps, guardrails, and holdouts

Industrial experimentation adds operational layers. Ramp-up: start at 1–5% traffic to bound risk, then ramp to full power once guardrail metrics (crashes, latency, revenue per user) clear — the early ramp is a safety check, not a decision point, because it is underpowered by design. Guardrail metrics: a small set of must-not-regress outcomes monitored with the same rigor as the primary metric; a win on clicks that tanks retention is not a win. Mutual-exclusion layers and overlapping experiments: at scale, dozens of experiments share traffic; orthogonal randomization (hash-based bucketing with independent salts per experiment layer) keeps them from confounding each other, and interaction checks catch the cases where they do. Long-term holdouts: keep a small permanent control group to measure novelty effects, learning effects, and the cumulative impact of the launch program — the short experiment measures the immediate effect; the holdout measures what the business actually experiences. Document everything: the randomization seed, the population definition, the metric definitions, and the decision — the experiment you cannot reconstruct is the experiment you cannot trust.

Worked examples

Example 1: CUPED halves a feed-ranking experiment's runtime

A social app tests a new ranking model on "time spent per user per day." Baseline variance is high: pre-post correlation of the metric is ρ = 0.65. The standard A/B needs 3 weeks at 10% traffic to detect a 1% lift. With CUPED — regressing post-period time on each user's pre-period time and analyzing residuals — variance falls by (1 − 0.65²) ≈ 0.58, so the required sample falls 42%: the experiment reaches the same power in under 2 weeks, or detects a 0.76% lift in the original 3 weeks. Implementation detail: θ̂ is estimated from pre-period data pooled across arms (or from the control arm) so the adjustment cannot absorb treatment effects. The team pre-registers the CUPED specification alongside the raw metric; both are reported, and they agree.

Example 2: Peeking turns a null into a "win" (simulation)

A team runs an experiment with a true effect of zero and checks the p-value daily for 28 days, stopping the first day p < 0.05. Simulate this 10,000 times: the naive procedure "finds significance" in roughly 25–30% of runs instead of 5% — the false-positive rate is quintupled by peeking. With a group-sequential design (looks at days 7, 14, 21, 28 with O'Brien–Fleming boundaries) the false-positive rate returns to 5%, at the cost of slightly wider early boundaries. Alternatively, an always-valid confidence sequence lets the team look every day and stop whenever the interval excludes zero, with the 5% guarantee intact. Python sketch of the peeking problem:

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
sims, days, n_per_day = 10_000, 28, 5_000
false_pos = 0
for _ in range(sims):
    # true effect zero; accumulate users, peek daily
    diffs = rng.normal(0, 1, (days, n_per_day)).mean(axis=1)
    for d in range(1, days + 1):
        se = 1 / np.sqrt(d * n_per_day)
        if abs(diffs[:d].mean() / se) > 1.96:   # naive peek
            false_pos += 1
            break
print("false positive rate with peeking:", false_pos / sims)  # ≈ 0.25-0.30

Example 3: Switchback pricing on a ride-hail marketplace

User-level randomization is impossible for a city-wide price multiplier — every rider sees the same price. The team runs a switchback: alternate 4-hour blocks of multiplier 1.2× vs. 1.0× over 6 weeks, randomizing block order. Carryover is the threat: a 1.2× block may suppress demand into the next block. Design response: 30-minute washout buffers between blocks (excluded from analysis) and a model with lagged treatment terms; a robustness check compares estimates with and without the first hour after each switch. Result: +1.2% revenue per hour at 1.2×, but driver utilization falls 3% — the equilibrium effect a user-level test could never have measured. Standard errors account for temporal correlation (Newey–West / block bootstrap), not the naive i.i.d. formula.

Example 4: Bandit for push-notification copy, A/B for the launch decision

Marketing wants the best of 8 notification wordings. A Thompson-sampling bandit allocates sends adaptively over 2 weeks, minimizing regret — users mostly receive good copy during the test, and the bandit identifies 2 clear finalists having spent only 15% of sends on the 6 losers. But the bandit's data cannot support a clean "winner beats control by x%" claim (adaptive allocation biases naive means). So the team runs a confirmatory fixed-horizon A/B: finalist A vs. finalist B vs. control, 50/25/25, pre-registered metric (7-day retention), no peeking. The A/B estimates +0.6 pp retention for the winner, p = 0.02 — the number that goes into the launch review. Learning goal and earning goal, each with the right tool.

Key results to remember

  • Choose the randomization unit to match the treatment's reach; write down interference channels and check SUTVA before trusting a user-level A/B.
  • CUPED cuts variance by (1 − ρ²) using pre-experiment covariates — free power with no design change, as long as covariates are pre-treatment.
  • Naive peeking inflates the false-positive rate toward 1; fix the horizon and do not look, or use group-sequential / always-valid methods built for looking.
  • Under interference, redesign: cluster randomization for network spillovers, switchbacks for market-level treatments, egocentric designs to measure spillovers directly.
  • Bandits minimize regret (earning); A/B tests estimate effects with controlled error (learning). Do not compute p-values on adaptively collected bandit data.
  • Ramp for safety, monitor guardrail metrics with full rigor, isolate overlapping experiments with independent randomization layers, and keep long-term holdouts for novelty and cumulative effects.
  • An experiment you cannot reconstruct — seeds, populations, metric definitions, decisions — is an experiment you cannot trust. Document everything.

Check your understanding

  1. A messaging feature's value depends on contacts also having it. You run a standard user-randomized A/B. What goes wrong?
    Answer

    SUTVA is violated: treated users' outcomes depend on their contacts' assignments (spillovers). The estimate mixes direct and network effects and does not identify a well-defined causal effect — consider cluster or egocentric randomization.

  2. Pre-post correlation of your metric is ρ = 0.8. Roughly how much does CUPED shrink the required sample?
    Answer

    Variance falls by (1 − 0.8²) = 0.36, so the required sample is about 36% of the unadjusted size — a ~64% reduction.

  3. Your team peeks daily at a fixed-horizon test and stops when p < 0.05. The true effect is zero. Is the false-positive rate 5%?
    Answer

    No — repeated peeking inflates it far above 5% (toward 25–30% over a month of daily looks in the simulation). Use a pre-fixed horizon without looking, or a sequential/always-valid method.

  4. When should you prefer a multi-armed bandit over an A/B test?
    Answer

    When the goal is cumulative performance during the test (minimizing regret) rather than an unbiased, publishable effect estimate — e.g., optimizing creative where the winner is simply kept.

  5. Why does a switchback design need washout periods?
    Answer

    To let carryover effects from the previous treatment block decay before measurement begins; without washouts, residual effects contaminate the adjacent block's estimate.
