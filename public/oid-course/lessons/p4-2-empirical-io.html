4.2 Empirical Industrial Organization

Built from Wharton ECON 8450: Empirical Methods for Industrial Organization

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

App stores, cloud platforms, ad exchanges, EV-charging networks: technology markets are differentiated-product oligopolies with network effects, switching costs, and algorithmic pricing. When a regulator asks whether a merger of two cloud providers will raise prices, or a product team asks how much share a new GPU instance type will steal, reduced-form regressions cannot answer — you need a model of demand and supply that can simulate counterfactuals. Empirical IO is that machinery: estimate demand from observed choices and prices, recover marginal costs from firms' pricing first-order conditions, then recompute equilibrium under the counterfactual (a merger, a new entrant, a different auction rule). The BLP demand model you learn here is the workhorse behind modern antitrust analysis and, increasingly, behind pricing and assortment decisions inside platform companies themselves.

Learning objectives

  • Write down a discrete-choice demand model (logit, nested logit, random-coefficients) and derive the Berry (1994) inversion that recovers mean utilities from market shares.
  • Explain why price is endogenous in demand estimation and choose valid instruments: cost shifters, Hausman instruments, and BLP-style rival-characteristic instruments.
  • Describe the BLP (1995) estimation loop — contraction mapping on mean utilities plus GMM on moment conditions — and state what can go wrong (weak instruments, unstable inner loop).
  • Measure market power from estimated demand (Nevo's approach) and simulate a merger by recomputing the Nash pricing equilibrium.
  • Recover valuations in first-price auctions from observed bids (Guerre–Perrigne–Vuong) and describe entry-threshold logic (Bresnahan–Reiss).

Core content

Discrete choice: from logit to BLP

Start with the random-utility model. Consumer i in market t gets utility u_ijt = δ_jt + μ_ijt + ε_ijt from product j, where δ_jt = x_jt β − α p_jt + ξ_jt is the mean utility (observed characteristics, price, and the unobserved quality ξ_jt — brand prestige, UI polish, anything the data miss), μ_ijt captures individual taste heterogeneity, and ε_ijt is an idiosyncratic logit shock. In the plain logit (μ = 0), market shares are s_jt = exp(δ_jt) / (1 + Σ_k exp(δ_kt)), with an outside good normalized to δ_0 = 0. Berry (1994) showed the system inverts: δ_jt = ln(s_jt) − ln(s_0t). This is powerful — mean utilities, including the unobserved ξ, are recovered from observed shares alone. But the plain logit imposes the IIA property: substitution is proportional to share, so a new budget airline steals share from luxury and budget carriers equally. That is rarely credible. Nested logit relaxes IIA within pre-specified nests (e.g., iOS vs. Android apps); the random-coefficients (BLP, 1995) model lets coefficients vary across consumers, μ_ijt = Σ_k σ_k ν_ik x_jtk, so substitution patterns are estimated from the data — business laptops substitute to business laptops, gaming laptops to gaming ones — at the cost of simulation-based estimation.

The endogeneity problem and instruments

Price correlates with the unobserved quality ξ_jt: better products cost more to make and command higher prices. OLS of the inversion equation ln(s_jt) − ln(s_0t) = x_jt β − α p_jt + ξ_jt therefore understates price sensitivity (α biased toward zero). Three instrument families are standard. Cost shifters: input prices or taxes that move price without shifting demand unobservables — e.g., regional electricity prices shifting data-center costs for cloud instances. Hausman instruments: prices of the same product in other markets, valid if cost shocks are correlated across markets but demand shocks are not. BLP instruments: sums of rival products' characteristics (and of own other products' characteristics), which shift markups through the competitive environment while being excluded from own demand. None is automatic — each needs a first-stage diagnostic and an economic argument for exclusion. With instruments in hand, estimation is GMM on the moment condition E[Z′ ξ(θ)] = 0, where the structural error ξ is computed from the inversion at each parameter guess.

The BLP estimation loop

BLP estimation nests two loops. The inner loop solves for the mean utilities δ consistent with observed shares at a candidate parameter vector θ₂ (the nonlinear parameters σ, the taste-dispersion terms): iterate the contraction mapping δ^{h+1} = δ^h + ln(s^{obs}) − ln(s(δ^h; θ₂)), which Berry, Levinsohn & Pakes (1995) proved converges to the unique δ matching observed shares. The outer loop runs GMM: minimize ξ(θ)′ Z W Z′ ξ(θ), where ξ(θ) = δ(θ) − Xβ + αp comes from projecting the inverted mean utilities on observables with instruments. Practical discipline: verify inner-loop convergence (tight tolerance — loose tolerance injects noise into the objective and wrecks optimization), use enough simulation draws for the market-share integrals (or better, quadrature/sparse grids), and report sensitivity to the instrument set. If demand estimates are unstable, fall back to nested logit and check the qualitative answers survive — a simpler model you trust beats a fancy one you do not.

Measuring market power and simulating mergers

Demand estimates unlock the supply side. Under Bertrand–Nash pricing, each firm's first-order condition is s(p) + Ω ⊙ (∂s/∂p)′ (p − mc) = 0, where Ω is the ownership matrix (1 if the same firm owns products j and k). Rearranged, marginal costs are recovered: mc = p − [Ω ⊙ Δ]^{−1} s(p), with Δ = −∂s/∂p. Nevo (2000, 2001) used exactly this to measure market power in ready-to-eat cereal: implied price-cost margins, cross-checked against accounting margins as a sanity check. A merger simulation then changes the ownership matrix Ω (the merging firms' products now price jointly) and solves the new system of first-order conditions for post-merger prices — predicting price increases with and without assumed efficiencies, under an explicit conduct assumption (usually Bertrand). Regulators and merging parties both run these models; the honest presentation reports ranges under alternative conduct and instrument assumptions, because the answer depends on them.

Entry, collusion, and auctions

Three more pillars of the empirical IO toolkit. Entry models (Bresnahan & Reiss 1991): the number of firms observed in a market reveals bounds on fixed costs and competitive toughness — if a second entrant appears only in markets twice as large, competition must be driving margins down fast; the entry threshold (population needed to support N firms) is identified from cross-market variation in market size and firm counts. Collusion detection (Porter 1983 on the 19th-century Joint Executive Committee railroad cartel): estimate a regime-switching model where conduct alternates between collusive and punishment phases, identified from the joint behavior of prices and quantities. Auction estimation (Guerre, Perrigne & Vuong 2000): in a first-price sealed-bid auction with independent private values, the bidder's first-order condition gives v = b + G(b)/((n−1)g(b)), where G, g are the bid distribution and density — so valuations are nonparametrically identified from observed bids, letting you simulate counterfactual reserve prices or auction formats. For a tech engineer, this is the machinery behind ad-auction design and cloud spot-market analysis.

Worked examples

Example 1: Berry inversion on app-store data (numbers)

Three paid productivity apps in a month: shares s = (0.30, 0.20, 0.10), outside good s_0 = 0.40 (users who buy nothing). Mean utilities: δ_1 = ln(0.30) − ln(0.40) = −0.288, δ_2 = ln(0.20) − ln(0.40) = −0.693, δ_3 = ln(0.10) − ln(0.40) = −1.386. Suppose app 1 raises its price 10% and its share falls to 0.27 while app 2's rises to 0.215 and app 3's to 0.105. The plain logit says diversion goes proportionally to share — app 1's lost 0.03 share splits 0.20/0.70 to app 2 etc. A random-coefficients model instead estimates σ on the "offline-mode" characteristic: apps 1 and 3 both offer offline mode, so the estimated diversion from 1 to 3 is 55% rather than the logit's 33%. Product decision: cutting app 1's price steals disproportionately from app 3, the close substitute — the logit model would have missed the targeting.

Example 2: Merger simulation for two cloud GPU providers

Two mid-size GPU-cloud firms propose to merge. Estimated random-coefficients demand over instance types gives own-price elasticities around −3.5 and margins (p − mc)/p ≈ 0.29 from the Bertrand inversion — consistent with the firms' reported ~30% gross margins (Nevo-style sanity check). Instruments: regional electricity prices (cost shifters) and sums of rivals' VRAM offerings (BLP instruments); first-stage F on price ≈ 45. Simulate the merger: update Ω so the two firms' 12 instance types price jointly, hold mc fixed (no efficiencies), solve the new FOC system. Predicted price increase: 6–9% on overlapping instance families, 1–2% elsewhere. With a claimed 5% marginal-cost efficiency, the increase falls to 2–4%. The honest report presents both, plus a Cournot-flavored alternative conduct check — because the conclusion ("challenge" vs. "clear") hinges on the efficiency claim, not just the model.

Example 3: GPV auction estimation for ad slots

You observe 5,000 bids in first-price auctions for a display-ad slot, n = 6 bidders each. Estimate the bid CDF Ĝ and density ĝ (kernel). For a bid b = $2.40 with Ĝ(2.40) = 0.55 and ĝ(2.40) = 0.42, the implied valuation is v̂ = 2.40 + 0.55/((6−1)·0.42) = 2.40 + 0.262 = $2.66 — the bidder shades 26¢. Repeating over all bids recovers the valuation distribution. Counterfactual: raising the reserve price from $1.00 to $1.50. Simulating the symmetric equilibrium with the estimated valuation distribution predicts revenue +4.2% but 8% fewer auctions clearing — the classic reserve-price tradeoff, now quantified from data rather than assumed. Python sketch of the inversion:

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import gaussian_kde
bids = np.loadtxt("bids.csv")          # observed bids, n bidders each auction
n = 6
kde = gaussian_kde(bids)
G = np.array([kde.integrate_box_1d(-np.inf, b) for b in bids])
g = kde(bids)
v_hat = bids + G / ((n - 1) * g)        # GPV: pseudo-valuations
print("mean valuation:", v_hat.mean(), " mean bid:", bids.mean())

Example 4: Entry thresholds for EV-charging networks

Bresnahan–Reiss logic applied to fast-charging stations across 200 highway corridors. Corridors with traffic below 8,000 vehicles/day support zero stations; 8,000–22,000 support exactly one; above 22,000 support two. The entry threshold for the second firm (22k) is much more than double the first (8k) — per-firm traffic needed rises with N, implying margins fall as competition intensifies (conduct is competitive, not collusive). For a network planning expansion, the estimated threshold function maps any corridor's traffic forecast to a predicted entrant count — and the gap between the monopoly and duopoly thresholds quantifies how aggressively entry erodes profits, disciplining the build-vs.-wait decision.

Key results to remember

  • Berry (1994): in logit demand, mean utilities invert from shares, δ_j = ln(s_j) − ln(s_0) — the foundation everything else builds on.
  • Plain logit imposes IIA and proportional substitution; nested logit and random-coefficients (BLP 1995) models let substitution patterns be estimated.
  • Price is endogenous (correlated with unobserved quality ξ); use cost shifters, Hausman, or BLP rival-characteristic instruments, with first-stage diagnostics and an economic exclusion argument.
  • BLP estimation = contraction mapping on δ (inner loop) + GMM on E[Z′ξ] = 0 (outer loop); verify inner-loop convergence tightly and use enough simulation draws.
  • Marginal costs are recovered from Bertrand first-order conditions, mc = p − [Ω ⊙ Δ]^{−1}s(p) — then sanity-check implied margins against accounting data (Nevo 2000, 2001).
  • Merger simulation recomputes the Nash pricing equilibrium under the merged ownership matrix; report results with and without efficiencies and under alternative conduct.
  • Guerre–Perrigne–Vuong (2000): valuations are nonparametrically identified from bids via v = b + G(b)/((n−1)g(b)) — estimate first, then simulate auction-rule counterfactuals.
  • Never simulate a counterfactual from reduced-form estimates; name the Lucas-critique risk by stating which parameters you assume invariant to the policy.

Check your understanding

  1. Why does OLS of ln(s_j) − ln(s_0) on price and characteristics give a biased (toward zero) price coefficient?
    Answer

    Price is correlated with unobserved quality ξ (better products cost more and are priced higher). This positive correlation between price and the error term biases the price coefficient upward — toward zero or even positive — understating price sensitivity.

  2. What is a BLP instrument, and what exclusion argument does it need?
    Answer

    Sums of rival (or own-other-product) characteristics. They shift a product's markup through the competitive environment. The exclusion argument: rival characteristics do not enter the consumer's utility for this product and are uncorrelated with its demand unobservable ξ.

  3. In the BLP contraction mapping δ^{h+1} = δ^h + ln(s^{obs}) − ln(s(δ^h)), what does convergence deliver?
    Answer

    The unique vector of mean utilities δ at which predicted market shares exactly equal observed shares, for the current guess of the nonlinear parameters — the δ needed to compute the structural error ξ for GMM.

  4. A merger simulation predicts +8% prices with no efficiencies and +2% with 5% marginal-cost savings. Which should you report?
    Answer

    Both, as a range, plus the conduct assumption (Bertrand) — the conclusion depends on the efficiency claim and conduct, so the honest deliverable is the sensitivity, not a single number.

  5. Two bidders in a first-price auction; you observe a bid of $100, with estimated G(100) = 0.6 and g(100) = 0.02. What is the implied valuation?
    Answer

    v̂ = 100 + 0.6/((2−1)·0.02) = 100 + 30 = $130.
