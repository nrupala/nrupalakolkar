5.4 Behavioral Research Methods That Work

Doctoral course basis: Methods Stumblers — Pragmatic Solutions to Everyday Challenges in Behavioral Research

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Every claim about human behavior in your systems — "users prefer explanation style A," "the new onboarding lifts activation," "operators trust the copilot" — rests on an experiment or a study. The replication crisis showed that most published behavioral findings do not replicate, and the reasons are mundane: underpowered studies, undisclosed analytic flexibility, stimuli that do not generalize, standard errors that ignore clustering. OIDD 9370 is the pragmatic fix-it course for exactly these stumbles. For an engineer running UX experiments, human-AI interaction studies, or survey research, these are not academic niceties; they are the difference between shipping a real effect and shipping noise dressed up as a p-value.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how undisclosed researcher flexibility inflates false positives (Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn, 2011).
  • Run an honest power analysis using realistic (deflated) effect-size assumptions.
  • Apply stimulus sampling, clustered standard errors, and bootstrapping where textbook defaults fail.
  • Use modern mediation analysis (bootstrapped indirect effects) instead of the Baron–Kenny steps.
  • Diagnose publication bias with p-curve logic and design studies that replicate.

Core content

False-positive psychology and researcher degrees of freedom

Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn (2011), "False-Positive Psychology," showed that undisclosed flexibility in data collection and analysis — choosing sample size after peeking, trying multiple dependent variables, dropping conditions, adding covariates — lets researchers present anything as significant. Their demonstration is deliberately absurd: with these freedoms they "proved" that listening to a Beatles song makes people younger. The problem is not fraud; it is that each undisclosed choice is a fork in the analysis garden, and the published result conditions on the path that reached p < .05. The false-positive rate compounds with every undisclosed decision. The fixes are procedural and cultural: decide the sample size rule in advance, report all measures collected and all conditions run, distinguish confirmatory from exploratory analysis, and preregister the design and primary analysis so the forks are visible. Data Colada — the methods-forensics blog run by Simonsohn, Nelson, and Simmons — is the field's public enforcement mechanism for exactly this discipline.

Power: design for the effect size you will actually get

Cohen (1992) gave the field its power conventions — 80% power at α = .05, with small/medium/large standardized effects of d = 0.2/0.5/0.8 — but the OIDD 9370 lesson is that published effect sizes are inflated by publication bias, so powering on the literature's estimate underpowers your study. The pragmatic rule: discount the published estimate (often by half or more), power for the smallest effect you care about, and remember that an underpowered study is not merely inconclusive — with publication bias it actively fills the literature with exaggerated, often wrong-signed estimates (Ioannidis, 2005; Open Science Collaboration, 2015). For UX and human-AI experiments, where n is often "however many users showed up this week," this means pre-committing the stopping rule and, when traffic is thin, choosing within-subjects designs or higher-signal outcomes rather than hoping a small sample detects a small effect.

Stimulus sampling: your materials are a random factor too

A classic stumble: you test whether "explanations increase trust" using three specific explanation texts, find an effect, and claim a general result about explanations. Judd, Westfall, and Kenny (2012) show this confounds the effect with your particular stimuli — the finding may not survive a different set of texts. The fix is stimulus sampling: sample stimuli from the population you want to generalize to (many explanation texts, many prompts, many UI variants) and treat stimuli as a random factor in the analysis, exactly as you treat participants as random. For AI evaluation this is load-bearing: a claim like "prompt style X beats style Y" tested on 5 hand-picked prompts is a fixed-stimulus result; testing on 50 sampled prompts with stimulus random effects is a generalizable one. LLM-as-judge studies and red-teaming evaluations need the same discipline.

Clustered standard errors and bootstrapping

Two more everyday stumbles. First, non-independence: when observations are nested — multiple ratings per user, multiple users per team, repeated prompts per model — treating them as independent understates standard errors and inflates t-statistics. Cluster standard errors at the level of randomization or repeated observation (the user, the team, the prompt template); never pretend nested data are independent. Second, when distributional assumptions are shaky — skewed response times, indirect effects that are products of coefficients — bootstrap: resample with replacement, recompute the statistic thousands of times, and read the confidence interval off the empirical distribution. Preacher and Hayes' resampling approach to indirect effects is the canonical application, and it generalizes to any awkward statistic your experiment produces.

Modern mediation and p-curve

The old Baron and Kenny (1986) causal-steps approach to mediation — requiring a significant total effect before testing the indirect path — is obsolete. Zhao, Lynch, and Chen's framework keeps the focus where it belongs: the indirect effect (a·b) with a bootstrapped confidence interval. Mediation can exist without a significant total effect (suppression, opposing paths), and the Sobel test's normality assumption on a product of coefficients is routinely violated — hence bootstrapping. On the literature side, p-curve (Simonsohn, Nelson & Simmons, 2014) diagnoses publication bias and p-hacking across a set of studies: a literature with real evidential value produces a right-skewed p-curve (many p-values just below .05 and many tiny ones, i.e., concentrated near zero), while a hacked literature piles up just below .05. Before you build a product on "the literature says X," check whether the literature has evidential value or just a file drawer.

Designing for replication

The through-line of OIDD 9370 is pragmatic: run the analysis code on pilot data before the main collection; publish materials, data, and code; pre-commit attrition and exclusion rules; use between-subjects designs when the hypothesis is transparent (reducing demand effects); and run a well-powered direct replication of a key effect before extending it — many apparent moderators turn out to be noise-chasing. Complement lab evidence with a field or archival test of the same mechanism; multi-method evidence answers the generalizability questions no single design can. And know the Bayesian-versus-frequentist debate for what it is: a live disagreement without methodologist consensus, where the pragmatic move is to pre-specify one framework and report robustness to the other.

Worked examples

Example 1: Honest power analysis for a UX experiment

import math
from scipy import stats

def n_per_group(d, power=0.8, alpha=0.05):
    z_a = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha/2)
    z_b = stats.norm.ppf(power)
    return math.ceil(2 * ((z_a + z_b) / d) ** 2)

published_d = 0.6          # what the literature claims
honest_d = published_d / 2 # deflate for publication bias
print("n per group (published d):", n_per_group(published_d))
print("n per group (honest d):   ", n_per_group(honest_d))

Powering on d = 0.6 needs ~45 users per group; powering on the deflated d = 0.3 needs ~176. If your weekly traffic gives you 60 users total, the honest calculation tells you — before you spend a month — that you need a stronger manipulation, a within-subjects design, or a higher-signal outcome, not hope.

Example 2: Clustered SEs for AI-output ratings

Forty engineers each rate 10 model explanations for clarity (400 ratings). A naïve regression of clarity on explanation style treats n = 400 as independent — but ratings from the same engineer share that engineer's leniency, so effective n is far smaller. Clustering standard errors at the engineer level (the repeated-observation unit) typically doubles or triples the standard error; an effect that was "p = .02" naïvely becomes p = .15 when clustered. The rule: cluster at the level of randomization or repeated observation, and if the clustered result kills your finding, the finding was never there.

Example 3: Stimulus sampling for a prompt-style claim

You claim "chain-of-thought prompting beats direct answering on math word problems," tested on 8 hand-written problems. A reviewer (correctly) notes the problems are a fixed stimulus set — perhaps you unconsciously chose problems where CoT shines. The fix: sample 60 problems from a defined bank (e.g., GSM8K), run both prompt styles on all of them, and fit a mixed model with random intercepts for problems. Now the standard error reflects problem-to-problem variation, and the claim generalizes to the bank, not just your 8 favorites.

Example 4: Reading a p-curve before trusting a literature

A vendor cites "12 studies showing explanation style A increases user trust (all p < .05)" to justify a redesign. You pull the 12 p-values: nine are between .04 and .05, three are below .01. That left-skewed pile-up just under .05 is the signature of p-hacking/selective reporting, not evidential value — a real effect would concentrate p-values near zero (right-skew). Decision: run your own preregistered, well-powered test rather than shipping on the vendor's literature.

Key results to remember

  • Undisclosed flexibility (peeking at n, multiple DVs, dropped conditions, covariates) compounds the false-positive rate — preregister the design, sample-size rule, and primary analysis.
  • Power on deflated effect sizes: published estimates are inflated by publication bias; an underpowered study produces exaggerated, often wrong-signed estimates.
  • Treat stimuli as a random factor (Judd, Westfall & Kenny, 2012): sample prompts, texts, and UI variants from the population you claim to generalize to.
  • Cluster standard errors at the level of randomization or repeated observation; nested data analyzed as independent is a false-positive machine.
  • Bootstrap indirect effects and awkward statistics instead of trusting normal approximations; modern mediation (Zhao, Lynch & Chen) tests the bootstrapped a·b path, not Baron–Kenny steps.
  • P-curve (Simonsohn, Nelson & Simmons, 2014) separates evidential value (right-skewed p-values) from selective reporting (pile-up just below .05).
  • Design for replication: pilot the analysis code, publish materials/data/code, pre-commit exclusion rules, and replicate a key effect at high power before extending it.

Check your understanding

  1. A colleague ran an experiment, checked significance at n = 50 (p = .09), added 30 more participants, and stopped at p = .04. What is wrong, and what should the preregistration have said?
    Answer

    Optional stopping on significance inflates the false-positive rate — the reported p = .04 does not mean what it claims. The preregistration should have fixed the sample-size rule in advance (e.g., n = 80 determined by power analysis, or a pre-specified sequential design with alpha spending).

  2. You test two onboarding flows with 25 users each and find no significant difference. Your manager says "no effect, move on." What is the methodological objection?
    Answer

    Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence: with n = 25 per group the study is powered only for huge effects, so a null result is uninformative about realistic effect sizes. Report the confidence interval (which will be wide) and either run a powered study or switch to a higher-signal design.

  3. Why does testing an LLM evaluation claim on 6 hand-picked prompts threaten the conclusion, and what is the fix?
    Answer

    Fixed stimuli confound the effect with the researcher's choice of materials — the result may not generalize beyond those 6 prompts. Fix with stimulus sampling: draw many prompts from a defined population and model prompts as a random factor.

  4. Twelve engineers each label 50 tickets, and you regress label quality on a UI change with n = 600. What is the correct standard-error treatment?
    Answer

    Cluster standard errors at the engineer level (the repeated-observation unit), since the 50 labels from one engineer are not independent. The naïve n = 600 analysis understates uncertainty and inflates significance.
