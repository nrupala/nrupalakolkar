4.3 Empirical Methods in Corporate Finance

Built from Wharton FNCE 9260: Empirical Methods in Corporate Finance

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Technology firms are the most interesting corporate-finance laboratories alive: massive R&D optionality, intangible-heavy balance sheets, equity-financed moonshots, and debt covenants written against cash flows that machine-learning businesses barely resemble. When a semiconductor firm asks whether issuing debt to fund a fab disciplines its investment or strangles it, or when a cloud company evaluates a data-center build-or-lease decision, the empirical methods are the same ones corporate-finance researchers use on Compustat panels: panel regressions with careful fixed effects, standard errors clustered where the treatment lives, instruments defended with an economic story, and duration models for time-to-default or time-to-investment. FNCE 9260 is the PhD course that teaches this applied toolkit — emphasis on applications, not theory. This lesson distills its hard-won discipline: the mistakes that sink empirical work are almost never about fancy estimators; they are about standard errors, fixed effects, and weak instruments.

Learning objectives

  • Choose the fixed-effects structure of a panel regression deliberately and state which variation (within vs. between) identifies the coefficient.
  • Cluster standard errors at the level where treatment is assigned, following Petersen (2009), and show robustness to alternative clustering choices.
  • Diagnose weak instruments in a corporate setting and defend the exclusion restriction with an economic story.
  • Check covariate balance before and after matching and state the selection-on-observables assumption honestly.
  • Set up a duration/hazard model for time-to-event outcomes (default, investment, adoption) and test the proportional-hazards assumption.

Core content

Panel data: what variation identifies your coefficient?

The workhorse is y_it = x_it′β + α_i + λ_t + ε_it. Firm fixed effects α_i absorb all time-invariant firm traits — culture, founding team, location — so β is identified from within-firm variation over time; time effects λ_t absorb common shocks. The discipline FNCE 9260 drills: state which variation identifies your coefficient and whether that variation is the policy-relevant one. Firm FE is not a panacea — it does nothing about time-varying omitted variables (a productivity shock that raises both leverage and investment), and it discards informative between-firm variation; Lemmon, Roberts & Zender (2008) showed capital structure is dominated by a persistent firm fixed effect, meaning most of the action is cross-sectional and within-firm regressions may be answering a different question than you think. Random effects buys efficiency under the strong assumption that α_i is uncorrelated with x_it — test it with a Hausman test, but remember the test's null is often economically implausible in corporate data. Dynamic panels (lagged y as a regressor) need Arellano–Bond-style GMM, not OLS with FE, because the lagged dependent variable correlates with the demeaned error by construction.

Clustered standard errors: Petersen (2009)

The single highest-return fix in applied corporate finance. Petersen (2009) showed that with firm-level panel data, residuals are correlated both within firms over time and within years across firms; failing to cluster understates standard errors and wildly over-rejects. The rule: cluster at the level where treatment is assigned — if the policy varies by state-year, cluster by state; if by firm, cluster by firm. Two-way clustering (firm and year) handles both dimensions when both matter. Always show how results move under alternative clustering choices — a result that survives firm clustering but dies under state clustering is telling you something about where the identifying variation lives. With few clusters (say, under 30), clustered standard errors are biased downward; use the wild cluster bootstrap. Note the connection to Bertrand, Duflo & Mullainathan (2004) from Lesson 4.1: same disease (correlated residuals), same cure (cluster where treatment varies).

Fixed-effects discipline and the "kitchen sink"

Three related pathologies. First, absorbing the treatment: with firm and year fixed effects plus a treatment that turns on once per firm, the coefficient is a DiD estimate — fine — but adding industry-by-year effects can absorb the very variation you need, leaving identification off thin residual slivers. Second, bad controls: conditioning on variables that are themselves outcomes of the treatment (e.g., controlling for current profitability when studying the effect of leverage on investment) biases the estimate by blocking causal paths or opening collider paths. Third, over-controlling away the mechanism: every control should have a stated reason — confounder, precision, or fixed effect — not "it was available in Compustat." Roberts & Sufi (2009) on control rights and capital structure is the course's exemplar of careful design: the treatment (covenant violation shifting control to creditors) is plausibly exogenous conditional on observables near the threshold, and the paper argues the economics rather than hiding behind fixed effects.

IV in corporate finance: stories, then statistics

Corporate treatments — leverage, governance, cash holdings — are chosen by managers with private information, so endogeneity is the norm. Every instrument needs an economic story for exclusion before any statistic: why does this shifter affect the outcome only through the endogenous regressor? Angrist & Krueger (2001) is the template for the defense. Then report weak-instrument diagnostics for each first stage (F statistics; the ~10 rule of thumb), because corporate instruments (industry averages, historical values, regulatory quirks) are often weak — and with weak instruments, 2SLS is biased toward OLS and the "significant" result is an artifact. Chava & Roberts (2008) on debt covenants and investment illustrates the payoff when design is credible: covenant violations create sharp, plausibly exogenous shifts in creditor control, letting the authors measure effects on investment cleanly.

Duration models for time-to-event outcomes

Default, bankruptcy, time-to-IPO, adoption of a technology, exercise of a real option — corporate finance is full of when questions with censoring (you observe firms that have not yet defaulted). OLS on observed durations is biased; the hazard framework (Kiefer 1988) models the instantaneous event rate h(t|x) = h_0(t)·exp(x′β). The Cox proportional-hazards model leaves the baseline h_0(t) nonparametric and estimates β by partial likelihood; the key assumption is proportionality — covariate effects multiply the baseline hazard by a constant over time. Test it (Schoenfeld residuals; interactions with time), because in tech it often fails: the effect of burn rate on default risk is not constant over a startup's life. Parametric alternatives (Weibull, log-normal) buy efficiency and let the baseline shape be the object of interest (does the IPO hazard rise then fall with firm age?), at the cost of distributional assumptions. Handle censoring explicitly and distinguish it from truncation; competing risks (acquisition vs. IPO vs. failure) need a competing-risks or multinomial treatment, not independent Cox models.

Matching, balance, and honest causal claims

Matching on observables (Rosenbaum & Rubin 1983) is common in corporate finance — match each treated firm (e.g., each firm that restated earnings) to controls on size, industry, and pre-trends. The checklist: check covariate balance before and after matching (standardized differences, not just t-tests); report how much of the treated sample survives common support; and never present the matched comparison as causal without a selection-on-observables argument — why would the remaining unobservables be balanced? Meyer (1995) on natural and quasi-natural experiments is the course's reminder that the best corporate-finance papers find genuine quasi-experimental variation (regulatory changes, discontinuities in rules) rather than squeezing causality from matching alone. Lee & Lemieux (2010) and Imbens & Lemieux (2008) on RDD, and Hahn, Todd & Van der Klaauw (2001) on identification, round out the program-evaluation core the course shares with Lesson 4.1 — here applied to firms rather than people.

Worked examples

Example 1: Clustering changes the answer (Petersen 2009 in action)

A researcher regresses investment on leverage for 4,000 tech and manufacturing firms, 2000–2020, with firm and year fixed effects. OLS standard errors: coefficient −0.18, t = −6.2, "highly significant." Cluster by firm: t = −2.4, still significant at 5%. Two-way cluster by firm and year: t = −1.6, not significant. What happened? Leverage is persistent within firms (serial correlation) and co-moves across firms within years (common credit cycles) — both correlations inflate t-statistics when ignored. The honest conclusion: the within-firm association between leverage and investment is fragile, and the interesting variation may be the credit-cycle component absorbed by year effects. The paper is rewritten around the cycle, not the firm. Python sketch:

import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
# df: firm-year panel, firm and year dummies via C()
m = smf.ols("investment ~ leverage + C(firm) + C(year)", data=df).fit()
print(m.summary())                                            # naive SEs
print(m.get_robustcov_results(cov_type="cluster",
      groups=df["firm"]).summary())                           # one-way
# two-way clustering: use linearmodels PanelOLS
from linearmodels import PanelOLS
pdf = df.set_index(["firm", "year"])
r = PanelOLS(pdf.investment, pdf[["leverage"]],
             entity_effects=True, time_effects=True).fit(
             cov_type="clustered", cluster_entity=True, cluster_time=True)
print(r.summary)

Example 2: Do R&D tax credits raise R&D? A DiD with staggered state adoption

Twelve states introduce or expand R&D tax credits between 2005 and 2018; firms are assigned to their headquarters state. Outcome: R&D / sales. A naive TWFE regression gives +0.8 pp. But adoption is staggered, so the analyst uses a Callaway–Sant'Anna estimator with not-yet-treated states as controls, clusters standard errors by state (treatment is assigned at the state level — clustering by firm would be wrong), and plots the event study: pre-treatment coefficients of −0.1, +0.05, −0.08 (flat), post-treatment rising to +1.2 pp by year 3. Wild cluster bootstrap (only 38 states have enough firms) confirms significance. Mechanism check: patent filings rise with a 2-year lag; debt issuance does not move — consistent with credits funding research, not financial engineering. The TWFE number understated the long-run effect because early-treated states served as controls for later ones.

Example 3: Hazard model for startup default

A venture lender models time-to-default for 2,500 startups, 2015–2025; 40% default (observed), 60% are censored (still alive or exited). Covariates: burn multiple, months of runway, sector. A Cox model gives a hazard ratio of 1.35 per unit of burn multiple — but the Schoenfeld test rejects proportional hazards (p = 0.01): burn hurts more in year 1 than year 4. Fix: interact burn multiple with log(time), or stratify by funding stage. The stratified model shows burn multiple's hazard ratio is 1.8 in seed stage but 1.1 post-Series B — the risk content of burn decays as firms mature. Business use: the lender prices loan covenants by stage, not with a single pooled coefficient. A Weibull parametric alternative additionally estimates the baseline: default hazard peaks at 18 months post-funding then declines — the "valley of death" quantified.

Example 4: Covenant violations and investment (Chava & Roberts style)

When a firm violates a debt covenant, control rights shift to creditors, who typically cut investment. But violators differ from non-violators (worse performance). The design: a fuzzy RDD around covenant thresholds (e.g., current ratio below 1.2 triggers violation). Just above vs. just below the threshold, firms are comparable; violation probability jumps discontinuously. Suppose investment/sales falls 1.5 pp at the threshold while violation probability jumps 0.6: the LATE is −1.5/0.6 = −2.5 pp for compliers — firms pushed into violation by the threshold. Standard errors clustered by firm (treatment varies at firm-quarter level). The mechanism (creditor control, not just distress) is argued by showing the effect is larger when the lead lender is a bank with a history of intervention — the economic story doing the work that fixed effects cannot.

Key results to remember

  • State which variation identifies your panel coefficient — within-firm (firm FE) vs. between-firm — and check it is the variation your question needs (cf. Lemmon, Roberts & Zender 2008 on the persistence of capital structure).
  • Cluster standard errors where treatment is assigned (Petersen 2009); use two-way clustering when residuals correlate along both dimensions; show robustness to alternative clustering choices.
  • Firm fixed effects do not fix time-varying omitted variables; avoid bad controls (outcomes of treatment) and do not absorb the treatment with overly rich fixed effects.
  • Every IV needs an economic story for exclusion first (Angrist & Krueger 2001), then first-stage F diagnostics; weak instruments make 2SLS collapse toward OLS.
  • Matching is only as credible as selection on observables: check balance before and after, report common support, and run sensitivity analyses — never present it as causal by itself.
  • For time-to-event outcomes use hazard models (Kiefer 1988); test proportional hazards (Schoenfeld residuals) and handle censoring and competing risks explicitly.
  • The best corporate-finance designs find quasi-experimental variation (Meyer 1995) — covenant thresholds, regulatory changes, rule discontinuities — rather than relying on controls alone.

Check your understanding

  1. A state-level policy is evaluated with firm-level data and standard errors clustered by firm. What is wrong?
    Answer

    Treatment is assigned at the state level, so residuals correlate within states; clustering by firm understates standard errors. Cluster by state (and consider the wild bootstrap if few states).

  2. With firm fixed effects, your leverage coefficient is identified from what variation? Give one threat to its causal interpretation.
    Answer

    Within-firm variation over time (changes in a firm's leverage associated with changes in its outcome). Threat: time-varying omitted variables — e.g., a productivity shock that moves both leverage and investment.

  3. You control for current profitability in a regression of investment on leverage. Why might this be a "bad control"?
    Answer

    Profitability is itself affected by leverage (and by investment opportunities); conditioning on it can block part of the causal path or induce collider bias, distorting the leverage coefficient.

  4. In a Cox model of time-to-default, the Schoenfeld test rejects proportional hazards for your key covariate. Name two fixes.
    Answer

    Interact the covariate with (a function of) time, or stratify the baseline hazard by levels of the covariate (e.g., funding stage).

  5. Your matched sample of treated and control firms shows perfect balance on observables. Can you now claim causality? Why or why not?
    Answer

    No. Matching only balances observables; selection on unobservables (managerial quality, private information) can still bias the comparison. Causality requires the untestable conditional-independence assumption plus an argument for it.
