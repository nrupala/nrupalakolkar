2.6 Causal Inference and Observational Studies

Doctoral course basis: Observational Studies

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

You cannot randomize everything. You cannot A/B test a datacenter architecture, randomize which customers get a price increase, or rerun last year's outage as a control. Observational causal inference is the discipline of extracting credible causal answers from data you didn't generate by experiment — the situation you are in most of the time. This lesson gives you the potential-outcomes language in which every causal claim must be stated, the design-based toolkit (matching, weighting, discontinuity, difference-in-differences) for making those claims credible, and Rosenbaum's sensitivity analysis for the honest question every observational study must answer: how much hidden bias would it take to overturn my conclusion?

Learning objectives

  • Formalize a causal question with potential outcomes, SUTVA, and the ignorability assumption.
  • Estimate treatment effects with propensity-score matching and weighting, diagnose balance and overlap, and combine models doubly robustly.
  • Quantify robustness to hidden bias with Rosenbaum sensitivity bounds and design sensitivity.
  • Interpret IV estimates as LATE, and implement RDD and difference-in-differences designs with their falsification checks.
  • Use causal DAGs to distinguish confounders, colliders, and mediators and to choose adjustment sets.

Core content

The potential-outcomes framework

For each unit i, define potential outcomes Yi(1) (treated) and Yi(0) (untreated); the causal effect is τi = Yi(1) − Yi(0). The fundamental problem: you observe only Yi = DiYi(1) + (1−Di)Yi(0), where Di is treatment — the counterfactual is missing by definition. Two assumptions make progress possible. SUTVA (stable unit treatment value assumption): no interference between units and no hidden versions of treatment — unit i's outcome depends only on i's treatment. In networked systems SUTVA is routinely violated (treating one server shifts load to others; treating one user affects their contacts), and you must either design around interference (cluster randomization) or model it explicitly. Ignorability (unconfoundedness): {Yi(1), Yi(0)} ⊥ Di | Xi — conditional on covariates X, treatment is as good as random. Under ignorability, E[Y(1) − Y(0)] = E[E[Y|D=1,X] − E[Y|D=0,X]]: the causal effect is identified by covariate adjustment. Everything in observational causal inference is either a strategy for making ignorability plausible (design) or a way to probe what happens when it fails (sensitivity analysis).

Propensity scores, matching, weighting

The propensity score e(Xi) = P(Di=1|Xi) is a balancing score (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983): conditional on e(X), treatment is independent of X — D ⊥ X | e(X). This collapses high-dimensional covariate adjustment into a one-dimensional score. Two uses: matching pairs each treated unit with control units at similar propensity scores (optimal matching or fine-balance methods beat greedy nearest-neighbor), then compares outcomes within pairs — mimicking a randomized block experiment; weighting (IPW) reweights to a pseudo-population where treatment is unconfounded, τ̂ = (1/n)Σ[DiYi/êi − (1−Di)Yi/(1−êi)]. Two mandatory diagnostics: overlap — propensity scores must stay away from 0 and 1 (no overlap, no comparison; trim or restrict the estimand); balance — after matching/weighting, standardized mean differences on all covariates should be small (below 0.1 is the conventional target). If balance fails, redesign the match or respecify the score — never proceed to the outcome analysis on unbalanced data. Doubly robust estimators combine an outcome model μ̂d(X) with the propensity model: τ̂DR = (1/n)Σ{[Di(Yi−μ̂1)/êi + μ̂1] − [(1−Di)(Yi−μ̂0)/(1−êi) + μ̂0]}. Consistency requires only one of the two models to be correct — two chances instead of one — and it is the default estimator you should reach for.

Sensitivity analysis: Rosenbaum bounds

Ignorability is untestable — no data can prove there is no unmeasured confounder. Rosenbaum's sensitivity analysis asks the honest quantitative question: how strong would hidden bias have to be to overturn the conclusion? Model hidden bias by Γ ≥ 1: two units matched on observables may differ in their odds of treatment by at most a factor of Γ due to an unobserved covariate. Γ = 1 is a randomized experiment; Γ = 2 means hidden bias could double one unit's treatment odds relative to its match. For each Γ, compute the worst-case p-value over all allowed hidden-bias configurations; report the threshold Γ* at which the conclusion breaks (significance lost). A study that breaks at Γ = 1.2 is fragile — a tiny unmeasured confounder kills it; one surviving to Γ = 3 is robust. Design sensitivity goes further: for a given data-generating process, it's the Γ-value at which the study's power tends to 1 — a property of the design, favoring designs (strong instruments, sharp discontinuities, well-matched pairs) whose conclusions are inherently hard to explain away. Evidence factors split one study into multiple (nearly) independent tests of the same hypothesis affected by different biases — corroboration across factors is far more persuasive than a single analysis.

IV as LATE, RDD, and difference-in-differences

With noncompliance, IV estimates a local average treatment effect (LATE) (Imbens & Angrist): under relevance, exclusion, independence, and monotonicity (the instrument pushes everyone weakly in the same direction — no defiers), β̂IV = E[Y(1)−Y(0) | complier] — the effect on compliers, those whose treatment status follows the instrument. Always state whose effect you're estimating: compliers in a server rollout are the servers that adopt when assigned — often the well-behaved ones, not the fleet average.

Regression discontinuity (RDD): treatment assigns by a cutoff, Di = 1{Xi ≥ c} (a rate limit kicks in above a traffic threshold; a feature unlocks above a usage tier). If units can't precisely manipulate X around c, assignment near the cutoff is as good as random — compare outcomes just above vs. just below with local linear regression on each side. Mandatory checks: the running variable's density must be continuous at c (McCrary test — a spike means sorting/manipulation), and predetermined covariates must be smooth through the cutoff. Probe robustness with donut specifications (drop observations nearest the cutoff) and alternative bandwidths.

Difference-in-differences (DiD): compare the change over time in treated units to the change in never-treated controls: τ̂ = (Ȳtreat,post − Ȳtreat,pre) − (Ȳctrl,post − Ȳctrl,pre). Identification rests on parallel trends: treated and control would have evolved identically absent treatment. Diagnose with event-study plots — coefficients on leads of treatment should be zero pre-treatment — but remember that failing to reject a low-power pre-trend test is not evidence of parallel trends. With staggered adoption (units treated at different times), the classic two-way fixed-effects estimator is a weighted average of 2×2 DiDs with some negative weights — potentially badly biased; use modern estimators (Callaway–Sant'Anna, Sun–Abraham, Borusyak et al.) that aggregate clean cohort-specific comparisons. Run placebos: fake treatment dates, fake treated units, outcomes the treatment shouldn't move.

Causal DAGs

Directed acyclic graphs encode causal assumptions visually: arrows are direct causal effects, and d-separation reads off conditional independencies. Three structures to internalize: confounder (X → D, X → Y: adjust for X or be biased); collider (D → C ← Y: do not adjust for C — conditioning on a collider induces spurious association, the classic "selected sample" trap); mediator (D → M → Y: adjusting for M blocks the very effect you want). The backdoor criterion gives the valid adjustment set: block all backdoor paths from D to Y without conditioning on colliders or mediators. For an engineer drowning in telemetry, the DAG is the tool that tells you which of your 200 covariates to control for — and, just as important, which to leave alone. Conditioning on a downstream metric (e.g., controlling for "requests served" when estimating the effect of a load balancer on latency) is the most common DAG error in systems work: you've conditioned on a mediator/collider and biased the estimate.

Worked examples

Example 1: Propensity-score matching for a feature rollout (with balance discipline)

Product rolls out an "early access" feature to 5,000 users who opted in; 50,000 didn't. Opt-in correlates with engagement, so naive comparison is confounded. You estimate propensity scores on pre-rollout covariates (sessions, tenure, device, region), 1:1 optimal-match, then check balance: before matching, standardized differences up to 0.9; after matching, all below 0.06. Overlap is good (scores span 0.05–0.6 for both groups). The matched difference in 30-day retention is +2.1 points (SE 0.4). You also run doubly robust as a check: +2.0 — agreement is reassuring. Then the sensitivity analysis: the effect stays significant until Γ = 2.3 — an unmeasured confounder would need to more than double treatment odds within matched pairs to explain it away. Takeaway: balance-then-estimate-then-sensitize is the complete observational workflow.

Example 2: RDD at a rate-limit threshold

An API rate limit throttles clients above 1,000 requests/min (running variable X = requests/min, cutoff c = 1,000). You compare error rates just above vs. just below: local linear regression with bandwidth 150 req/min gives a jump of +4.2 points (SE 0.8) — the causal effect of throttling at the threshold. Checks: the density of X is smooth through 1,000 (no manipulation — clients can't precisely control their rate), predetermined covariates (client size, plan tier) show no jumps. A donut dropping |X − 1000| < 20 barely moves the estimate. Takeaway: thresholds in your systems are ready-made experiments — but only if the running variable can't be gamed at the cutoff.

Example 3: Staggered DiD for a datacenter migration

Twelve datacenters migrate to a new scheduler in staggered waves over 18 months. Naive two-way fixed effects gives −6% energy use. But treatment timing correlates with datacenter age (older first), and TWFE with staggered timing can weight early-vs-late comparisons negatively. You switch to Callaway–Sant'Anna: group-time ATTs aggregated to an overall −4.2% (SE 1.1). The event-study plot shows flat pre-trends (leads ≈ 0, with honest confidence bands) and a gradual post-treatment decline — consistent with a learning curve. A placebo using fake migration dates gives ≈ 0. Takeaway: with staggered adoption, modern DiD estimators aren't optional refinement — TWFE can get the sign wrong.

Example 4: A DAG that saves you from a collider

You want the effect of a new congestion-control algorithm (D) on flow completion time (Y). Available covariates include retransmission count (R). The DAG: D → R ← (unobserved path quality) → Y — R is a collider between D and unobserved path quality. Controlling for R (tempting — "hold retransmissions fixed") opens the collider path and biases the estimate: within fixed-R strata, D becomes correlated with unobserved path quality. The correct adjustment set excludes R. You estimate without R: −18 ms; with R: −6 ms — the collider-controlled estimate is badly attenuated. Takeaway: draw the DAG before choosing covariates; more controls can mean more bias.

Key results to remember

  • Potential outcomes define the estimand; SUTVA (no interference) and ignorability (unconfoundedness given X) are the two assumptions every observational design stands on — state them explicitly.
  • The propensity score balances covariates in one dimension; matching mimics blocked experiments, IPW reweights to an unconfounded pseudo-population. Require overlap and post-adjustment balance (standardized differences < 0.1).
  • Doubly robust estimators are consistent if either the outcome model or the propensity model is right — use them as the default.
  • Rosenbaum sensitivity bounds report the Γ at which hidden bias overturns the conclusion; design sensitivity and evidence factors judge the design's inherent robustness.
  • IV with noncompliance estimates LATE — the effect on compliers — under monotonicity; always say whose effect you've estimated.
  • RDD needs a non-manipulable running variable (check density continuity) and estimates a local effect at the cutoff; DiD needs parallel trends (event-study plots, honest about power) and modern estimators under staggered adoption.
  • DAGs: adjust for confounders, never for colliders, and don't block mediators when you want the total effect. Conditioning on downstream metrics is the classic systems error.

Check your understanding

  1. After matching, the standardized mean difference on "prior sessions" is 0.35. Can you proceed to estimate the treatment effect?
    Answer

    No — balance has failed (target < 0.1). Redesign the match (different algorithm, calipers, more covariates in the score) until balance holds; estimating on unbalanced data reintroduces the confounding you matched to remove.

  2. Your sensitivity analysis breaks at Γ = 1.15. How do you report this?
    Answer

    Honestly: the result is fragile — an unmeasured confounder that shifts treatment odds by just 15% within matched pairs could explain the finding away. Treat the result as suggestive, strengthen the design, or look for corroborating evidence factors.

  3. In a staggered rollout, why is classic two-way fixed effects DiD potentially biased?
    Answer

    TWFE is a variance-weighted average of all 2×2 DiDs, including comparisons of newly treated units against already-treated units — whose trends are already affected by treatment. Some weights can be negative, so the estimand need not even be a convex average of treatment effects. Use cohort-based estimators instead.

  4. You control for "server CPU temperature" when estimating the effect of a cooling-policy change on failure rates. What DAG structure makes this wrong?
    Answer

    Temperature is a mediator (policy → temperature → failures): controlling for it blocks the causal path and biases the total effect toward zero. It's also potentially a collider if unobserved workload affects both temperature and failures. Either way, don't condition on it for the total effect.
