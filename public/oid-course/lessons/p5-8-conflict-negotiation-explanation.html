5.8 Conflict, Negotiation, and Explanation

Doctoral course basis: Conflict Management Seminar / Explaining Explanation

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Two skills close out Part 5, and both are daily engineering realities. First, negotiation: you negotiate GPU contracts, vendor SLAs, scope with stakeholders, and priorities with other teams — and the analytic toolkit (BATNA, ZOPA, integrative bargaining) turns haggling into solvable structure. Second, explanation: "explainable AI" is now a product requirement and a regulatory one, yet as OIDD 9530 argues, researchers and practitioners routinely conflate three different meanings of "explanation" — causal mechanism, predictive power, and subjective sense-making. Knowing which one your stakeholder actually needs determines whether you ship SHAP plots, a causal analysis, or a plain-language narrative — and shipping the wrong kind is how explainability theater happens.

Learning objectives

  • Define BATNA, reservation price, and ZOPA and compute the bargaining zone for a structured negotiation.
  • Distinguish distributive from integrative bargaining and use logrolling to expand the pie.
  • Identify cognitive barriers to agreement, especially the mythical fixed pie and the negotiator's dilemma.
  • Distinguish the three meanings of explanation (mechanism, prediction, sense-making) and match each to the right XAI technique.
  • Evaluate explanation claims with the explain-or-predict discipline (Shmueli; Yarkoni & Westfall).

Core content

Negotiation analytics: BATNA, reservation price, ZOPA

Every negotiation is structured by three quantities. Your BATNA — best alternative to a negotiated agreement — is what happens if you walk away; it is the source of all bargaining power. Your reservation price is the worst deal you will accept, set by your BATNA (adjusted for transaction costs and risk). The ZOPA — zone of possible agreement — is the overlap between the parties' reservation prices: if your maximum and their minimum overlap, a deal exists; if not, no agreement is possible without changing the alternatives or the issues. Preparation therefore means: (1) quantify your BATNA in dollars, (2) estimate theirs, (3) compute the ZOPA, and (4) plan moves that improve your BATNA or worsen theirs before bargaining begins. Most negotiators skip straight to tactics; the analytics say the deal is largely determined before anyone sits down.

Distributive versus integrative bargaining

Walton and McKersie (1965) distinguished distributive bargaining — fixed-pie, value-claiming, one issue (usually price) — from integrative bargaining — variable-pie, value-creating, multiple issues with different priorities. The integrative toolkit: logrolling (trade across issues — concede on what you value less in exchange for what you value more), unbundling issues to reveal differences in valuation, and contingent contracts (bet on beliefs about the future) when parties disagree on likelihoods. Fisher and Ury's principled negotiation (Getting to Yes) adds process discipline: separate the people from the problem, focus on interests rather than positions, invent options for mutual gain, and insist on objective criteria.

Two barriers recur. The mythical fixed pie (Bazerman, Magliozzi & Neale, 1985): negotiators assume their interests are directly opposed even when compatible tradeoffs exist, leaving integrative value unclaimed — in experiments, most dyads fail to discover win–win trades sitting in plain sight. The negotiator's dilemma (Lax & Sebenius): creating value requires openness about interests, but claiming value rewards concealing them — so rational negotiators under-share and shrink the pie they then fight over. The prescription is staged: create value before claiming it, use objective criteria and contingent contracts to make openness safe, and watch for deception — Schweitzer and Croson (1999) show that direct questions curtail lying by forcing a choice between truth and detectable omission.

What "explanation" means: three distinct senses

OIDD 9530 (Duncan Watts) argues that social science — and now AI — uses "explanation" to mean three logically distinct things. (a) Causal mechanism: identifying what produces the outcome (the gears). (b) Prediction: accounting for variance — a model "explains" 80% of the variance whether or not it names a mechanism. (c) Sense-making: producing subjective understanding, the feeling that something now makes sense. Conflating them causes real confusion: a high-R² model is taken to reveal mechanism (it may just exploit a confounded proxy); a satisfying narrative is taken to predict (stories are cheap, forecasts are hard — Watts, Everything Is Obvious). Shmueli (2010), "To explain or to predict?", shows the modeling choices actually differ: explanatory modeling prioritizes unbiased coefficients on theoretically meaningful variables; predictive modeling prioritizes out-of-sample accuracy and will happily use atheoretical proxies. Breiman's (2001) "two cultures" and Yarkoni and Westfall (2017) extend the point: psychology's explanation-first culture underuses prediction, while ML's prediction-first culture mistakes predictive success for understanding.

The science of explanation for XAI

Map the three senses onto explainable-AI practice. When the stakeholder needs sense-making — an operator asking "why did the model flag this transaction?" — deliver contrastive, selective explanations: why this outcome rather than the expected one, citing the few most decisive factors (this is the logic behind LIME, Ribeiro et al., 2016: local, interpretable approximations of a complex classifier's decision boundary). When the need is prediction-accounting — "how much does each feature drive the model's output variance?" — SHAP values and global importance measures are the honest answer, with the caveat that they describe the model, not the world. When the need is causal mechanism — "will intervening on this feature change outcomes?" — neither suffices: you need causal identification (randomization, natural experiments, structural assumptions — Part 2's toolkit), because a feature can be highly predictive and causally inert. The XAI failure mode is sense-making theater: a plausible-looking explanation that satisfies the user while answering none of the three questions rigorously. Evaluate every explanation product against the question it claims to answer — and against the replication-crisis lesson that confident causal stories routinely outrun their evidence.

Worked examples

Example 1: BATNA and ZOPA in a GPU cloud contract

You need 500 A100-equivalents for a year. Your BATNA: a competitor's quote at $2.20/GPU-hour with acceptable terms. Your reservation price: $2.20 (walk away above it). The vendor's BATNA: selling the capacity spot at an expected $1.60; their reservation: $1.60. ZOPA: [$1.60, $2.20] — any price in between beats both walk-aways. Analytics before tactics: can you improve your BATNA (a second competing quote at $2.05 narrows the ZOPA in your favor)? Can you discover their capacity utilization (high idle capacity lowers their true reservation)? The opening offer then anchors within the ZOPA — but the ZOPA itself was set by the alternatives, not the anchoring.

Example 2: Logrolling a vendor SLA

Negotiation stalls on price: you want $1.80/GPU-hour, the vendor holds at $2.00 — a distributive deadlock over $0.20. Unbundle: you care more about preemption protection (your training runs die expensively) than about the headline rate; the vendor cares more about commitment length than about $0.10 of rate. Logroll: accept $1.95 and a 2-year commit (cheap for you, valuable to them) in exchange for contractual no-preemption windows (cheap for them at their utilization, precious to you). The mythical fixed pie said price was the only issue; integrative bargaining found $0.15 of rate plus the terms each side actually valued.

Example 3: Which explanation does the stakeholder need?

Three requests for "explainability" on your credit-risk model, three different products. (a) A denied applicant asks why: sense-making — deliver a contrastive explanation ("your application was declined rather than approved primarily because of X; changing X to x′ would change the decision"), LIME-style, in plain language. (b) Your risk team asks which features drive model variance: prediction-accounting — SHAP summary plots with the explicit caveat "this describes the model." (c) The product team asks whether lowering the weight on feature X will reduce defaults: causal mechanism — neither LIME nor SHAP answers this; you need an experiment or quasi-experiment on the decision threshold. Shipping (a)'s technique for (c)'s question is explainability theater.

Example 4: Negotiation prep worksheet in Python

def zopa(my_reservation, their_reservation_estimate):
    # buyer: will pay at most my_reservation; seller: accepts at least theirs
    lo, hi = their_reservation_estimate, my_reservation
    return (lo, hi) if lo <= hi else None

my_walkaway = 2.20    # competitor quote, $/GPU-hr
their_walkaway = 1.60 # expected spot revenue
zone = zopa(my_walkaway, their_walkaway)
print("ZOPA ($/GPU-hr):", zone)   # (1.6, 2.2): deal exists
# Target: open at 1.75 (inside ZOPA, near their reservation),
# concede toward 1.95, never cross 2.20.

The worksheet forces the analytic work — quantifying walk-aways — before any tactical discussion of opening offers and concession patterns. If the function returns None, stop preparing tactics and start improving your BATNA.

Key results to remember

  • BATNA determines bargaining power; reservation prices are set by BATNAs; the ZOPA (overlap of reservation prices) determines whether any deal exists — analytics precedes tactics.
  • Distributive bargaining claims fixed-pie value; integrative bargaining creates value via logrolling, unbundling, and contingent contracts (Walton & McKersie).
  • Principled negotiation (Fisher & Ury): separate people from the problem, interests from positions, invent options, insist on objective criteria.
  • Cognitive barriers: the mythical fixed pie (assuming opposition where tradeoffs exist) and the negotiator's dilemma (openness creates value but concealing claims it).
  • "Explanation" means three different things: causal mechanism, predictive variance-accounted-for, and subjective sense-making — conflating them is the root XAI confusion (Watts; OIDD 9530).
  • Explanatory vs. predictive modeling optimize different things (Shmueli; Breiman's two cultures): unbiased mechanism coefficients versus out-of-sample accuracy.
  • Match the XAI technique to the question: contrastive local explanations (LIME-style) for sense-making, SHAP-style attribution for prediction-accounting, causal identification for mechanism — and call out explainability theater when the technique cannot answer the question asked.

Check your understanding

  1. Your BATNA is $100K (alternative vendor), the supplier's reservation is $120K. What should you do, and why?
    Answer

    There is no ZOPA — your maximum ($100K) is below their minimum ($120K), so no deal beats both walk-aways. Do not bargain harder; improve your BATNA (better alternative quote) or change the issues (unbundle terms, add contingent clauses) to create overlap.

  2. Two teams deadlock over API rate limits: Team A wants a higher cap, Team B wants lower latency. How could logrolling resolve this?
    Answer

    The deadlock assumes a fixed pie on one issue. Unbundle: A values throughput most, B values p99 latency most. Trade — A accepts off-peak scheduling (cheap for A) for a higher cap, B accepts the higher cap in exchange for latency SLO guarantees during peak. Each concedes on the dimension it values less.

  3. A SHAP plot shows "account age" strongly drives your fraud model's scores. The product team proposes intervening on account age to reduce fraud. What is wrong?
    Answer

    SHAP answers prediction-accounting (what drives model variance), not causal mechanism. Account age may be a proxy for unobserved risk factors; intervening on it need not change outcomes. The causal question requires an experiment or quasi-experimental design, not feature attribution.

  4. A user asks "why was my loan denied?" and you show them a global SHAP summary plot. Which explanation sense did they need, and what should you have shown?
    Answer

    They needed sense-making: a contrastive, local explanation of their decision ("denied rather than approved because of X; changing X would change the outcome"). A global importance plot answers a different question and leaves their actual question unanswered.
