5.5 Research Design

Doctoral course basis: Seminar in Research Design

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Lesson 5.4 fixed statistical stumbles; this lesson steps back to the design itself. Whether you are evaluating a human-AI teaming intervention, surveying engineers about tool adoption, or mining logs to claim a feature "caused" engagement, the credibility of the conclusion is decided before data collection — by the match between your question and your design. Shadish, Cook, and Campbell's validity framework is the shared language reviewers use to attack or defend behavioral and organizational research. Learning it lets you anticipate the objections to your own studies and choose designs (experiment, quasi-experiment, survey, archival, qualitative) whose strengths line up with the claim you actually want to make.

Learning objectives

  • Define the four validities — internal, external, construct, statistical-conclusion — and name key threats to each.
  • Choose among experimental, quasi-experimental, survey, archival, and qualitative designs based on the claim and constraints.
  • Apply the methodological-fit principle to match research questions with appropriate methods.
  • Distinguish a theoretical contribution from a mere empirical finding (Whetten; Sutton & Staw).

Core content

The four validities

Shadish, Cook, and Campbell (2002) organize every threat to a causal or descriptive claim into four validities. Internal validity: does the observed covariation reflect a causal relationship, free of confounds? Threats include history (an external event coincides with the treatment — a product launch during your experiment), maturation (participants change over time regardless), selection (groups differed before treatment), regression to the mean (extreme scorers drift inward on retest), attrition (differential dropout), instrumentation (the measure itself changed), and testing (the pretest sensitizes participants). Random assignment is the master defense because it equates groups on all confounds, observed and unobserved, in expectation.

External validity: to what populations, settings, times, and operationalizations does the effect generalize? A lab finding with undergraduates, or a field test at one tech company, may not travel. Construct validity: do your manipulations and measures actually capture the theoretical constructs — is your "trust" manipulation really trust, or just familiarity? Statistical-conclusion validity: are the statistical inferences sound — adequate power, correct error terms, assumptions met (this is lesson 5.4's territory). The four trade off against each other: tightening internal validity with a controlled lab study typically costs external validity, and vice versa. Good design is explicit about which validity is being prioritized and why the claim at stake licenses that choice.

Design choices and their logics

Five design families cover most behavioral and organizational research. Randomized experiments (lab or field) maximize internal validity; field experiments (A/B tests, randomized rollouts) add realism at the cost of control. Quasi-experiments lack random assignment but exploit structure: regression discontinuity around a cutoff (e.g., a feature gated at a usage threshold), difference-in-differences around a staggered rollout, interrupted time series. Surveys measure attitudes, beliefs, and self-reported behavior at scale but face common-method bias and the attitude–behavior gap (lesson 5.7 covers measurement). Archival/observational designs mine logs, tickets, and telemetry — high realism and scale, but causal identification needs an explicit strategy (see the econometrics spine in Part 2). Qualitative work — interviews, ethnography, case studies (Yin) — builds theory about mechanisms and meaning where constructs are not yet measurable.

Edmondson and McManus's methodological fit principle ties the choice to the maturity of the theory: nascent theory calls for open-ended qualitative work; intermediate theory for hybrid designs that propose and test provisional relationships; mature theory for quantitative hypothesis testing. Misfit — e.g., running a tightly controlled experiment on a phenomenon nobody has described yet — produces precise answers to premature questions. For human-AI interaction, much of the territory is still nascent-to-intermediate: interview operators first, then design the experiment.

What counts as a theoretical contribution

MGMT 9530 stresses that top journals reward theory, not just findings. Whetten (1989) asks of any paper: what is new, and so what — what are the what, how, and why of the theoretical argument, and what are its boundary conditions (when does it not hold)? Sutton and Staw (1995), "What theory is not," warn that references, data, lists of variables, diagrams, and hypotheses are not themselves theory — theory explains why the relationships hold. Colquitt and Zapata-Phelan (2007) document the field's shift toward theory testing over pure theory building, which raises the bar: a contribution now typically needs both a clear mechanism and a discriminating test. For applied engineers the translation is direct: a study that finds "dashboard X improves incident response by 20%" is a finding; a study that shows why — because it restructures attention during the first 5 minutes, with boundary conditions on team size — is a contribution that travels to the next system you build.

Framing the question and writing for the claim

Design starts before methods: with the research question. Singleton and Straits' guidance, echoed in the seminar, is to frame questions that are important, tractable, and capable of producing a discriminating answer — then let the question choose the design, not the reverse. A practical habit: write the "threats" section of your study before collecting data, listing the three most damaging alternative explanations for the conclusion you hope to draw, and design specifically to rule them out. Reviewers will write that section if you do not; writing it first is how you keep the study you run aligned with the claim you want to make. Multi-method designs earn special credit here: when a lab experiment (strong internal validity) and an archival analysis of production telemetry (strong external validity) converge on the same mechanism, each covers the other's weakness, and the combined claim is far harder to dismiss than either alone.

Worked examples

Example 1: Threats to an A/B test of a recommender UI

You randomize users to a new recommendation carousel and see +8% click-through. Threat audit before celebrating: history — a holiday promotion launched mid-test; attrition — the new UI crashes on old devices, so treated users who stay are a selected sample; instrumentation — logging changed with the UI deploy, so clicks are counted differently; construct — is "clicks" engagement or just novelty curiosity? Each threat suggests a design patch: run a holdout through the promotion, track crashes by arm, freeze logging, and pre-specify a retention-based primary outcome alongside clicks.

Example 2: Methodological fit for studying on-call burnout

Theory about how AI copilots change on-call stress is nascent: nobody has mapped the mechanisms. Fit says start qualitative — shadow five on-call rotations, interview engineers about which moments feel stressful and why. That yields candidate mechanisms (alert triage load, loss of skill confidence). Only then design the intermediate-theory study: a survey measuring those mechanisms across teams, or a quasi-experiment comparing teams with and without the copilot. Jumping straight to a randomized trial would test hypotheses you do not yet have.

Example 3: From finding to contribution

Finding: "Engineers with AI code assistants close 15% more tickets." Contribution version: propose why — assistants reduce the activation cost of starting unfamiliar tasks (mechanism), predict the effect concentrates in unfamiliar codebases and vanishes for routine tickets (boundary conditions), and test both predictions with a design that separates task familiarity from tool access. The first is a metric; the second is theory that tells the next team when to expect the gain and when not to.

Key results to remember

  • Four validities: internal (is it causal?), external (does it generalize?), construct (is it the right concept?), statistical-conclusion (is the inference sound?) — design trades them off explicitly.
  • Core internal-validity threats: history, maturation, selection, regression to the mean, attrition, instrumentation, testing; random assignment is the master defense.
  • Five design families — randomized experiments, quasi-experiments, surveys, archival/observational, qualitative — each buys some validities at the price of others.
  • Methodological fit (Edmondson & McManus): match the design to theory maturity — qualitative for nascent theory, hypothesis-testing for mature theory.
  • A theoretical contribution needs what, how, and why plus boundary conditions (Whetten); data, variables, and diagrams are not theory (Sutton & Staw).
  • Write the threats section before collecting data: name the three most damaging alternative explanations and design to rule them out.

Check your understanding

  1. A team pilots a new incident-review process in Q1 and compares incident counts to Q4. Name two internal-validity threats and a design fix.
    Answer

    History (something else changed between quarters, e.g., a deploy freeze) and regression to the mean (Q4 may have been unusually bad, prompting the pilot). Fix: stagger the rollout across teams (difference-in-differences) or randomize teams to early/late adoption.

  2. Why might a tightly controlled lab study of "trust in AI explanations" have high internal but low external validity?
    Answer

    Random assignment and controlled stimuli support causal claims about the manipulation (internal), but student participants, artificial tasks, and no real stakes may not generalize to operators making consequential decisions under time pressure (external).

  3. Your research question is "how do grid operators actually use probabilistic forecasts in practice?" Which design family fits and why?
    Answer

    Qualitative (interviews, shadowing): the theory is nascent — the phenomenon is undescribed, constructs are not yet measurable, and you need mechanisms and meaning before you can design a quantitative test. This is methodological fit for nascent theory.
