1.1 Applied Regression and ANOVA

Built from Wharton STAT 5000: Applied Regression and Analysis of Variance

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Regression is the workhorse of empirical engineering: you fit a calibration curve to sensor data, predict a chip's failure rate from operating temperature and voltage, estimate how antenna gain varies with design parameters, or quantify the effect of a firmware change on latency. ANOVA is the statistical machinery behind designed experiments — A/B tests of two radio protocols, comparing battery drain across firmware builds. Almost every ML model you will ever train is regression with fancier features, and every "does this coefficient differ from zero" question in your engineering reports is answered by the same linear-model theory. Master the linear model here and the rest of Part 1 (penalized regression, GLMs, modern ML) is a short walk.

Learning objectives

  • Derive the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator from the geometry of projection, and interpret the hat matrix and residuals.
  • State the Gauss-Markov theorem precisely and name the assumptions that make OLS the best linear unbiased estimator.
  • Decompose total variation into model and error sums of squares (ANOVA), and test general linear hypotheses with F-tests.
  • Run residual diagnostics — leverage, studentized residuals, Cook's distance — and decide when a model must be refit.
  • Recognize multicollinearity through variance inflation factors and remedy it rather than trusting unstable coefficients.

Core content

The linear model and OLS geometry

The multiple linear regression model is y = Xβ + ε, where y is an n-vector of responses, X is an n×p design matrix (usually with a column of ones for the intercept), β is a p-vector of unknown coefficients, and ε is an n-vector of errors with E[ε] = 0 and Cov(ε) = σ²I. OLS chooses β̂ to minimize the squared error ‖y − Xβ‖². Setting the gradient to zero gives the normal equations X'Xβ̂ = X'y, and when X has full column rank, β̂ = (X'X)⁻¹X'y.

The geometry matters more than the algebra. The fitted values ŷ = Xβ̂ = Hy, where H = X(X'X)⁻¹X' is the hat matrix, the orthogonal projection onto the column space of X. Residuals e = y − ŷ = (I − H)y are orthogonal to every column of X, i.e., X'e = 0 — the residuals carry no information left over that is linearly predictable from the regressors. This single fact explains why residual plots are so diagnostic: anything systematic left in the residuals is what the model failed to capture.

Gauss-Markov and the classical assumptions

Gauss-Markov theorem. Under the model y = Xβ + ε with E[ε|X] = 0 and Cov(ε|X) = σ²I, OLS is the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE): among all estimators that are linear in y and unbiased for any linear combination c'β, β̂ has the smallest variance. "Best" is conditional — it is best among linear unbiased estimators, and only under homoskedastic, uncorrelated errors with correct mean specification. Proof sketch: any linear unbiased estimator of c'β is c'(X'X)⁻¹X'y + d'y with X'd = 0; its variance is σ²[c'(X'X)⁻¹c + d'd] ≥ σ²c'(X'X)⁻¹c, the OLS variance.

Gaussianity (ε ~ N(0, σ²I)) buys exact small-sample inference: β̂ ~ N(β, σ²(X'X)⁻¹), (n−p)s²/σ² ~ χ² with n−p degrees of freedom (s² = e'e/(n−p)), and these are independent. The Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem gives the clean interpretation of individual coefficients: the OLS estimate of β_j equals the simple regression of y on x_j after both have been "partialled out" — residualized against all other regressors. This is why you should read each coefficient as "the effect of x_j holding the others fixed," and it is also the computational engine behind partial regression plots.

ANOVA decompositions and the F-test

Analysis of variance is regression with categorical predictors, plus a disciplined bookkeeping of where the variation goes. The total sum of squares decomposes: SST = SSR + SSE, i.e., Σ(y_i − ȳ)² = Σ(ŷ_i − ȳ)² + Σ(y_i − ŷ_i)². Under Gaussianity these pieces are independent chi-squares (Cochran's theorem), so their ratio is F-distributed. The F-test for the general linear hypothesis H₀: Cβ = 0 rejects when F = ((SSR_full − SSR_red)/q)/(SSE_full/(n−p)) is large, comparing full and reduced models.

One-way ANOVA models y_ij = μ + α_i + ε_ij for i = 1..k groups, j = 1..n_i replicates; the F-test compares between-group to within-group variation. Two-way and factorial ANOVA add row effects, column effects, and interactions — the effect of factor A at different levels of factor B. Rule of practice: interpret interactions before main effects, because a significant interaction makes the "average" main effect misleading. A classic EE example: testing three modem modulation schemes (factor A) across two channel conditions (factor B); if scheme rankings flip between clean and noisy channels, the interaction is the story.

When Gauss-Markov's homoskedasticity assumption fails — the residual funnel — OLS stays unbiased but the classical standard errors are wrong. White's heteroskedasticity-consistent (sandwich) covariance estimator replaces σ²(X'X)⁻¹ with (X'X)⁻¹(X'Ω̂X)(X'X)⁻¹, where Ω̂ = diag(e_i²): it is consistent for the true sampling variance of β̂ without modeling the heteroskedasticity. In engineering data with variance growing in the signal (power measurements, counts), sandwich SEs are cheap insurance — report them whenever the residuals-vs-fitted plot fans out.

Prediction, transformation, and reporting

Distinguish the confidence interval for the mean response E[y|x₀] = x₀'β — narrow, shrinking with n — from the prediction interval for a new observation y₀ — wider by ±σ, never shrinking to zero. Confusing them is a classic reporting error: a calibration lab quoting the confidence band as the uncertainty of the next measurement understates risk. When diagnostics show skew or multiplicative error structure, transform: log(y) for positive quantities with proportional noise (then back-transform with the smearing correction, not naive exponentiation), or Box-Cox. A transformation chosen to fix the diagnostics is modeling, not cheating — but re-run all diagnostics after transforming, since a fix for variance can break linearity.

Diagnostics: leverage, influence, residuals

The diagonal of the hat matrix, h_ii, is the leverage of observation i: how far its x-values sit from the center of the data. High-leverage points can tilt the whole fit. But leverage alone is not influence — an outlier in x with a y exactly on the line changes nothing. Cook's distance D_i combines leverage and residual size, measuring how much β̂ would move if observation i were deleted; D_i > 1 (or far above the pack) demands investigation. Studentized residuals (residuals divided by their own standard error) flag y-outliers against a t reference.

Every fit deserves: (a) residuals vs. fitted — should be a formless cloud; a funnel means heteroskedasticity, curvature means missing nonlinearity; (b) normal QQ plot of residuals — heavy tails or skew threaten t/F inference; (c) residuals vs. each predictor and vs. time order — catches omitted variables and autocorrelation. When assumptions fail: use White heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors, transform y or x, add polynomial or spline terms, or switch to robust regression. Multicollinearity — near-linear dependence among predictors — inflates Var(β̂_j) by the variance inflation factor VIF_j = 1/(1 − R²_j); VIF above 5–10 signals trouble. The remedy is to drop or combine collinear regressors, never to quote the unstable individual coefficients as if they were identified.

Worked examples

Example 1: Calibrating a temperature sensor

A thermistor is tested at known reference temperatures x = {−20, −10, 0, 10, 20, 30} °C, and the measured voltage y (mV) is {38.1, 44.2, 50.5, 55.9, 62.3, 68.0}. OLS gives ŷ = 49.83 + 0.599x (rounded), R² = 0.9997. The hat-matrix diagonal at x = 30 is h = 0.36 (of n = 6), the highest leverage — a check measurement at 40 °C would disproportionately set the slope. The residual at x = 10 °C is −0.1 mV, studentized residual −0.4: no outlier. By Gauss-Markov, with homoskedastic error this slope is BLUE; Frisch-Waugh-Lovell says the 0.599 mV/°C estimate is exactly the slope of y-on-x after removing means. Inverting the calibration (predicting temperature from voltage) is a different regression — do not invert the fitted line; refit x on y.

Example 2: A/B test of two firmware builds (one-way ANOVA)

Build A and build B are each flashed onto 20 boards; packet loss rate (%) over a 24-hour stress test: A = {1.2, 1.5, 1.1, ...}, ȳ_A = 1.30; B: ȳ_B = 0.95. One-way ANOVA with k = 2 groups: between-group SS = 20(1.30 − 1.125)² + 20(0.95 − 1.125)² = 1.225 on 1 df; within-group SS = 4.10 on 38 df; F = (1.225/1)/(4.10/38) = 11.35, p ≈ 0.002. Reject equality of means. Note this equals the two-sample t-test squared (F = t² when k = 2) — ANOVA generalizes it to any number of groups. Residual QQ plot is straight; Levene's check suggests equal variances, so the pooled F is trusted. Conclusion for the report: build B reduces mean packet loss by ~0.35 percentage points (95% CI roughly ±0.22).

Example 3: Modulation × channel interaction (two-way ANOVA)

Three modulation schemes (QPSK, 16-QAM, 64-QAM) × two channels (AWGN, Rayleigh fading), 10 trials each, measuring bit-error rate (log scale). The interaction F is significant: 64-QAM beats 16-QAM in AWGN but collapses in Rayleigh fading, while QPSK is mediocre-but-stable everywhere. The main effect "64-QAM is best on average" is therefore misleading — the interaction says there is no single winner; the choice depends on the channel. Reporting marginal means without the interaction plot would actively mislead a design review. This is why interactions get interpreted first.

Example 4: Cook's distance catches a logging glitch

Regressing end-to-end latency (ms) on offered load for a messaging service, n = 200: OLS slope looks alarming — 4.2 ms per 100 concurrent connections. Residuals-vs-fitted shows one point far out: at load 8,500, latency 9,800 ms, with h_ii = 0.11 and studentized residual 6.1, Cook's D = 2.3 — by far the largest. Investigation finds a logging daemon froze and timestamps were buffered; the true latency was ~120 ms. Dropping that observation, slope becomes 1.1 ms/100 conn. and D of the next-largest point is 0.08. Lesson: never ship a regression conclusion without Cook's distance and a residual plot, and always refit without influential points to see whether the engineering conclusion changes before acting on it.

import numpy as np, statsmodels.api as sm
X = sm.add_constant(load)          # design matrix with intercept
fit = sm.OLS(latency, X).fit()
infl = fit.get_influence()
cooks_d = infl.cooks_distance[0]   # Cook's distance per point
leverage = infl.hat_matrix_diag     # hat-matrix diagonal h_ii
sm.qqplot(fit.resid, line='s')      # normality check

Key results to remember

  • OLS geometry: ŷ = Hy projects y onto the column space of X; residuals e = (I − H)y satisfy X'e = 0. Read the hat matrix, not just the coefficients.
  • Gauss-Markov: OLS is BLUE — best among linear unbiased estimators — under E[ε|X] = 0 and Cov(ε|X) = σ²I. "Best" fails if errors are heteroskedastic or the mean is misspecified.
  • Frisch-Waugh-Lovell: each β̂_j is the slope of y on x_j after partialling out the other regressors — the exact meaning of "holding other variables fixed."
  • ANOVA: SST = SSR + SSE with independent χ² pieces (Cochran), giving F-tests for general linear hypotheses, one-way, two-way, and factorial designs.
  • Interactions first: in factorial designs, interpret interaction effects before main effects, or the main effects can actively mislead.
  • Diagnostics are mandatory: residuals vs. fitted, QQ plots, leverage h_ii, studentized residuals, Cook's distance; refit without influential points and compare conclusions.
  • Multicollinearity (VIF): collinear predictors inflate variances; drop or combine them rather than reporting unstable coefficients.

Check your understanding

  1. Why does the residual vector e always satisfy X'e = 0? What does this imply about the sample correlation between any predictor and the residuals?
    Answer

    Because e = (I − H)y and H projects onto col(X), (I − H) projects onto its orthogonal complement, so X'e = X'(I − H)y = (X' − X'H)y = (X' − X')y = 0 since X'H = X'. Hence every column of X (each predictor, including the intercept) has exactly zero sample correlation with the residuals.

  2. An engineer says "OLS is the best estimator, so I don't need to worry about heteroskedasticity." What is wrong?
    Answer

    Gauss-Markov's "best" is among linear unbiased estimators and assumes homoskedastic, uncorrelated errors. Under heteroskedasticity OLS is still unbiased but no longer efficient, and the usual standard errors are wrong (typically too small). Use White heteroskedasticity-consistent SEs or weighted least squares.

  3. In a two-way ANOVA of chip yield by foundry (2 levels) and process node (3 levels), the interaction is significant but the foundry main effect is not. Can you conclude foundries don't matter? Explain.
    Answer

    No. A significant interaction means the foundry effect differs by node — e.g., foundry A wins at one node and loses at another — so the averaged-out main effect can be near zero while foundry choice matters enormously within each node. Report simple effects per node and the interaction plot, not the main effect.

  4. An observation has huge leverage but tiny residual. Can it still have a large Cook's distance? Why or why not?
    Answer

    No — Cook's distance is proportional to (studentized residual)² times a leverage factor, so a point sitting nearly on the fitted line has small D regardless of leverage. But remove caution: it still "anchors" the fit, and a small change to its y could make it very influential.

  5. Two predictors have VIFs of 12 each. What does that mean numerically, and what would you do?
    Answer

    Each coefficient's variance is inflated 12× relative to orthogonal predictors (standard error inflated by √12 ≈ 3.5). Check their correlation; drop one, combine them (e.g., a sum or ratio), or use ridge regression — but don't publish the two unstable coefficients as separately identified effects.
