0.1 Probability Refresher

Bridge lesson — mathematical foundations for the OID PhD core

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Probability is the language in which AI systems, communication channels, and sensor networks are specified: noise is a random process, a classifier's output is a conditional probability, and every estimator's error bar is a statement about convergence. The OID doctoral core — statistical inference, econometrics, stochastic optimization — assumes you can compute expectations fluently, recognize the handful of distributions that appear everywhere, and read limit theorems the way a signal engineer reads a spectrum. This lesson rebuilds that fluency with the models you will actually meet: additive Gaussian noise, bit errors, packet arrivals, and detection under uncertainty.

Learning objectives

  • Compute expectations, variances, and conditional expectations of random variables, and apply linearity of expectation and the tower property to engineering problems.
  • Identify the correct model among Bernoulli, Gaussian, Poisson, and exponential distributions for a given noise, arrival, or detection scenario, and recall their means, variances, and memoryless property.
  • State the weak and strong laws of large numbers and the central limit theorem precisely, and explain what each says about Monte Carlo averaging and measurement error.
  • Distinguish the four modes of convergence — in r-th mean, almost surely, in probability, and in distribution — and recognize when each is the right tool.
  • Formulate a detection problem (e.g., signal in noise) as a hypothesis test on random variables.

Core content

Random variables and distributions

A random variable X is a function from a sample space to numbers (say, the real line); its distribution is the full description of where those numbers land. For a discrete X we write the probability mass function p(x) = P(X = x); for a continuous X we write the density f(x), so that P(a ≤ X ≤ b) is the integral of f over [a, b], and the cumulative distribution function F(x) = P(X ≤ x) is the running total. Every result in this lesson, and much of statistical inference, flows from these definitions plus one workhorse: the expectation E[X], the probability-weighted average of the values X takes. For functions, E[g(X)] = Σ g(x) p(x) or ∫ g(x) f(x) dx, depending on the type — the "law of the unconscious statistician," called that because you use it before you can prove it.

Variance, Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])²] = E[X²] − (E[X])², measures spread about the mean, and the standard deviation is its square root. Linearity of expectation — E[aX + bY] = a E[X] + b E[Y] with no independence assumption at all — is the single most used fact in applied probability. Variance is not linear: Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2 Cov(X, Y), where Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μ_X)(Y − μ_Y)]; the covariance vanishes when X and Y are independent, but independence is a strictly stronger condition than zero covariance. Correlation ρ = Cov(X,Y) / (σ_X σ_Y) rescales this to [−1, 1]. Markov's inequality, P(|X| ≥ t) ≤ E[|X|] / t, and its corollary Chebyshev's, P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k², give crude but universal tail bounds from nothing but a moment — the raw material of which asymptotic proofs are built.

Conditioning: the engine of learning and detection

The conditional distribution of X given Y = y describes your updated beliefs about X once y is observed. For discrete variables, P(X = x | Y = y) = P(X = x, Y = y) / P(Y = y). The conditional expectation E[X | Y] is itself a random variable — a function of Y — and it is the optimal (minimum mean-squared-error) predictor of X given knowledge of Y. Two identities govern almost all calculations. The law of total expectation (the tower property): E[X] = E[E[X | Y]] — average the conditional average over the conditioning variable. The law of total variance: Var(X) = E[Var(X | Y)] + Var(E[X | Y]) — total uncertainty splits into average within-group uncertainty plus between-group uncertainty. Bayes' rule is the conditional-distribution version of the same idea: P(H | data) ∝ P(data | H) P(H). In communications, this is the matched-filter receiver's job: form P(symbol | received signal). In ML, the softmax output of a classifier is an estimate of P(class | features). Independence means the conditional equals the marginal — observing Y tells you nothing about X.

The four distributions you will meet constantly

Bernoulli(p). A single coin flip: P(X = 1) = p, P(X = 0) = 1 − p. Mean p, variance p(1 − p). It models one bit error, one packet drop, one click. A sum of n independent Bernoullis is Binomial(n, p) with mean np and variance np(1 − p) — the count of successes.

Gaussian (normal) N(μ, σ²). Density proportional to exp(−(x − μ)² / 2σ²). The most important distribution in engineering because of the central limit theorem below: sums of many small independent disturbances become Gaussian regardless of their source. Thermal noise in a receiver is modeled as additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN); measurement errors, log-returns, and standardized test statistics all lean Gaussian. Key facts: fully determined by mean and variance; any linear combination of jointly Gaussian variables is Gaussian; the 68–95–99.7 rule quantiles; and the squared standardized variable ((X − μ)/σ)² follows a chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom — the seed of all the t, F, and χ² inference machinery in linear models.

Poisson(λ). P(X = k) = e^(−λ) λ^k / k! for k = 0, 1, 2, …. Counts of events in a fixed interval when events arrive independently at a constant average rate: photons at a detector, packets at a router, calls at a service desk. Mean and variance both equal λ — a diagnostic signature: if your count data has variance ≈ mean, Poisson is a candidate. Sums of independent Poissons are Poisson.

Exponential(λ). Density λe^(−λt) for t ≥ 0; the inter-arrival time between Poisson events. Mean 1/λ, variance 1/λ². Its defining property is memorylessness: P(T > s + t | T > s) = P(T > t) — a component that has survived s hours is "as good as new," which is why it models waiting times and lifetimes of memoryless systems. The only continuous memoryless distribution.

Laws of large numbers: why averaging works

Let X_1, X_2, … be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with mean μ and finite variance. The weak law of large numbers (WLLN) says the sample mean X̄_n = (X_1 + ··· + X_n)/n converges in probability to μ: for every ε > 0, P(|X̄_n − μ| > ε) → 0. Chebyshev's inequality proves this in a few lines, since Var(X̄_n) = σ²/n. The strong law (SLLN, Kolmogorov) strengthens "in probability" to "almost surely": P(X̄_n → μ) = 1, with only E|X| < ∞ required — no finite variance needed. These are the justification for every Monte Carlo simulation, every sensor average, and every empirical risk minimization: averages of repeated draws settle on the truth. The SLLN is also why relative frequencies converge to probabilities, grounding the frequentist interpretation you will use in estimation.

The central limit theorem: the universal noise model

With X_i i.i.d., mean μ, variance σ² < ∞, the Lindeberg–Lévy CLT says that √n (X̄_n − μ) / σ converges in distribution to N(0, 1) — the standard normal. In words: the scaled error of an average is approximately standard Gaussian for large n, no matter what the X_i look like (dice, packet delays, bit errors). This is why AWGN is the default noise model: real noise is the sum of countless microscopic disturbances, so CLT says it looks Gaussian. In practice, the approximation is already good for n in the dozens for well-behaved distributions — but "large enough" depends on skewness and tail weight, which is why the CLT comes with no universal rate. Variants relax the identical-distribution requirement: the Lindeberg–Feller CLT handles independent but non-identical variables provided no single one dominates. The multivariate CLT gives joint normality of vector averages, the foundation of large-sample inference for regression coefficients.

Modes of convergence: a hierarchy

There are four standard ways a sequence X_n can approach X. Convergence in r-th mean: E|X_n − X|^r → 0 (r = 2 is "mean-square convergence," the workhorse for estimator MSE). Almost-sure convergence: P(X_n → X) = 1 — the strongest, used in SLLN. Convergence in probability: P(|X_n − X| > ε) → 0 for all ε > 0 — the WLLN's mode, and enough for consistency of estimators. Convergence in distribution: F_n(x) → F(x) at continuity points of F — the CLT's mode, the weakest. They nest: r-th mean ⇒ almost sure ⇒ in probability ⇒ in distribution (mean ⇒ a.s. requires care, but the practical chain holds as stated). Convergence in distribution only concerns the laws, not the variables themselves: √n(X̄_n − μ)/σ never "settles down" to a number, yet its distribution settles on the standard normal. Two transfer tools close the loop. Slutsky's theorem: if X_n converges in distribution to X and Y_n converges in probability to a constant c, then X_n + Y_n →ᵈ X + c and X_n Y_n →ᵈ cX — this is how you replace an unknown σ by its estimate in confidence intervals. The delta method: if √n(T_n − θ) →ᵈ N(0, τ²) and g is differentiable at θ with g′(θ) ≠ 0, then √n(g(T_n) − g(θ)) →ᵈ N(0, (g′(θ))² τ²) — asymptotics propagate through smooth transformations.

Characteristic functions and detection

The characteristic function φ(t) = E[e^(itX)] determines a distribution completely (the inversion theorem): proving that sums converge in distribution often means proving their characteristic functions converge pointwise — this is the standard route to the CLT. They factor over sums of independent variables, turning convolutions into multiplication, which is why they appear in every serious proof of limit theorems.

Finally, detection ties it together. Receiving a bit over a noisy channel is a hypothesis test: under H₀ the observation is pure noise, under H₁ it is signal plus noise. The Neyman–Pearson lemma says the most powerful test at a given false-alarm rate rejects for large values of the likelihood ratio f₁(x)/f₀(x). For Gaussian noise this yields the familiar threshold detector, and its error probabilities (miss, false alarm) are Gaussian tail probabilities — the Q-function. This single setup — two hypotheses, a likelihood ratio, tail probabilities of the CLT's limit — previews the entire hypothesis-testing apparatus of Lesson 1.x on inference.

Worked examples

Example 1: Bit errors over a noisy link

A binary symmetric channel flips each transmitted bit independently with probability p = 0.01. Over a block of n = 1000 bits, the number of errors E is Binomial(1000, 0.01): mean 10, variance 9.9. The probability of more than 15 errors can be approximated by CLT: (E − 10)/√9.9 is approximately N(0,1), so P(E > 15) ≈ P(Z > 5/3.147) = P(Z > 1.589) ≈ 0.056. A quick sanity check in Python:

import math, random
n, p, trials = 1000, 0.01, 20000
cnt = sum(sum(random.random() < p for _ in range(n)) > 15 for _ in range(trials))
print("simulated P(E>15) ≈", cnt / trials)   # near 0.056
z = (15.5 - n*p) / math.sqrt(n*p*(1-p))        # continuity correction
print("normal approx:", 1 - 0.5*(1+math.erf(z/math.sqrt(2))))

Example 2: Poisson arrivals and exponential waiting

Requests arrive at a server as a Poisson process at λ = 2 per second. The number arriving in 5 seconds is Poisson(10): P(exactly 12) = e^(−10) 10^12 / 12! ≈ 0.095. The waiting time until the next request is Exponential(2), mean ½ s. By memorylessness, if nothing has arrived in the last 3 seconds, the expected remaining wait is still ½ s — no "overdue" arrivals. The probability the next request takes longer than 1 s is e^(−2·1) ≈ 0.135. Queueing theory for routers and cloud schedulers is built on exactly this duality.

Example 3: Averaging a noisy sensor (LLN in action)

A sensor reports temperature T = θ + ε with ε zero-mean, σ = 2 °C. One reading has standard error 2 °C; the average of n = 100 readings has standard error σ/√n = 0.2 °C — the square-root law. Chebyshev guarantees P(|T̄ − θ| ≥ 0.5) ≤ (0.2)²/(0.5)² = 0.16, a crude bound; the CLT refines it to ≈ P(|Z| ≥ 2.5) ≈ 0.012. The law of large numbers is why every sensor datasheet quotes accuracy "after averaging," and the CLT is why the quoted confidence intervals use z-scores.

Example 4: Threshold detection in AWGN

Transmit s = +A for bit 1 and s = −A for bit 0; receive y = s + n with n ∼ N(0, σ²). The Neyman–Pearson-optimal rule decides 1 when the likelihood ratio exceeds a threshold, which for Gaussian noise reduces to comparing y to 0. With A = 1, σ = 0.5, the bit-error probability is P(n > 1) under bit 0: P(Z > 2) ≈ 0.0228. Doubling A halves σ/A and the error collapses via the Gaussian tail — this is the entire link-budget calculation for a digital receiver in one integral.

Key results to remember

  • Linearity of expectation needs no independence; variance adds only when variables are uncorrelated.
  • E[X | Y] is the best mean-squared predictor of X given Y; the tower property E[X] = E[E[X | Y]] and the variance decomposition Var(X) = E[Var(X|Y)] + Var(E[X|Y]) organize every conditioning calculation.
  • Bernoulli/Binomial model single trials and counts of trials; Gaussian models sums of small disturbances (AWGN); Poisson models counts of rare events; exponential models waiting times and is the unique continuous memoryless law.
  • The weak law says sample means converge in probability to the mean; the strong law upgrades this to almost-sure convergence under only E|X| < ∞.
  • The CLT: √n(X̄_n − μ)/σ converges in distribution to N(0, 1), regardless of the underlying distribution (finite variance required) — the justification for Gaussian noise models and z-based inference.
  • Convergence hierarchy: r-th mean ⇒ almost surely ⇒ in probability ⇒ in distribution; consistency of an estimator is convergence in probability, while its limit distribution (CLT) is convergence in distribution.
  • Slutsky's theorem lets you plug estimated constants into limit statements; the delta method propagates asymptotics through smooth transformations.
  • The Neyman–Pearson likelihood-ratio test is the optimal detector: for Gaussian noise it reduces to thresholding the observation.

Check your understanding

  1. Let X_1, …, X_n be i.i.d. with mean μ and variance σ². What are the mean and variance of the sample mean X̄_n, and what does the CLT say about the distribution of √n(X̄_n − μ)/σ as n grows?
    Answer

    E[X̄_n] = μ and Var(X̄_n) = σ²/n by linearity and independence. The CLT says √n(X̄_n − μ)/σ converges in distribution to the standard normal N(0, 1) — so for large n, X̄_n is approximately N(μ, σ²/n).

  2. Packets arrive at a switch as a Poisson process with rate λ = 4 per second. What is the distribution of the number of arrivals in 3 seconds, and what is the distribution of the time until the next arrival? What is the probability that no packet arrives in the next 0.5 s?
    Answer

    Counts are Poisson(λt) = Poisson(12); the waiting time is Exponential(4), mean 0.25 s. P(no arrival in 0.5 s) = e^(−4·0.5) = e^(−2) ≈ 0.135.

  3. Explain the difference between convergence in probability and convergence in distribution, and give one example of each from this lesson.
    Answer

    Convergence in probability means P(|X_n − X| > ε) → 0: the variable itself gets close to a target (WLLN: X̄_n → μ). Convergence in distribution means only the CDFs converge: the CLT's √n(X̄_n − μ)/σ never settles to a number, but its law approaches N(0, 1).

  4. A detector observes y and must choose between H₀ (noise only) and H₁ (signal + noise). What does the Neyman–Pearson lemma say is the optimal test, and why does it become a simple threshold for Gaussian noise?
    Answer

    Reject H₀ for large values of the likelihood ratio f₁(y)/f₀(y) — it is the most powerful test at any fixed false-alarm rate. For Gaussian noise the log likelihood ratio is linear in y, so the ratio test reduces to comparing y against a threshold.
