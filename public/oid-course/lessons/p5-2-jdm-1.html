5.2 Judgment and Decision Making I

Built from Wharton MKTG 9500: Judgment and Decision Making Perspectives on Consumer Behavior — Part A

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

Lesson 5.1 gave you the normative ideal: expected utility. This lesson is the descriptive corrective — the heuristics-and-biases program and prospect theory — and it is the reason your systems cannot assume rational users. Every recommender, dashboard, alert, and configuration UI is a choice environment, and its users will anchor on defaults, overweight vivid failures, and treat losses as roughly twice as painful as equal gains. If you build AI for humans — copilots, decision support, explainable models, product UX — these regularities are design constraints, not trivia. Know them and you can predict user behavior, debias your own engineering judgments, and design interfaces that work with the grain of human cognition.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the three classic heuristics — representativeness, availability, anchoring-and-adjustment — and one canonical demonstration of each.
  • Explain how each heuristic produces systematic, predictable biases (base-rate neglect, miscalibrated risk perception, insufficient adjustment).
  • State the components of prospect theory: reference dependence, diminishing sensitivity, loss aversion, and probability weighting.
  • Distinguish the editing phase from the evaluation phase and explain framing effects with the Asian disease problem.
  • Apply heuristics-and-biases and prospect-theory insights to AI product and interface design decisions.

Core content

The heuristics-and-biases program

Tversky and Kahneman (1974) argued that under uncertainty people do not compute probabilities; they rely on a small number of heuristics — mental shortcuts that are usually useful but produce systematic, predictable errors called biases. Three heuristics anchor the program. Representativeness judges probability by similarity: how much does this instance resemble the category's stereotype? The classic demonstration: subjects read a personality sketch ("Steve is shy, withdrawn, helpful but with little interest in people") and judge him more likely a librarian than a farmer — ignoring that farmers vastly outnumber librarians. Similarity swamps the base rate. The same mechanism drives insensitivity to sample size and the gambler's fallacy (after five reds, black feels "due," as if a short sequence must be representative of the long-run process).

Availability judges frequency by ease of recall: vivid, recent, or emotionally charged events feel more probable. After a widely reported plane crash, people overestimate flight risk and drive instead — a statistically worse choice. For engineers, availability is why the last outage dominates the next capacity plan and why a single dramatic model failure outweighs a thousand quiet successes in a stakeholder's mind. Anchoring-and-adjustment starts from an initial value — even an arbitrary one — and adjusts insufficiently. In the classic experiment, spinning a rigged wheel to land on 10 versus 65 before asking subjects to estimate the share of African nations in the UN moved median answers from 25% to 45%. Any number in the environment — last year's budget, the first hyperparameter tried, the default slider position — becomes an anchor.

Prospect theory: reference dependence

Kahneman and Tversky (1979) built the leading descriptive theory of choice under risk as a direct challenge to expected utility. Its first break with the normative theory is reference dependence: people evaluate outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point (the status quo, an expectation, a goal), not as final wealth states. The same $5 price cut feels like a gain against a $50 reference price and like a reduced loss against a $60 one — which is why framing, not just substance, moves behavior.

The value function v(x) defined over gains and losses has three signature properties. It is concave for gains and convex for losses (diminishing sensitivity: the difference between $100 and $200 feels larger than between $1,100 and $1,200, and symmetrically for losses). It is steeper for losses than for gains (loss aversion: losses loom larger than gains, with the loss-aversion coefficient λ ≈ 2 — a loss hurts about twice as much as an equal-sized gain pleases). And it explains risk attitudes that expected utility struggles with: people are risk-averse over gains (preferring a sure $500 to a 50% chance of $1,000) but risk-seeking over losses (preferring a 50% chance of losing $1,000 to a sure $500 loss) — the reflection effect. This is why users fight to keep a deprecated feature (loss) far harder than they lobbied to get it (gain), and why decommissioning anything needs a migration story, not just a shutdown notice.

Probability weighting and the two phases

The second break with expected utility is probability weighting: people transform stated probabilities through a weighting function π(p) that overweights small probabilities and underweights moderate-to-large ones. Overweighting explains lottery tickets and excessive fear of rare catastrophes; underweighting explains why certain outcomes feel disproportionately more attractive than 99% chances (the certainty effect). Cumulative prospect theory (Tversky & Kahneman, 1992) later repaired technical problems with rank-dependent weights, but the qualitative shape — an inverse-S curve, steep near 0 and 1 — is the working fact.

Prospect theory also splits choice into two phases. In the editing phase, the decision maker recodes the problem: setting the reference point, coding outcomes as gains or losses, combining and segregating prospects, and discarding common components. In the evaluation phase, the edited prospects are valued with v and π. Editing is where framing effects live. The Asian disease problem (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981): a program saving 200 of 600 people for sure is preferred to a 1/3 chance of saving all 600 — but when the same options are described as 400 people dying for sure versus a 2/3 chance of 600 dying, the risky option wins. Logically identical outcomes, reversed preferences, purely from the gain/loss frame. Keep this conceptually distinct from prospect theory's valuation machinery: framing is about how descriptions are edited; prospect theory is about how edited prospects are evaluated.

What this means for design

Three design implications recur. First, reference points are design variables: defaults, status-quo options, and progress indicators set the zero against which users code gains and losses. Second, because of loss aversion and diminishing sensitivity, the same information should be framed to minimize perceived loss when you need adoption (migrations, pricing changes, permission prompts). Third, probability weighting means users will misread the uncertainties your system communicates: rare model-failure modes will be overweighted, and "99% accurate" will be underweighted relative to certainty. Calibrated communication — frequencies instead of probabilities, concrete comparisons — is a prescriptive response built directly on the descriptive findings.

Worked examples

Example 1: Base-rate neglect in fraud detection triage

Your fraud model flags a transaction with a test that is 95% sensitive and has a 5% false-positive rate. An analyst sees a flagged transaction from a segment where fraud base rate is 1 in 1,000 and judges it "probably fraud" — representativeness (it looks like the fraud profile) swamps the base rate. Bayes: P(fraud | flag) = (0.95·0.001) / (0.95·0.001 + 0.05·0.999) ≈ 0.0187, under 2%. The interface fix is prescriptive: display the posterior probability ("about 2 in 100 flagged transactions in this segment are fraud") instead of the raw flag, so the analyst's judgment starts from the right number rather than the vivid profile.

Example 2: Loss-framed upgrade messaging

You need users to migrate off a legacy API. Gain frame: "Upgrade to get 30% faster inference." Loss frame: "Staying on the legacy API costs you 30% slower inference — and support ends in 90 days." With λ ≈ 2, the loss frame roughly doubles the motivational weight of the same 30% figure, and the deadline sets a reference point that makes inaction a coded loss. Field evidence consistently shows loss-framed, deadline-anchored migration prompts outperforming gain-framed ones — the same information, edited differently.

Example 3: Anchoring in capacity planning

Last year's GPU cluster budget was $2M. The planning committee "adjusts" to $2.2M. An outside-view analysis — reference-class forecasting over similar ML teams' actual spend — suggests $3.5M. Anchoring-and-adjustment predicts the committee's number stays near $2M regardless of the evidence, because adjustment from an anchor is typically insufficient. The debiasing procedure: require the outside-view estimate to be written down before last year's number is mentioned in the meeting, so the anchor never enters the room.

Example 4: Probability weighting in risk communication

# inverse-S style weighting (illustrative, not a fitted function)
def w(p, gamma=0.6):
    return p**gamma / (p**gamma + (1-p)**gamma)**(1/gamma)

for p in [0.001, 0.01, 0.5, 0.99, 1.0]:
    print(f"stated p={p:<6} perceived weight={w(p):.3f}")

Output shows small probabilities inflated (0.001 → ~0.02) and high probabilities deflated (0.99 → ~0.93). If your dashboard says a cascading-failure scenario has p = 0.001, stakeholders will behave as if it were ~20× more likely — so present rare risks as frequencies ("about 1 year in 20 at current scale") and pair them with the expected-loss calculation rather than the raw probability.

Key results to remember

  • Representativeness judges probability by similarity, producing base-rate neglect, sample-size insensitivity, and the gambler's fallacy.
  • Availability judges frequency by ease of recall — vivid, recent events are systematically overweighted in risk judgment.
  • Anchoring-and-adjustment starts from any salient number and adjusts insufficiently; control the anchor or require outside-view estimates first.
  • Prospect theory's value function is reference-dependent, concave for gains, convex for losses, and steeper for losses (λ ≈ 2): risk-averse over gains, risk-seeking over losses.
  • Probability weighting overweights small probabilities and underweights moderate-to-large ones (inverse-S shape); the certainty effect follows.
  • Choice has an editing phase (reference point, gain/loss coding, framing) and an evaluation phase (v and π) — the Asian disease problem shows logically identical descriptions reversing preferences.
  • Reference points, frames, and probability formats are design variables: set defaults deliberately, frame to minimize perceived loss, and communicate uncertainty as frequencies with expected consequences.

Check your understanding

  1. A security dashboard shows "threat score: 87/100" for an event. An analyst treats it as near-certain compromise. Which heuristic is at work, and what information is being neglected?
    Answer

    Representativeness: the vivid high score resembles the analyst's stereotype of an attack, swamping the base rate of true compromises among flagged events. The neglected information is the base rate — without it, the score cannot be converted into a posterior probability.

  2. Why does loss aversion (λ ≈ 2) make product deprecations harder than feature launches, and what is the prescriptive fix?
    Answer

    Removing a feature is coded as a loss against the status-quo reference point and hurts ~2× as much as gaining the feature pleased. The fix: frame the change around what users keep and gain, provide a migration path that preserves the reference point's key attributes, and announce early so expectations (the reference) adjust before the loss lands.

  3. In the Asian disease problem, why is the preference reversal a violation of expected utility but not of prospect theory?
    Answer

    Expected utility evaluates final outcomes, which are identical across frames, so it predicts identical choices. Prospect theory evaluates edited prospects: the "lives saved" frame codes outcomes as gains (risk aversion → sure option), while the "lives lost" frame codes them as losses (risk seeking → gamble). The editing phase makes framing part of the theory.

  4. Your team overweights a 0.1% catastrophic model-failure probability in planning. Which component of prospect theory explains this, and how should you communicate the risk instead?
    Answer

    Probability weighting: small probabilities are overweighted by π(p). Communicate as a frequency over a concrete horizon ("roughly once in 20 years at current deployment scale") and pair it with expected loss, so the decision can be made on consequences rather than the distorted weight.
