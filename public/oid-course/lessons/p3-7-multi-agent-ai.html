3.7 Multi-Agent AI: Auctions, Matching, Mechanism Design

Bridge lesson — connecting OID core to AI, technology, and electrical systems

Why this matters for AI, technology, and electrical systems

This is where Part 3 pays off. Modern AI systems are multi-agent systems: ad auctions allocate billions of impressions daily, matching algorithms assign students to schools and drivers to riders, and fleets of RL agents must be given incentives to cooperate rather than defect. The game theory, auction theory, and mechanism design of Lessons 3.2 and 3.5 are not background — they are the design tools. This lesson translates them into the four domains where an AI/EE engineer will actually use them: sponsored search and ad auctions, matching markets, incentive design for multi-agent RL, and spectrum auctions.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how sponsored-search auctions work (GSP vs. VCG) and why platforms moved to first-price.
  • Define stable matching and run deferred acceptance; identify strategy-proofness for the proposing side.
  • Design incentive schemes (reward shaping, contracts) that align multi-agent RL agents with system objectives.
  • Describe spectrum auction design (simultaneous multiple-round, incentive auctions) as applied mechanism design.
  • Diagnose a multi-agent AI failure as an equilibrium problem and propose a mechanism fix.

Core content

Sponsored search: the auction that funds the internet

When you search, an auction runs in milliseconds. The classic design is Generalized Second Price (GSP): advertisers bid per click, are ranked by bid × quality score, and each pays the minimum bid needed to keep its position (roughly the next-ranked advertiser's bid). GSP is not truthful — bidding your value is not dominant — but it has locally envy-free equilibria that resemble Vickrey outcomes, which is why it survived so long. The theoretically clean alternative is Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG): each advertiser pays the externality it imposes on others (the welfare loss its presence causes), making truthful reporting a dominant strategy.

Why did Google (2019) and others move display advertising from second-price to first-price auctions? Lesson 3.2's revenue equivalence says it shouldn't matter — under IPV, symmetry, risk neutrality. Real ad markets violate all three: values are affiliated (common components in user value), bidders are asymmetric (different data and algorithms), and header bidding let publishers observe and exploit second-price dynamics. With soft floors, dynamic reserve prices, and first-price formats, platforms moved toward Myerson-style optimal design: set bidder-specific reserves from estimated value distributions (virtual valuations, Lesson 3.5) and let first-price bidding aggregate. The engineering lesson: auction theory's "irrelevance" results tell you exactly which assumption to violate when you want to change revenue — and ML-based bid shading prediction is now part of the mechanism itself.

Matching markets: deferred acceptance

Many allocations can't use prices: school choice, medical residency, kidney exchange, refugee resettlement. The tool is two-sided matching. A matching is stable if no unmatched pair prefers each other to their assigned partners (no blocking pair). Gale and Shapley (1962) deferred acceptance algorithm: one side proposes in preference order; the other side tentatively holds the best offer and rejects the rest; rejected proposers propose to their next choice; iterate until no rejections. The result is stable, and it is proposer-optimal (each proposer gets the best stable partner achievable) — and strategy-proof for the proposing side: proposers can never gain by misreporting preferences.

The design choices matter enormously in practice: in school choice, having students propose (strategy-proof for families) vs. schools propose changes who gets the favorable stable matching; Boston's old manipulable mechanism was replaced by deferred acceptance precisely to remove gaming. Kidney exchange is matching without money on a compatibility graph — cycles and chains of donations — where the mechanism must handle dynamic arrivals and incentive compatibility of hospitals (which might withhold easy-to-match pairs). For AI engineers: any assignment problem with preferences on both sides and no (or constrained) prices — mentor matching, shift allocation, reviewer assignment, ride dispatch with driver preferences — is a matching-market design problem, and deferred acceptance is the default algorithm.

Incentive design for multi-agent RL

Now put AI agents inside the games. In multi-agent RL (MARL), each agent maximizes its own reward; the system designer wants a global objective (throughput, safety, fairness). This is mechanism design with learning agents: choose the reward functions (the "contracts," Lesson 3.5) so that the learning equilibrium implements the desired outcome. The failure modes are game-theoretic: independent Q-learners in a social dilemma converge to mutual defection (the Nash equilibrium of Lesson 3.2's prisoner's dilemma, learned rather than derived); reward shaping that isn't potential-based changes the equilibrium set; agents learn to exploit loopholes in the reward (specification gaming = the informativeness principle violated — you're conditioning on a gameable signal).

The design toolkit: (a) contract-theoretic reward design — pay agents on informative, hard-to-game signals of contribution (Holmström's principle: use the likelihood-ratio test on your reward features); (b) mechanism layers — run a VCG-style payment rule on top of learned policies so truthful revelation of costs/values is dominant, then optimize the physical allocation; (c) equilibrium selection — when the folk theorem gives you many equilibria (Lesson 3.2), use centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE), shared reward shaping toward the Pareto-dominant equilibrium, or opponent-shaping (agents that account for how their behavior shapes others' learning, e.g., LOLA) to steer learning to the good equilibrium; (d) commitment devices — let a trusted coordinator move first (Stackelberg), since commitment has value. Concretely: autonomous vehicle fleets negotiating merges are a bargaining game (Rubinstein) — design the protocol (who yields, what signal) rather than hoping learned policies discover courtesy.

Spectrum auctions: mechanism design at national scale

The EE tie-in. Spectrum licenses are worth tens of billions, with complementarities (a nationwide footprint is worth more than the sum of regional licenses) and common-value components (everyone's value depends on uncertain demand). The simultaneous multiple-round auction (SMRA, pioneered for the 1994 FCC PCS auctions by Milgrom, McAfee, and Wilson): all licenses auctioned simultaneously in rounds, bids public, activity rules force engagement — lets bidders arbitrage across substitutes and assemble complementary packages, with the linkage principle (Milgrom–Weber) raising revenue through information revelation. Exposure risk (winning some but not all of a complementary package) was later addressed by package bidding (combinatorial auctions). The 2016–17 FCC incentive auction added a reverse auction (buying back licenses from TV broadcasters via a descending-clock mechanism) followed by a forward auction — a two-sided mechanism repacking spectrum from low-value (broadcast) to high-value (mobile broadband) use. For the engineer: 5G/6G spectrum policy, CBRS priority-access licenses, and any future dynamic spectrum sharing regime are mechanism-design problems where the auction format determines who builds what networks.

A diagnostic template

When a multi-agent AI system misbehaves, run this checklist from Part 3: (1) Write down the game — players, actions, payoffs, timing, information (Lesson 3.2; the skill's first rule). (2) Find the equilibrium — is the bad behavior a Nash equilibrium of the incentives as designed? If yes, no amount of better learning fixes it; redesign the mechanism. (3) Check the information structure — hidden types or actions? Then it's adverse selection or moral hazard: apply screening menus or informativeness-principle reward design (Lesson 3.5). (4) Check for multiplicity — folk-theorem multiplicity means you need equilibrium selection: commitment, coordination devices, or shaped rewards. (5) Validate experimentally — lab-test the mechanism with human or agent subjects before deployment (Lesson 3.3), and check the behavioral assumptions — are your agents (or users) present-biased, loss-averse, fairness-sensitive? (Lesson 3.4). Most "AI alignment" failures in multi-agent settings are mechanism-design failures with better branding.

Worked examples

Example 1: Simulating GSP vs. VCG in sponsored search

Two ad slots with click-through rates 0.10 (top) and 0.04; three advertisers with per-click values $5, $3, $2. Under GSP ranked by bid: suppose equilibrium bids are $5, $3, $2 (truthful for illustration). Advertiser 1 gets slot 1, pays ≈ $3/click (the bid needed to beat advertiser 2); advertiser 2 gets slot 2, pays $2. Revenue per impression: 0.10×3 + 0.04×2 = $0.38. Under VCG, each pays the externality imposed: advertiser 1's presence demotes 2 and 3 one slot each, so 1 pays (0.10−0.04)×3 + (0.04−0)×2 = $0.26 per impression... wait — VCG charges bidder 1 the welfare others lose: without 1, bidder 2 gets slot 1 (0.10×3 = 0.30) and bidder 3 gets slot 2 (0.04×2 = 0.08); with 1, others get 0.04×3 + 0 = 0.12. Externality = 0.38 − 0.12 = $0.26. Truthful bidding is dominant under VCG but not GSP — yet GSP's simplicity won historically. The code below computes both; extend it with bid shading to see GSP's equilibrium bids fall below values.

ctrs = [0.10, 0.04]
vals = [5.0, 3.0, 2.0]  # per-click values, advertisers 1..3
# GSP with truthful bids: rank by bid, pay next bid
order = sorted(range(3), key=lambda i: vals[i], reverse=True)
gsp_rev = sum(ctrs[s] * vals[order[s + 1]] for s in range(2))
# VCG: each winner pays externality imposed on others
def welfare(assign):  # assign: advertiser -> slot or None
    return sum(ctrs[slot] * vals[i] for i, slot in assign.items())
vcg_pay = {}
full = {order[0]: 0, order[1]: 1}
for s, i in enumerate(order[:2]):
    without = {j: t for t, j in enumerate([x for x in order[1:] if x != i][:2])}
    # recompute optimal assignment excluding i
    rest = [x for x in order if x != i]
    alt = {rest[0]: 0, rest[1]: 1}
    vcg_pay[i] = welfare(alt) - (welfare(full) - ctrs[s] * vals[i])
print(f"GSP revenue/impression: ${gsp_rev:.3f}")
print("VCG payments:", {f"adv{i+1}": round(p, 3) for i, p in vcg_pay.items()})

Example 2: Deferred acceptance for GPU-cluster time

Three research teams (A, B, C) need one week each on a scarce GPU cluster; the scheduler has three weeks with different desirability (week 1: conference deadline — everyone wants it). Teams' preferences over weeks: A: 1≻2≻3; B: 1≻3≻2; C: 2≻1≻3. The scheduler (representing "weeks") has priority orderings, e.g., week 1 prioritizes A≻B≻C (grant seniority). Deferred acceptance with teams proposing: Round 1: A,B propose to week 1; C to week 2. Week 1 holds A (highest priority), rejects B; week 2 holds C. Round 2: B proposes to week 3 (next choice), held. Result: A→1, C→2, B→3 — stable (no team–week blocking pair: B would prefer week 1 but week 1 prefers A). Strategy-proof for teams: B cannot get week 1 by lying (week 1's priority is fixed and A outranks B). Replace "weeks" with "reviewers," "drivers," or "spectrum bands" — the algorithm is identical. This is how you fairly allocate any scarce, preference-laden resource without prices.

Example 3: Fixing a MARL tragedy of the commons

Ten RL agents share a compute cluster; each agent's reward is its own job throughput. Each learns to submit jobs aggressively (large batches, no backoff) — the learned equilibrium is mutual congestion: throughput per agent collapses, though all would prefer coordinated restraint. Diagnosis via the template: (1) the game is a prisoner's dilemma (defect = flood, cooperate = backoff); (2) mutual flooding is the Nash equilibrium — more training won't fix it; (3) actions are partially hidden (agents can't see others' queues) → moral hazard; (4) repeated interaction gives folk-theorem multiplicity. Fixes: (a) contract-theoretic rewards — each agent's reward = own throughput − λ×(measured congestion contribution), where the congestion signal passes the informativeness test (per-agent queue impact estimated from scheduler logs, not raw submission counts which are gameable); (b) a coordinator commits to a congestion-pricing schedule first (Stackelberg) — agents best-respond to prices rather than to each other; (c) validate in a simulated lab market (Lesson 3.3) before deploying the pricing to the real cluster. The reward is the mechanism; design it like one.

Example 4: CBRS spectrum sharing as mechanism design

The FCC's Citizens Broadband Radio Service (3.5 GHz) has three tiers: incumbents (naval radar), Priority Access Licenses (PAL, auctioned), and General Authorized Access (GAA, unlicensed-style). This is a mechanism-design stack: the PAL auction (a simultaneous multiple-round format) allocates paid priority using bids to reveal value; GAA is a commons managed by a Spectrum Access System (SAS) — essentially an automated etiquette protocol of the kind Lesson 3.2's spectrum example called for, turning the prisoner's dilemma into a coordinated game by changing the stage-game payoffs (the SAS assigns channels and power limits, removing the "defect" action). Incumbent protection is handled by exclusion zones and dynamic sensing. The design insight: use auctions where values differ and willingness-to-pay should decide (PAL), and use engineered coordination (SAS rules) where transaction costs would swamp auction gains (GAA). Matching the institution to the friction — prices for heterogeneity, protocols for commons problems — is the applied moral of Part 3.

Key results to remember

  • GSP (pay next-ranked bid) is simple but not truthful; VCG (pay externality imposed) is truthful but complex — platforms' move to first-price reflects violated revenue-equivalence assumptions (affiliation, asymmetry) plus ML-estimated reserves.
  • Deferred acceptance (Gale–Shapley) yields a stable matching, proposer-optimal and strategy-proof for proposers — the default algorithm for assignment without prices.
  • MARL failures are usually equilibrium problems, not learning problems: if bad behavior is a Nash equilibrium of the reward game, redesign rewards (contracts), don't just train longer.
  • Reward design = contract design: condition on informative, hard-to-game signals (informativeness principle); use mechanism layers (VCG payments) and commitment (Stackelberg) for equilibrium selection.
  • Spectrum auctions (SMRA, package bidding, the incentive auction's reverse+forward design) show mechanism design allocating tens of billions under complementarities and common values.
  • Match the institution to the friction: auctions for heterogeneous values, engineered protocols (etiquette/SAS) for commons problems, matching algorithms for two-sided preferences without prices.
  • Diagnostic template: write the game → find the equilibrium → check information structure → check multiplicity → validate experimentally → check behavioral assumptions.

Check your understanding

  1. In the GSP example, advertiser 1 (value $5) considers bidding $2.01 instead of $5. Would it still win slot 1, and what would it pay? What does this say about GSP truthfulness?
    Answer

    Yes — $2.01 still beats advertiser 2's $3? No: $2.01 < $3, so advertiser 1 drops to slot 2, paying $2/click for CTR 0.04: payoff 0.04×(5−2) = $0.12 vs. truthful 0.10×(5−3) = $0.20. Shading that far loses money here, but the point stands: in GSP your payment depends on others' bids relative to yours, so optimal bids shade below value in general (bid the minimum needed to hold position given beliefs). Truth-telling is not dominant — unlike VCG, where the payment (externality) is independent of your own bid.

  2. A university assigns PhD students to advisors. Both sides have preferences; money can't be used. Which mechanism do you recommend, who should propose, and why?
    Answer

    Deferred acceptance with students proposing: it yields a stable matching (no student–advisor pair that would defect), is student-optimal among stable matchings, and is strategy-proof for students — they can report true preferences safely. Have advisors propose only if you want the advisor-optimal stable matching; the proposing side gets the favorable end, so propose with the side whose welfare/strategy-proofness you prioritize (usually the students).

  3. Your MARL warehouse robots keep blocking each other's paths (each rushes the narrow aisle). Training longer hasn't helped. Apply the diagnostic template: what is the likely problem and give two mechanism fixes.
    Answer

    Diagnosis: rushing is a Nash equilibrium of the reward game (each robot's reward = own delivery speed; yielding slows itself down) — a chicken/prisoner's-dilemma structure. More training converges to the same bad equilibrium. Fixes: (1) reward redesign per the informativeness principle — penalize measured aisle-congestion contribution (from overhead tracking, hard to game) rather than raw speed; (2) a coordinator protocol with commitment: a traffic manager assigns aisle time-slots (Stackelberg — coordinator moves first), or a VCG-style priority auction where robots bid delay-costs for right-of-way, making truthful urgency revelation dominant.

  4. Why might a first-price ad auction with ML-estimated bidder-specific reserves raise more revenue than the second-price auction it replaced, even though revenue equivalence says formats don't matter?
    Answer

    Revenue equivalence needs IPV + symmetry + risk neutrality; ad markets violate all three (affiliated/common-value components, asymmetric bidders with different data, budget constraints). Given the violations, the format matters, and the real gain is Myersonian: ML-estimated value distributions let the platform set bidder-specific optimal reserves (virtual-valuation logic), excluding low-virtual-value bids — something the old uniform second-price design didn't do. The format change is the vehicle; the reserves are the engine.
