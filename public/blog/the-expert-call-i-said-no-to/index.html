The Expert Call I Said No To

P.Eng. — 24+ years, safety-critical energy systems
2026-11-19 · Knowledge & Advisory
They offered good money for an hour of my expertise — in a field that is not mine. Saying no protected the yes that counts: judgment is only worth buying inside the lane where it was earned.

The request came back three times in three days.
Each time, it was polite. Each time, it was clear. Someone wanted to pay well for an hour of my time, on a call, to talk about one subject: how to inspect large storage tanks on a schedule based on risk.
It was good money for one hour. It would have been easy to say yes.
I said no. That short word was the most valuable work I did that week.

Adjacent is not the same as mine

Here is the trap. The subject was not random. It lives in the same industrial world I have worked in for more than twenty-four years. I have walked past tanks like those. I know the plants they sit in. I know the language people use around them.
That is exactly what made it dangerous.
There is a kind of knowledge that sits next to your real knowledge. It feels familiar. You can talk about it for a while and sound right. You know the words. You know the setting. What you do not have is the deep, daily, hands-on judgment that comes from doing that work for years.
Risk-based inspection is its own discipline. It belongs to people who live in mechanical integrity — people who think every day about metal, corrosion, failure, and when a vessel must be opened and checked. That is honest, skilled work. It is not my work.
My work is electrical. Motors. Drives. Power systems. Controls. The machines that turn, the power that feeds them, and the systems that keep them safe. That is the ground I have stood on for decades. That is where my judgment was earned, one real decision at a time.
An hour on a call does not sound like a big claim. But that is what it is. When someone pays for an expert hour, they are not buying small talk. They are buying judgment they plan to use. If the judgment is borrowed from the field next door, the fee does not make it real.

The question I asked before the answer

One of my agents does the first pass over requests like this. The agent reads each one, checks it against my record, and sorts it: clearly in my lane, clearly out, or borderline. This one landed in the middle pile. Borderline. Plant work, yes. My discipline, no.
So it came to me as a short brief, not as a pile of messages. That matters. My time is the scarcest thing I own, and I protect it on purpose. I automate the volume so that what reaches me is only the part that needs a human decision.
The decision took one line. The inspection topic is not for me. Say no.
No long debate. No “maybe I could prepare.” No quiet thought that an hour is an hour and money is money. The answer was no, and it was final. The request was declined with a plain, honest reason: I do not have the relevant expertise. That sentence was true, and it cost nothing to say — except the fee, which was never really mine to take.
Then we wrote the rule down, so the question would never reach me again. That whole topic family now screens out at the first pass, without asking. A good “no” should not have to be said twice. The first time, it is a decision. After that, it should become a system.

The yes that came the same day

The same day, a different request was waiting.
This one asked about industrial motor controls and power distribution — in my own country, in the market I know. How these systems are bought. How they are specified. Who supplies them, who installs them, and what makes one provider win over another.
That one was marked a strong fit, and it was. I read it and thought: this is mine. Not adjacent. Not familiar-sounding. Mine — the daily work of my career, asked in plain terms.
I said yes to that one.
Put the two answers side by side and the whole lesson is standing there. The no and the yes came from the same rule. I was not being difficult in the morning and generous in the afternoon. I was doing one thing both times: telling the truth about where my judgment lives.
And notice what the no did for the yes. A yes means something only when it is not given to everything. If I take calls outside my field, my yes inside my field stops being a promise. It becomes a habit. People pay for judgment because they trust the person to know its limits. The fence is not separate from the field. The fence is what makes the field worth paying for.

Why this is hard, and why it matters

Saying no to paid work feels wrong to many people. It felt like leaving money on the table. A younger version of me might have taken the call, prepared hard, and hoped that effort could stand in for experience.
Effort cannot do that. Preparation can teach you the shape of a subject in a weekend. It cannot give you the years of small, hard lessons that tell you which detail matters and which one can wait. On a live call, with real questions, that gap shows — usually at the exact moment someone is about to act on your answer.
There is a second cost, quieter than the fee. Every hour spent pretending at the edge of your field is an hour taken from the centre of it. I have said this to my own team in plain words: I am not paid for giving my time. I am paid for my judgment. That is why I have to be selective. That is why my time has to stay in my hands, free for the work only I can do — not pulled in every direction by any request that arrives with a price on it.
Exclusivity sounds like a luxury. It is not. It is how judgment stays sharp enough to be worth buying.

A check you can run today

You do not need my exact situation to use this. Any expert, consultant, or senior practitioner gets the same kind of requests: close enough to sound plausible, far enough to be a lie. The small script shipped with this post does the first pass on a request, using a lane list you write yourself:
Before you accept paid work outside your daily practice, ask:
1. Is this the work I actually do — this year, with my own hands and my own name?
2. If the questions get hard, will my answers come from years of doing it — or from a weekend of preparing?
3. Would I give the same answer if there were no fee at all?
If the first two answers are not a clear yes, the third question usually answers itself.
Write your lane down before the next request arrives, not while you are looking at its fee. A list made in calm weather is honest. A list made while money is on the table tends to grow.

What the no bought

The declined call is gone. I do not think about it, and I do not miss the fee.
What stayed is more useful. A rule that now runs without me. A first pass that protects my hours before they are spent. And a yes — the one I gave the same day, in my true field — that I can stand behind completely, because the person who receives it is getting the real thing.
Expertise is a fence as much as a field. The field is what you know. The fence is what you refuse to pretend to know. Keep both in good repair, and the word people are really buying — your judgment — stays worth its price.
