Two copies of the same manual that weren't

Two copies of the same manual sat side by side on my screen. Same name. Same cover. Same seventeen chapters, in the same order.
One of them was lying. Quietly, in two chapters out of seventeen.
I did not know that yet. At that moment, they looked like the easiest decision in a long week of hard ones: keep one copy, delete the other, and move on. Every library has duplicates. Finding two is not a problem. It is housekeeping.
Except housekeeping, done carelessly, is how a library loses the one chapter it will need most.

A manual worth keeping twice over

The manual was a transformer manual — a full set, published by a public engineering board, covering the life of a transformer from the day it arrives to the day it is tested, erected, and kept alive. Contents. General guidance. Erection, maintenance, and commissioning. A test manual. Notes on losses. Specifications for bushings, for instrument transformers up to 420 kV, for earthing, fire protection, valves, and voltage control.
If you work with transformers, this is not decoration. This is the book you reach for when a decision has weight — sometimes literally, in tonnes, and always in consequences if you get it wrong.
The set had reached me twice, by two different doors, years apart — once in one inbox, once in another. That is how real libraries grow. A colleague shares a set. You save it. Later, someone shares it again, or you send it to yourself so it will be where you need it. Time passes. The second copy settles in beside the first, wearing the same name, and nobody remembers which one came first.
Both copies passed their first checks. Both opened clean. Both held seventeen members, with the same titles in the same order. By every quick test, they were the same manual.
Quick tests are where this story could have ended — badly.

The small clue in the totals

There was one thing that did not fit, and it was small enough to wave away.
The two copies were not quite the same size. The difference was about fifty thousand bytes — a rounding error, you might say, in a set of more than six million. Less than one percent.
I have learned to be careful with clues that size. In engineering, the difference that looks too small to matter is often the only honest signal you get before something larger goes wrong. A vibration slightly above the line. A temperature slightly off the pattern. A total that should match, and does not.
Fifty thousand bytes is not noise in a set of documents. Documents do not change size by accident. Something inside one of those copies was different. The only question was what — and whether it mattered.
So we did not delete anything. We opened both sets, and we compared them properly: member by member, chapter by chapter, each one measured and fingerprinted, so that "same" would mean proven same, not looks same.
same name → a claim same chapter list → a hint same fingerprint → the same file a different one → a question worth answering

Fifteen yeses, and then two nos

The comparison came back in a pattern I did not expect.
Fifteen of the seventeen members matched exactly — the same size, the same fingerprint, byte for byte. Contents, general guidance, erection and maintenance, losses, most of the specifications: identical twins, proven so.
Then came the two that were not.
The specification for transformer bushings — the parts that carry high voltage safely through the tank wall, up to 420 kV class — was different in each copy. Not slightly. One version was near forty thousand bytes larger than the other. Same title. Same place in the set. Different substance.
And the test manual — the chapter that tells you how to prove a transformer is fit before you trust it — was different too, by about fifty thousand bytes.
Two chapters, quietly different. Not damaged. Not corrupt. Different revisions. Somewhere between the day the first copy reached me and the day the second one did, the manual had been revised, and the revision had travelled under exactly the same name as the original. There was one more small tell: one copy kept its chapters loose, in a flat list, while the other wrapped them inside a folder. Same family. Different packaging.
Now think about what the easy decision would have done.
Keep one, delete the other — a coin toss. Heads, I keep the newer test manual and lose nothing. Tails, I delete the newer bushing specification and keep the old one, believing I hold the current word on a part that works at 420,000 volts. And I would never know. The name would still say the right thing. The chapter list would still look complete. The loss would be silent, and it would sit in my library like a fact, waiting for the day someone trusted it.
That is the real danger of a duplicate. It is not clutter. It is the feeling of certainty, arriving before the proof.

Both stay

So both copies stayed.
Each one was kept whole, labelled for what it is, with the difference written down beside it: fifteen members identical, two members different revisions, and which copy holds which version. Nothing was merged. Nothing was tidied away. A merged set — newest of this, oldest of that, under one familiar name — would have been the worst outcome of all: a manual that never existed, assembled by my own housekeeping.
This is slower than deleting. It asks you to keep two of something in a world that praises a clean shelf. But a library is not a shelf. A library is a record of what you know and how you know it. When two copies disagree, the disagreement itself is knowledge. Delete one copy, and you do not just lose a chapter. You lose the knowledge that there was ever a question.
Anyone who keeps standards, codes, or manuals for a living has lived a version of this. A standard is reissued. The new edition lands in the same folder as the old, under the same name, and for a while both circulate. Work done to the old revision is not wrong because someone was careless. It is wrong because a file looked familiar, and familiar felt like current.
The defence is not memory. Nobody’s memory survives years of shared files. The defence is comparison — done member by member, on the day the second copy arrives, with the result written down where the next person will find it.

What I keep from this

"Duplicate" is a conclusion you earn, not a guess you make from a name.
Two files can share a name, a chapter list, and fifteen of their seventeen parts — and still not be the same book. The part that differs may be small. It may also be the part that matters most: the specification, the test. In my trade, those are exactly the chapters you cannot afford to lose quietly.
So now the rule in my library is plain. When a second copy arrives, it is compared before it is judged. Identical copies can be recorded as duplicates and set aside with a clear conscience. Copies that differ are both kept, labelled, and never silently thinned to one.
It costs a little storage. It costs a little time.
It is cheap insurance against the day the manual is opened in earnest — and the chapter inside is not the one you thought you kept.
