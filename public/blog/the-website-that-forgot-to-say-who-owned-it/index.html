The Website That Forgot to Say Who Owned It

The rejection was short, polite, and hard to argue with.
I had applied to a well-known startup program. The form asked for my company and my website, and I gave both, carefully. Days later the answer came back: the website does not meet the qualifications. It does not reflect the company name provided.
I read that line twice. Then I opened my own website the way a stranger would — no memory, no shortcuts, just the front door — and started looking for my company.
It was not in the footer. It was not on the About page. It was not on the Contact page. It was not in the Privacy Policy. My name was there. My work was there. Pages and pages of work, in fact. The one thing that was nowhere was the legal company that owned all of it.
The reviewer had done exactly what any careful person would do. They took the name I gave them, went to the address I gave them, and checked whether the two belonged together. On my own site, they did not.

The most embarrassing kind of failure

There is a special sting in being rejected for a reason that is true.
If the answer had been about the quality of the work, I could have argued, or improved, or tried again with better evidence. There was nothing to argue here. A company that asks to be trusted could not answer the simplest question a website can be asked: whose is this?
And the deeper I looked, the clearer the real problem became. It was not a missing sentence. Sentences are easy. The problem was that no standard existed. Nobody — meaning me — had ever written down where a company’s name belongs on its own sites. Each page had been built well, one at a time, for its own purpose. Nobody had ever stood back and asked what all of them owed a stranger, together.
That is how most credibility fails. Not with a dramatic error. With a small, ordinary piece of housekeeping that was never assigned to anyone, and so was never done.

What a stranger actually checks

Think of the last time you landed on a company website and tried to decide whether to trust it. You did not read every page. You did what reviewers, customers, and partners all do.
You scrolled to the bottom, looking for a real name and a real place. You opened the About page, looking for who stands behind the work. You checked the Contact page, looking for a door a real person could knock on. And if you were careful, you opened the Privacy Policy, because a company that handles your information should be willing to say, in writing, who it is when it does so.
Four places. A footer on every page. An About page. A Contact page. A Privacy Policy. That is the whole test, and it takes a stranger about a minute to run it.
My site failed all four — not because the company was hidden on purpose, but because the test had never been written down. A test that is never written down is a test you will eventually fail in public, in front of exactly the person you hoped to convince.

The fix took an evening. The standard is the point.

The repair itself was simple, and I will not dress it up. The legal name of the company went into the footer of every page. It went onto the About page, in plain words. It went onto the Contact page. It went into the Privacy Policy. Then the same four checks were run on the next site, and the next, because a standard that covers one site and forgets the rest is not a standard. It is a patch.
What stayed with me was not the fix. It was how cheap the whole lesson was, and how expensive it could have been. One sentence, repeated in four honest places, was the difference between a website that describes work and a website that can be believed. The application that failed could be filed again once the house was in order. The next stranger might not have sent a rejection at all. They might simply have left, and I would never have known why.
There is a quiet rule in this for anyone who builds in public. Do the housekeeping before anyone asks. The time to write your name on your own door is not when an important visitor is standing outside it, reading the rejection they are about to send you. It is on an ordinary day, when nothing is at stake, and the work feels too small to matter.

A four-place check you can run today

You do not need a consultant for this. You need ten minutes and the eyes of a stranger. Open your own site in a private window, forget everything you know about it, and ask one question: if I gave a stranger my company’s name and this address, could they prove the two belong together without asking me?
Before you cite your website anywhere:
1. Footer, every page: the legal company name, as registered.
2. About: the same name, in the first screen, in plain words.
3. Contact: the same name beside the way to reach you.
4. Privacy Policy: the same name as the party responsible.
One name. Four places. Word for word the same. Then open the site signed out, and read it as a stranger.
If any one of the four is missing, fix it before your next application, proposal, or introduction — not after. And write the check down where your future self will find it, because the next site you build will forget exactly what this one forgot, unless the standard is waiting for it.

Why this small thing carries so much weight

A name in a footer looks like decoration. It is not. It is a claim that can be checked, made where anyone can check it. That is what trust is made of — not grand promises, but small claims, stated plainly, that turn out to be true when someone tests them.
Every builder I know has a version of this story. The portfolio that never said the work was for sale. The product page that never said what the product costs. The expert whose site never said what he is expert in. In each case the work was real. In each case the housekeeping was missing. And in each case a stranger made a fair decision with the only evidence we gave them: our own silence.
Your website will be checked by people who owe you nothing and will tell you nothing. Most of them will never write to explain why they left. The rejection I received was, in that sense, a gift — one of the rare times a stranger showed me exactly what my own house looked like from the street.
So I fixed the house, wrote the standard down, and put my company’s name where it should have been from the first day: on the door, in the open, where anyone can read it.
Credibility is mostly housekeeping done before anyone asks. Do yours on an ordinary day, while it is still cheap.
