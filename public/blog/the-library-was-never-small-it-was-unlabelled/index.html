I said, "There must be five hundred gigabytes of material. Not less."
It sounded like a guess. The kind of number you say about your own shelves because you have lived with them for years, and you can feel their weight even when you cannot prove it.
Then we measured.
I had underestimated my own library.
Not by a little. By a kind of error I did not expect: I had been counting only the part I could see. The rest was there all along. It was just wearing the wrong names — or no useful name at all.

The books with numbers for names

Every long career leaves a trail like this.
A book arrives as a file named with numbers. A manual is saved in a hurry, under whatever name it came with. A set of chapters lands in a folder that made sense for one week, years ago, and never made sense again. Nothing is lost. Nothing is careless, exactly. It is just labelled for the moment it arrived, not for the day you will need it again.
Over twenty-four years, those moments add up.
So when I finally asked a simple question — what do I actually have? — a search by filename could not answer it. Searching names only finds the things that were named well. The numbered books stay silent. The badly named manuals stay hidden. A search can come back empty while the book sits right there, a few folders away, telling the truth to nobody.
That is the trap. An empty search feels like an answer. It feels like proof that something is missing.
It is not proof. It is only proof that the label failed.

Read the book, not the label

So we changed the question.
Instead of asking, "What is this file called?", we asked, "What is this file?"
File by file, the work went through the drives I already own and identified each item by its own content: the title hidden in its own metadata, the words on its first pages, the author line it carries inside itself. Where a file had no readable title at all, it was not guessed at. It was written down, honestly, as unidentified.
It is slow work. It is not clever work. It is the opposite of clever: open, read, record, move to the next one.
And then the library I thought I knew started to change shape.
Books I believed were gone were there. Sets I thought were fragments were complete, scattered under plain numbers. Whole shelves appeared that no filename search had ever returned, because no filename on them contained the words I had been searching for.
The library had never been small. My map of it had been small.

The count that settled it

When the counting was done, the numbers were plain enough to check, and large enough to change the plan.
Documents alone — just the document class, before anything else was counted — came to 149,708 files, and 377.6 gigabytes.
That was only the first line.
Archives added another 480.9 gigabytes. Media added 594.3 gigabytes. Model weights added 374.9 gigabytes. Each class counted on its own, by what the files actually were, not by what their folders happened to be called.
My "five hundred gigabytes, not less" had not been wrong in spirit. It had been wrong in scale. I had described the visible part of the library and called it the library.
There is a lesson in that for anyone who keeps serious material — standards, manuals, drawings, reports, a lifetime of reference work. We tend to trust our memory of our own shelves. Memory is a good witness to effort. It is a poor witness to inventory. It remembers the famous books. It forgets the numbered ones, and the numbered ones are most of the shelf.

The honest remainder

The count also had a part I am just as glad we wrote down.
After all the content reading, 8,821 files stayed unidentified.
They were scanned pages with no text layer inside them — pictures of pages, not text a machine can read. They could be anything. A few are probably important. Most are probably ordinary. We did not guess.
They were listed as what they are: an unidentified remainder, waiting for a different kind of reading — the slow work that turns a picture of a page back into words — before anyone claims to know what they hold.
That line matters more than the big numbers.
A count that hides its remainder is not a count. It is a sales pitch. If I tell you I found everything, and I quietly leave out the 8,821 files I could not read, then the first number — 149,708 — stops being a fact and becomes a story I am telling myself. The honest version is less tidy and far more useful: this much was identified, by this method, and this much was not, for this reason.
Wealth you cannot name is hard to use. But a gap you can name is work you can plan.

What changed after the count

Nothing was bought. Nothing new was collected. The change was not in what I owned. It was in what I could find, prove, and use.
A book you cannot find might as well belong to someone else. A book you can find in a minute, and prove is the edition you think it is, is working capital. The difference between those two states is not money. It is labelling — patient, unglamorous labelling, done by reading the thing itself.
That is true well beyond one man’s drives.
Most teams I have met do not have a knowledge problem first. They have a finding problem. The report exists. The standard exists. The answer was written, once, by someone who knew. It sits in a shared drive under a name that meant something to the person who saved it, on the day they saved it, and to no one since. Then a new question arrives, the search comes back empty, and the work gets done again from scratch — slower, and sometimes worse than the answer already on the shelf.
"We don’t have it" is an expensive sentence. Often it is false. Often the true sentence is smaller and more fixable: "We can’t find it, because we never labelled it by what it is."

The rule I keep now

Now the rule in my library is plain.
Identify a thing by its content, not by its name. Keep the count honest, including the part you could not identify. And when someone who knows the shelf says, "It’s in there" — believe the scale of that claim, even while you distrust every label on the way to proving it.
I was right that the material was there. I was wrong about how much. Both halves of that sentence were worth learning, and neither half could be learned from filenames.
The library was never small.
It was unlabelled. There is a difference — and the difference, once you can see it, is work you can actually do.
