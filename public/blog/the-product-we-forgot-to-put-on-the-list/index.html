The Product We Forgot to Put on the List

P.Eng. — 24+ years, safety-critical energy systems
2026-11-10 · Field Notes with Muse
The brief said all our products. It went out with seven. The eighth was live, built, and ready — and missing from the page entirely. A list built from memory had forgotten it; a list built from the records could not.

The brief said "all our products." It went out clean, confident, and complete — with seven products on it.
Then I read it again and asked one question.
Where was the eighth?
It was not a small product. It was not an idea in a drawer. It was live. It had machinery of its own, built and waiting — a server other systems could talk to, and a packet prepared so a new platform could connect to it. People could have found it, used it, paid for it.
My assistant had left it off the list entirely. Not ranked low. Not set aside. Missing.
I found the gap. My assistant did not. That order matters, and it is why this story is worth telling.

The list I trusted

Here is how a list like that gets built, in my house and probably in yours.
Someone who knows the work sits down and writes down the products. The knowledge is real. Most of the list is right. The writing is quick because the writer has carried these names in his head for months. He has built them, fixed them, talked about them. Of course he knows them all.
Except memory is not a list. Memory is a story we tell about the list, and stories leave things out without telling us.
My assistant wrote the market brief from memory. Then it wrote the customer brief, also from memory. The same product was missing from both. That is the detail I keep coming back to. One omission could be a slip. The same omission, twice, in two different documents, is a method. The method was: trust what you remember, and write from there.
Nothing in either brief looked wrong. That is the hard part. A missing item does not leave a hole in the page. The page closes over it, smooth and tidy, the way water closes over a stone. Seven products read exactly like a complete list of seven products — because on the page, that is what it was.
My question, when I sent it back, was not gentle, and I will keep it as I wrote it:
You have completely discounted and not even developed it?
I had developed it. That was the sting. Work I had paid for, in time and in attention, was sitting live and ready — and in the two documents meant to take my work to the world, it did not exist.

What a missing product actually costs

It is easy to call this a typo-class mistake. It is not. A brief is not a diary. Other work is built on top of it.
The market brief decides where we look. The customer brief decides who we talk to. If a product is missing from both, then no market is mapped for it, no customer is listed for it, no effort is spent on it. It does not get a smaller share of the work. It gets none. And because the briefs looked complete, nobody downstream would ever ask about it. The omission would have defended itself, quietly, for months.
This is how good work dies in a growing house. Not in a fire. Not in an argument. In a list that nobody checked against the truth.
I have seen the same shape in engineering, long before I had an assistant to make the mistake for me. A drawing set goes out with one enclosure missing from the index. A test plan covers every relay except the one added last spring. The work that follows is careful, professional, and thorough — about everything except the thing that was never written down. You cannot do good work on an item that is not on your list. You cannot even fail at it. It simply never happens.

The fix was a list, not an apology

My assistant did what assistants do. It apologised, and it offered to add the missing product and send the briefs again.
That would have fixed the page. It would not have fixed the method. The same memory that dropped one product in October would drop another one in December, in a different brief, about a different product, and this time I might not be the one reading closely.
So the fix we set down was structural, and it is simple enough to write on a card:
Build the list from the sources, not from memory. Diff the brief against that list. Name every exclusion as a decision, in the brief itself. Only then does the brief go out.
Three things changed.
First, the product list is now built from the records that cannot forget: the place where products are registered to be sold, the ledger where the work is tracked, and the sweep of what has actually been built. If those three disagree, that disagreement is the first finding — before any brief is written.
Second, the brief is checked against that list, item by item. Not read over. Checked. A product that is in the records and not in the brief stops the brief.
Third — and this is the part I care about most — exclusions are allowed, but they must wear their names. Some of my products are set aside on purpose: one is for my own house only, others are paused by my word. Those are decisions, and a decision can be defended. What is not allowed is the silent kind — the product that is absent because nobody remembered it. In the brief, an excluded product appears in a line that says it is excluded, and why. An omission you have to write down stops being an omission. It becomes a choice, and choices can be argued with, improved, and reversed. Silences cannot.
The check is now mechanical. A small script holds the canonical list in one hand and the brief in the other, and it refuses to pass a brief that leaves an active product unmentioned. I do not have to catch the eighth product any more. The list catches it, every time, including the times when everyone in the room is sure.

What I took from it

I no longer ask, "Did we include everything?" That question invites a feeling, and feelings answer yes.
I ask a colder question: "What did you build the list from?" If the answer is a source — a register, a ledger, a sweep — I can check the answer. If the answer is a memory, including a very good memory, including my own, then the list is a draft wearing a finished list’s clothes.
That is the whole lesson, and it travels well beyond my small house of products. Any list that other work depends on — parts, tests, customers, promises, risks — deserves to be built from the place where the truth is kept, and diffed before it is trusted. The cost is an hour. The cost of the other way is a live product, built and ready, that the world never hears about because a tidy page forgot its name.
The eighth product is on the list now. It was always built. Now it is also counted.
A thing that is not counted might as well not exist. So count from the source — and let the list, not memory, have the last word.
