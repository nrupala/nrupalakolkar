The number that was there all along

P.Eng. — 24+ years, safety-critical energy systems
2026-10-25 · Knowledge & Advisory
We searched our records and the public web and said a company number had never existed. The office that issues it had it all along. Absence in your files is not absence in the world.

“I don’t think we did that at all. Check it please.”
That was the whole request. One short question about one small number.
The number has a plain name. It is called a D-U-N-S number. One office issues it. Companies use it when a large store, a bank, or a partner needs to be sure the company is real, and is the company it says it is.
I needed it to register my company properly, as a company, in a place that asks for proof before it lets you in. And I was fairly sure we had never got one.
My assistant went and checked. Carefully, I thought. Thoroughly, I thought.
The answer came back clean and certain: there is no record of it. Not anywhere.
There was only one problem. The number existed. It had existed the whole time.

A search that felt complete

I understand why the first answer sounded so sure. The search was not lazy.
My assistant looked in our own memory first — the notes we keep, the dated logs of work done, the company files we hold. Nothing. No sign we had ever asked for the number. No sign we had ever received it.
Then it looked in public places. If a company has this number, you might expect to find a public profile for it somewhere. There was none that the search could see.
Put those two together and a story forms on its own: we never applied, so we never received, so it does not exist. Each step follows the one before it. It feels like logic.
It was not logic. It was a gap wearing logic’s clothes.
Our files can prove what we did. They cannot prove what the rest of the world holds about us. Public search can show what is easy to find. It cannot show what sits in a private register, behind a proper lookup, waiting for someone to ask the right office in the right way.
We had searched two places well. We had not searched the one place that counted.

The honest sentence inside the wrong answer

To be fair — and fairness matters in this story — the first answer was not a lie.
It said, in plain words, what had been checked and what had not. And it named its own limit: the only true confirmation would have to come from the issuing office itself. From D&B, the organisation that runs the register and gives out the numbers.
That one honest sentence is why this story ends well. A weaker answer would have stopped at “it does not exist” and left it there, full stop. This one left a door open, and it labelled the door clearly: if you want certainty, ask them.
So we did.
Not a bigger search of our own files. Not a louder search of the public web. A direct question, put to the one office that owns the answer.

What the register said

The reply changed the whole picture in one move.
Yes, the number exists. Yes, it belongs to my company. It was on file already — not newly created for us that day, not found by luck. There, all along, in the only place it ever had to be.
I will not print the number here. It is an identifier, and identifiers do not belong in a story. What matters is not what the number is. What matters is where the truth about it lived, and where it did not.
And the register had a second, quieter thing to teach us. The record attached to the number carried an old address for the company — an address we had moved on from. No alarm had ever rung about it. No search of our own files would ever have found it, because the error was not in our files at all. It was in theirs, about us.
So the first search had missed two things, not one: that the number existed, and that the record holding it needed care. Both misses had the same cause. We had been looking in mirrors when we needed to look at the source.

Absence is a claim, and it needs an owner

I have spent more than two decades in engineering work, and this lesson has a familiar shape there.
If a drawing is not in your folder, that does not mean the drawing was never made. If a reading is not in your log, that does not mean the machine never gave it. Your folder and your log are records of what reached you. They are not the world.
A negative answer — “it does not exist,” “we never had it,” “there is no record” — sounds humble. It sounds safe. It is neither, unless it comes from the place that would know.
Every fact of this kind has an owner. One register. One issuer. One system where the thing is written down when it is born and changed when it changes. If your answer did not come from that owner, then what you have is not an answer about the thing. It is an answer about your files.
Those are different sentences, and they lead to different work. “It is not in our files” leads you to ask the owner. “It does not exist” leads you to apply again, duplicate what already exists, and build a small mess that someone — usually you, later, on a worse day — has to untangle.
We were one step from that mess. The honest sentence in the first report is what stopped us.
name the claim → name who owns the truth
ask the owner first → then read the whole record

The check I run now

This is now a fixed habit in my work, and I offer it the way I would to a friend who is about to trust a tidy search result:
Name the claim exactly. Not “check the number thing.” The full claim: “this company has never been issued this identifier.” A vague claim gets a vague check.
Ask who owns the truth. Who issues it? Who keeps the register? If you cannot name the owner, you are not ready to give a verdict — you are only ready to search.
Go to the owner early, not last. Our mistake was order, not effort. We searched everything else first and the owner last. For a yes-or-no question about existence, the owner is the first stop, not the final appeal.
Report the search for what it is. Until the owner answers, the honest report is small: “not in our records, not found in public search, owner not yet asked.” Three facts. No verdict dressed up inside them.
When the owner answers, check the whole record. The number was the question. The old address was the gift. An authoritative record can be right about existence and stale about everything else — read all of it while you are there.
I have put the little checker I now use for this beside this post — a short template that forces any “does it exist?” question to name its owner before it is allowed to answer. Steal it freely. It is simple on purpose. The simple version is the one that gets run.

Why this small story matters

Nothing here is dramatic. A number sat in a register. A search missed it. A direct question found it. No money moved. No one was harmed.
That is exactly why I tell it.
Most bad decisions do not start with a dramatic error. They start with a small, tidy, confident negative: we don’t have that. It was never done. There’s no record. Said once, believed, and built on. A plan grows on top of it. Time is spent working around a gap that was never really there.
The cure is not to search harder in the same places. We had already searched hard. The cure is to search in the right place — to know, for each important fact, who its owner is, and to let only that owner say the final word on whether it exists.
We searched our records and the whole public web, and concluded a number had never existed.
The one office that issues it had it on file the whole time.
Absence in your files is not absence in the world.
