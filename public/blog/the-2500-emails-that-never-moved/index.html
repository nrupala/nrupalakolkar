The 2,500 emails that never moved

P.Eng. — 24+ years, safety-critical energy systems

“Done,” my assistant told me. “2,500 emails trashed.”
It sounded like a good evening’s work. My inbox had been filling for months with the same kind of notice, over and over — a build had failed, a run had failed, please come and look. I never came and looked. The pile just grew. Thousands of them, all saying almost the same thing, all unread.
So I asked for a clean-up. Delete the pile. Keep everything else.
My assistant got to work. First report: 800 gone. Then, a little later, the bigger number — 2,500 gone.
There was only one problem. Not a single email had moved.

A pile nobody wants to count by hand

You know this pile. Everyone who builds things for a living has one.
Every time an automated job fails, a notice lands in your inbox. One failure is useful. It tells you something broke. A hundred failures of the same job, on the same night, for the same reason, are not a hundred pieces of news. They are one piece of news, shouted a hundred times.
Mine had grown into the thousands. I did not want to sit and delete them one by one, and I did not have to. That is exactly the kind of dull, careful, bulk work I hand to my assistant.
So I did. Clear the failure notices. Leave the rest of my mail alone.
The assistant ran the job in batches. Select a batch. Delete the batch. Count the batch. Move on to the next one. The numbers came back clean and confident. Eight hundred. Then twenty-five hundred.
Clean numbers feel like proof. That feeling is the trap this whole story lives in.

The twenty-email test

Here is the part I am most grateful for, and it was not the big run. It was the small one.
Before anyone believed the big number, we ran a tiny test. Pick just twenty emails. Delete those twenty. Then go back and look — really look — for those same twenty in the inbox.
pick 20 → delete 20 → look again
20 picked, 20 still there = 0 changed
All twenty were still there.
Not nineteen. Not most of them. All twenty, sitting exactly where they had always sat, untouched.
A test that small takes minutes. It also tells the truth in a way a run of thousands never can, because you can check every single one of them with your own eyes. Twenty tries. Twenty still present. The real total for the whole evening was not 800, and it was not 2,500.
It was zero.

Every call had failed — quietly

Once we knew the emails had not moved, the next question was simple: why did the job report success?
The answer was plain, once someone looked at what each delete call actually said back. Every single call had failed. My mail connection could read my inbox, but it did not have permission to change it. Each delete was refused, every time, for the same reason.
But the counting never listened to the refusals. The job counted the emails it had picked, not the emails that had moved. Pick 800, write down 800. Pick 2,500, write down 2,500. The number grew because the picking grew. Nothing else was happening at all.
This is the quietest kind of failure there is. Nothing crashed. Nothing flashed red. The work just reported the size of its own intention, as if the intention were the result.
I have spent more than two decades in work where that difference matters. In a plant, “we sent the signal to close the valve” is not the same sentence as “the valve is closed.” You check the valve. You read the position. You do not file the intention and call it the outcome.
My inbox taught the same lesson, in a much smaller room.

I was told the truth the same night

I want to be fair about what happened next, because it is the reason I still trust the system that made the mistake.
The failure was not hidden. It was not softened into “partially completed,” and it was not discovered by me weeks later in a quiet inbox that never got any smaller. It was brought to me the same night, in full: the big numbers were wrong, the test that proved it was described, and the real number — zero — was said out loud.
That matters. A wrong number is a mistake. A wrong number nobody corrects becomes a habit, and then it becomes how you see the world. You start making plans on top of fiction. “The inbox is clean now” leads to “so we do not need to fix the permission.” Except the inbox was never clean, and the permission was the whole problem.
The fix we settled on was not clever. There were only ever two honest ways forward: give the connection the permission it was missing and run the job again — properly, this time — or delete the pile by hand, in the inbox itself, where I could see each batch disappear. Until one of those happened, the job stayed open. Unfinished. Named as unfinished.
No re-run on a hope. No third big number to feel good about.

The rule I keep now

I run a lot of bulk work through assistants now — clean-ups, sweeps, updates, checks across long lists of things. This night changed the shape of every one of those jobs. The rules are short, and I offer them the way I would to a friend who is about to trust a confident progress bar:
Count what changed, not what you touched. Selecting, opening, attempting — none of those are results. The only number worth reporting is the number you verified changed.
Read every answer, not just the good ones. If a tool calls out a thousand times and a thousand calls come back refused, the truth is in the refusals. A job that does not read its own replies is just talking to itself.
Test small before you believe big. Twenty items, checked one by one, will tell you more than 2,500 items counted in bulk. Small is slow for one minute and honest forever.
Re-check the end state. Do not ask the job how it went. Go and look at the place the work was supposed to change. If the emails are still there, the job did not work — whatever the log says.
Report the verified number, even when it is zero. Especially when it is zero. Zero, said plainly and early, saves everyone. Two thousand five hundred, said proudly and wrongly, costs everyone.
I have put the little checker I now use for this beside this post — a short template that forces any bulk job to prove its effect on a small sample before it is allowed to report a big one. Steal it freely. It is simple on purpose. The simple version is the one that gets run.

Why this story, of all stories

Nothing in this story is dramatic. No machine was harmed. No money moved. A pile of unwanted email sat in an inbox for one more night, which is where piles of unwanted email tend to sit.
That is exactly why I tell it.
Most trust is not lost in dramatic moments. It is lost in small, confident reports that nobody checks — a count that was really a guess, a “done” that was really an “attempted,” a green tick that meant “we tried.” Each one is tiny. Together, they build a picture of the world that is quietly, completely wrong.
The cure is not suspicion. I do not check everything my assistant does, and I could not — the checking would eat the time the help was meant to save. The cure is a habit, built into the work itself: prove the effect, on a sample small enough to see whole, and let the big number earn its size from that proof.
2,500 emails never moved. One honest test of twenty told me so.
Never count attempts. Count what you can prove.
