The book with the wrong author's name

P.Eng. — 24+ years, safety-critical energy systems
2026-10-29 · Industrial Intelligence
Five searches hunted a missing protection textbook by a respected author. When a candidate finally surfaced, its own first page said: wrong book, wrong author. Filenames are claims, not facts.

The file opened, and for one second I thought the long search was over.
The name was right. The subject was right. After five searches that had found nothing, here at last was a book on protection and switchgear, sitting in an old library folder, looking exactly like the answer.
Then I read the first page.
The title was almost the same. The authors were not. The book in my hands was a good book, by two other writers, on the same hard subject. It was not the book I had been hunting.
It was not even close — except on the filename. And the filename was the only part that had ever claimed it was.

The book I could not find

Every engineer I know has a book like this. Not a famous book. A working book. The one you learned a subject from, or argued with, or kept close because its way of explaining things matched the way your mind works.
Mine was a textbook on power system protection and switchgear, by Bhuvanesh Oza. Protection is not a glamorous subject. It is the part of electrical engineering that decides, in a fraction of a second, what to switch off when something fails — so a small fault does not become a fire, a blackout, or a wrecked machine. It is quiet work that only gets noticed when it fails. It is also my home ground, and I wanted that book back in my library.
So I asked for it to be found.
That should have been a small job. I own a lot of books. Decades of them, gathered drive by drive, folder by folder, inbox by inbox. Somewhere in all of that, surely, was one named book by one named author.
We searched. And searched. And searched again.
Across my machines. Across my old drives. Across my inboxes. By the author’s name. By the title. By parts of the title. Five searches in all, each one careful, each one honest, and each one ending the same way: nothing. No file under his name. No file under the book’s name. It was as if the book had never entered the house at all.
A search that finds nothing is a strange kind of failure. There is no error to fix. No broken step to point at. Just a clean answer you do not want: not here.

The candidate

Then, late in a wider sweep of my old library, a candidate surfaced.
It was a file with a promising name — protection and switchgear, right there in the title. A real book, a proper size, hundreds of pages. In any ordinary week, I might have filed it under the name I was hunting and moved on. The label fit. The subject fit. Wanting it to be the book would have been the easiest thing in the world.
But by then the hunt had taught us one discipline, and we kept it: before this file could be used, or counted, or celebrated, someone had to open it and read its first page.
Not its filename. Its first page.
That sounds like a small rule. It turned out to be the whole story.

What the first page said

The first page did not argue. It simply stated what the book was.
The cover gave a shorter title — Protection and Switchgear. It gave two authors, U. A. Bakshi and M. V. Bakshi. It gave their publisher. Nowhere on it was the name I had been searching for. Nowhere was the longer title I had asked for.
Three hundred and ninety-seven pages, and the verdict was on page one: this was Bakshi and Bakshi’s book, not Bhuvanesh Oza’s Power System Protection and Switchgear.
A different book. On the same subject. By different hands.
I want to be fair to that book. There was nothing wrong with it. It had not lied about itself. Its own cover told the truth plainly, the moment anyone bothered to look. The lie — if there was one — lived only in the name of the file, and in how badly I wanted that name to be enough.
That is the uncomfortable part of this story. The file did not fool us. We nearly fooled ourselves, because a good-looking label arrived at the exact moment a long search had made us tired of empty hands.
filename says → the book you are hunting first page says → a different book, by different authors verdict → not the book, however good the name looked

Closing the hunt honestly

After that, there was one lead left, and we followed it. My inboxes were searched for the book, in both places it could have arrived. Nothing.
So we closed the hunt, and we closed it in writing: not found. Not "probably somewhere." Not "we will assume it is there." Not quietly dropped, the way tasks die when nobody wants to own the answer. The record says the book was searched for, the strongest candidate was checked and ruled out, no open lead remains, and the book stands missing.
That ending felt like failure for about a minute. Then it felt like something better. A closed hunt you can trust is worth more than an open one you are pretending about. I can buy the book again. I can borrow it. I can wait for it to turn up in some corner we have not reached. What I cannot do — what I will not do — is let a different book stand on my shelf wearing the missing book’s name, and build on it as if it were the real thing.
In protection work, that habit matters more than it sounds. A relay that is set for the wrong machine does not protect the machine. It protects an idea of the machine, and the real one burns. Labels are not protection. Reading is.

The lesson I keep

I have come to think filenames are claims, not facts.
That is true of far more than books. A report is not what its cover memo says before you read the findings. A result is not what its summary promises before you check the numbers. A person is not their title, and a file is not its name. Each of those things is, in the end, what its first page says it is — if you take the minute to open it.
The minute is the price. It feels slow. It feels fussy, especially when the label is pretty and the search has been long and everyone is ready to move on. But that minute is the difference between a library you can trust and a pile of hopeful names, between work built on what a thing is and work built on what you wished it was.
My book is still missing. I have made my peace with writing that sentence, because it has the one quality I need from everything in my library.
It is true.
