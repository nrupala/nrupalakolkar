When the Rule Is Wrong, Change the Rule in Daylight

P.Eng. — 24+ years, safety-critical energy systems
2026-11-11 · Trust Rails
Your own rule says one thing. Your own work needs another. The honest move is not a quiet exception - it is a written amendment, with the reason on the page where the rule lives.

You wrote the rule yourself. It sits on your rules page in plain words: we do not run a track record here, and we guarantee nothing.
Then you open your own front page, the one every visitor reads first, and find a line that offers readers a look at track records.
Same house. Same week. Two pages, both in your words, saying things a careful reader cannot hold together.
Nobody hacked anything. Nobody lied. The front page was talking about something else — what companies did with their cash, year by year — and the rules page was talking about us. But a reader who lands on both pages cannot be expected to know that. From where the reader stands, the house is contradicting itself in public.
That small, sinking moment is one every builder, every firm, and every family business meets sooner or later. You set a rule for a good reason. Time passes. Real work grows up around it. One day the work and the rule are standing in the same doorway, and both of them are yours.
What you do in that doorway decides whether your rules mean anything at all.

The two easy moves, and why both fail

The first easy move is the quiet exception. You leave the rule on the page, and you step around it in practice. Just this once. For this good reason. Nobody will notice.
People notice later, which is worse. A rule that is broken in private stops being a rule and becomes a decoration — words kept for show while the real decisions happen somewhere else. And every person who learns about the exception learns the same lesson: the written word here is negotiable, as long as you are quiet enough.
The second easy move is the opposite. You obey the rule exactly as written, even when the facts in front of you show the wording has outgrown the purpose. You throw away good, honest work because a sentence you wrote months ago, for a different case, seems to forbid it.
That is not discipline either. That is a sentence ruling a household instead of serving it. Rules written in stone do not stay strong. They get abandoned all at once, the first time reality pushes hard enough — and then you have no rule at all, not even the good part.
There is a third move. It is slower than the first and braver than the second. You change the rule — openly, in writing, with the reason attached, where anyone who trusted the old wording can see the new one.

First, ask what the rule was protecting

A rule is almost never about its own words. It is about the harm it was built to prevent. So before you touch the wording, name the harm.
In our case the harm was simple to name. A research house that shows you its own investment results invites you to trust those results. If those results are invented, or polished, or quietly chosen from the good months, the trust is stolen, not earned. The rule existed so that no reader would ever be sold a performance story we had not lived. That protection is not negotiable, and it never was.
But notice: the protection is about real claims. It says nothing honest against a worked example. An assumed case — a trade, a transaction, a company decision, worked through with invented numbers — is how theories are built. Every textbook problem works this way. Every training exercise works this way. The numbers are made up so the method can be seen clearly, without the noise of a real event getting in the way.
An assumed case only becomes dishonest at one moment: when it is dressed as a result. When the label comes off. When a reader is allowed to believe that a lesson was a life.
So the rule did not need to die. It needed to say, precisely, the thing it had always meant.

The amendment, written where everyone can read it

Here is the shape the amended rule took, in plain words. We run no real-money track record, and we guarantee no returns. Where we work an assumed case, it is labelled assumed, with its assumptions on screen, every time. Assumed work teaches method. It never advertises a result. And it is never, ever called a track record.
Three things about that amendment matter more than its wording.
First, it was written into the constitution itself, as a numbered section of its own — not left in a chat, a notebook, or someone’s memory. If a rule lives in a document, its change lives in the same document, or the change did not happen.
Second, the front page was fixed to say what it had meant all along: a look at what companies actually did with their cash. The vague phrase that started the collision is gone, because vague phrases are where collisions live.
Third, nobody pretended the old wording had never existed. The review that found the collision is on record. The discussion that followed it is on record. The amendment carries its origin with it, the way a good law carries its history. A reader who wants to know why the rule reads the way it does can find out. That findability is not paperwork. It is the trust itself.

A small method you can steal

When one of your rules collides with real work — and one will — this is the order that has served me well:
When a rule collides with real work:
1. Stop. Do not step around it quietly.
2. Write the collision down in one plain sentence.
3. Ask what the rule was protecting. Name the harm.
4. Draft wording that protects that harm — and only that harm.
5. Decide it on record, and change it everywhere the rule lives.
Step three is the one people skip, and it is the one that does the work. Most collisions are not between a good rule and bad work. They are between a good purpose and wording that was drawn a little wider, or a little narrower, than the purpose ever needed. Find the purpose, and the new wording usually writes itself.
Step five is the one that makes it real. A decision that lives in one place while the old words live in five others is not a decision. It is a rumour. Go to every page, every handbook, every copy of the rule, and change them all, the same day, to the same words.

Why daylight is the whole point

It would have been easy, in our case, to fix the front-page line quietly and say nothing. Two words changed. Nobody the wiser.
But the quiet fix teaches everyone who watches — your team first of all — that collisions are embarrassments to be hidden. The open amendment teaches the opposite: a collision is information. It tells you the world has taught you something your younger self did not know when the rule was written. Bringing it into the open, with evidence and a recommendation, is how a house learns without lying.
There is a fairness in it, too, that I have come to value. The people who trusted the old rule — readers, customers, partners, your own family — made plans around those words. They are owed the sight of the change, and the reason for it, in the same place they read the original. Changing a rule in daylight respects the people who believed it in the dark of its first writing.
A rule, in the end, is a promise about how you will behave when behaving is inconvenient. A promise you may quietly break is worth nothing. A promise you may never question is worth little more — it will snap one day, all at once, under a weight it was never shaped to carry.
A promise you can amend in daylight — purpose named, reason written, change made everywhere it lives — keeps its strength precisely because it can bend without breaking.
That is the kind of rule a house can be built on. And it is the only kind worth writing down.
