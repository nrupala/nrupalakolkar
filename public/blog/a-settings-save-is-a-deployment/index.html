The smallest change I know how to make

There is a kind of change that does not feel like a change at all. You open a settings page for a service that is already live. You paste in a new key — the old one is being retired, this is housekeeping. You press Save. No code was written. No release was announced. Nothing, in your mind, was deployed.
I have made that save. I suspect anyone who runs anything on the internet has.
Here is what I learned the slow way: on the platform in question, that Save did more than store the key. Sitting behind it was a newer version of the service that had been uploaded earlier and never put live — tested in part, waiting its turn. The settings save quietly promoted it. Production changed that afternoon, in a way nobody had chosen for that afternoon, and the change was only noticed later, by a watcher whose whole job is to notice exactly that.
Nothing broke. That is precisely why the story is worth telling. A system that only teaches you when it breaks will eventually teach you at the worst possible time.

The lie is in the button

The button said Save, and it told the truth — narrowly. It saved. It also deployed, and about that it said nothing.
Once you see it, you see the pattern everywhere. A settings screen is not a filing cabinet sitting beside the real work. It is a control surface attached to the real work, and some of its controls are wired to more than their labels claim. Treating it as paperwork — quick, informal, beneath ceremony — is a category error. In a plant, nobody would accept "I only adjusted a setting" as a reason a running unit changed behaviour without a record. Software deserves the same respect, because the consequences arrive through the same door: unrecorded change, discovered late, explained with difficulty.

The rule I run now

The rule is simple enough to fit on an index card: any touch on a live service is a deployment, whoever's hands are on the keyboard — mine included.
Two habits hang off it. Before the touch, a short declaration: what is about to change, on which live service, and what else might be waiting behind that save — in particular, whether a newer version has been uploaded but not yet put live, because that is the passenger a settings save can carry. After the touch, a certificate: what the live service actually looks like now, read back from the service itself, not remembered from the intention.
Declaration before, certificate after. The touch itself takes thirty seconds. The wrapper takes a few minutes. Those minutes are the entire price of never again being surprised by your own Save button.

The twin mistake: calling it complete

The same week taught me the companion lesson, and the two belong together. I had been ready to call a release completely deployed — and then someone asked, reasonably, about one surface the release owed its users: a small companion piece that runs in the browser. It had been built. It had never been sent anywhere. It was not on any list I was checking against, which is exactly how it got missed.
That is the pattern in both stories. The failure was never in the work. The failure was in the inventory — a surface nobody had written down, a side effect nobody had written down. Things that are not enumerated do not get checked. Things that do not get checked get discovered by accident, late, or by a customer.
So the second rule joins the first: a release is not called complete until every surface it owes is on one list — the main service, the small companions, the places users actually meet it — and every row on that list is either verified live, by looking, or deliberately set aside on the record. "Mostly deployed" is an honest phrase. "Completely deployed," said over a list with an unchecked row, is not a status. It is a hope wearing a status's clothes.

What it costs, what it buys

The cost is real and I will not pretend otherwise. Small changes get slower. A thirty-second paste becomes a small ceremony. A launch announcement waits until the last unglamorous row on the list is looked at with actual eyes. There are days when that feels like dragging an anchor.
What it buys is a particular kind of quiet: the knowledge that production only ever changes on purpose, on the record, and that when you say a thing is fully out in the world, the sentence has been earned row by row. Trust — in your own releases, first of all — is not built by the big launches going well. It is built by the small saves being treated as what they are.
A settings save is a deployment. Say it before you press it, prove it after you press it, and never let a list you did not write decide what "complete" means.

The artifact

The checker below runs both rules. Feed it two small CSV files: one listing every surface a release owes, with each row marked verified, waived (with who waived it), missing, or unverified; and one listing touches made through a settings screen, with whether each was declared before and certified after. It refuses the word "complete" while any applicable row is missing or unverified and unwaived, and it flags any production touch that skipped its wrapper. It is generic on purpose — bring your own list; writing the list is most of the work.
Download deploy_touch_check.py — the checker itself:
# surfaces.csv surface,applicable,status,waived_by website,yes,verified, mobile app,yes,missing, browser extension,yes,waived,owner 2026-10-22 # touches.csv touch,production,declared_before,certified_after "pasted a new key in settings",yes,no,yes python3 deploy_touch_check.py surfaces.csv touches.csv # FAIL mobile app: status is 'missing' — not verified, not waived # FAIL pasted a new key in settings: production touch with # no declaration before it # Not complete / not clean.
Press Save like it matters — because somewhere behind that button, it does.
