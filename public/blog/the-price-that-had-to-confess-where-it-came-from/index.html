The Price That Had to Confess Where It Came From

The report said the price was $0.00.
Not low. Not down a little. Zero. Free. A large, well-known company, the kind whose shares trade millions of times a day, was apparently worth nothing at all — and my own tool was saying it in clean type, with a dollar sign, as if it were a fact worth acting on.
A few lines further down, the same report told the truth. In plain words it said there was no data from the providers. One part of the page knew we had nothing. The other part had taken that nothing and dressed it up as a price.
That is the moment this story is really about. Not a wrong number. A missing number that refused to admit it was missing.

How a blank became money

We traced it step by step, from what the reader sees back to where it started.
The tool gets its prices from one declared source — a deliberate choice, made long before this night. On this night, that source would not answer us. The route to it was blocked by the kind of automated challenge that lets a person through and stops a program at the door. No malice in it. Just a closed gate. The request came back empty. No prices. No rows. Nothing.
Here is where the quiet fault lived. The code had been written as if a price always exists. When nothing arrived, it did not stop and say so. It reached for its default — and its default was zero. Zero flowed through the system exactly like a real price, until a formatter put a dollar sign in front of it and two neat decimals behind it.
$0.00. Confident. Formatted. Wrong in the worst way a number can be wrong — not by being inaccurate, but by pretending to exist at all.
Nobody had set out to lie. There was just a blank nobody had taught the system to respect, and a default that filled the silence with a precise-looking answer.

The easy fix I refused

There was an easy way to make the zeros go away. We had a second source — a convenient service that gathers prices from elsewhere and hands them over in one tidy feed. If we had quietly pointed the tool at that backup, the reports would have filled with numbers again that same night. No zeros. No gap. No awkward questions.
I refused it. Not because the backup is bad — for early building and testing, it is genuinely useful. I refused the quiet part.
A reader who trusts a report is trusting where the number came from, not just the number. Swap the source in silence and the figure may even be close to right — and the report would still be dishonest, because it claims one origin and uses another.
That rule was already written in our house: every set of data is wired to its first source of truth. A backup stays a backup, labelled as what it is, never swapped in silently. When the main source fails, the honest choices are only two: show the last good data and say it is stale, or show nothing and say it is missing. This night was simply the night the rule had to be kept when keeping it hurt.

What we chose instead, in the open

So we made the choice where everyone could see it.
For everyday prices, the tool now uses a free end-of-day source — and says so, on every report, in those words. Not dressed up as a live exchange feed, because it is not one. End of day means the price is the previous close, delayed by a day, and the report discloses that delay instead of hiding it.
Is a free, delayed source as good as the exchange itself? No. The label is how we handle that gap — not by pretending it away, but by printing it next to the number, every time.
Then we fixed the deeper fault. A price, in the code, is now allowed to be absent. In lines of code that was a small change. In meaning, it was the whole repair. Where the system once demanded a number and invented zero, it now carries the absence to the page — and the page, when it has no price, says two simple words: Not available.
We checked it live, on the real reports. The companies that had shown $0.00 now showed “Not available.” Not one fake zero remained. And when a real price does arrive, it still renders as a real price. We had taught the whole chain to tell the difference between zero and unknown — two ideas that look similar in a spreadsheet and mean opposite things in a decision.

Why this matters far beyond one tool

So many numbers reach us this way now. A dashboard fills in. A report generates. A summary states a figure with calm confidence. Behind it there is a chain like ours: a source, a request, a handler, a formatter. At any link, a missing value can be quietly replaced — by a zero, by yesterday’s figure with today’s date, by a backup nobody declared. The page rarely shows you the chain. It just shows you the number.
The useful habit is not “distrust all numbers.” That helps no one. It is one question, asked every time a number matters: where did this come from, and what does this page do when the answer is missing?
If the source is named, you can judge it. If the delay is disclosed, you can allow for it. If a gap is labelled a gap, you can decide with your eyes open. What no reader should be asked to do is defend themselves against a gap formatted to look like a fact.
That is why a price has to confess — as a working rule, not a slogan: every number that asks for your trust should carry its origin with it, the way a witness carries a name. Anonymous numbers may sometimes be right. They should never be believed on their formatting alone.

A check you can run today

You do not need my tool to use this. Any report, export, or dashboard your own work produces can be asked the same three questions — and the small script shipped with this post does the mechanical first pass on a text file:
For every number that matters, ask:
1. Is the source named on the page itself — not in a manual, not in someone’s memory, on the page?
2. If the source failed today, what would this page show? A labelled gap — or a zero?
3. Is there a backup source? If yes, would the page tell the reader when it was used?
If you cannot answer all three from the page alone, the numbers on it are asking for more trust than they have earned.
Run the questions on one of your own reports before you run them on anyone else’s. Mine failed question two, in production, in front of me. That is not a shameful place to start. It is the only honest one.

The two words I trust most now

“Not available” is not an exciting phrase. It will never be the headline of anything. But I have come to trust those two words more than any number my tools have ever shown me — because a system that can say them is a system that knows the difference between what it knows and what it does not.
The price on my reports still matters. I still want it accurate, current, and well sourced, and we still work at all three. But now, underneath every price, there is a confession travelling with it: where it came from, how old it is, and — when there is nothing to confess because there is nothing there — a plain admission of that too.
A labelled gap beats a confident fabrication. Every time. In markets, in engineering, in any work where someone else will act on what you showed them. “We don’t know” is data too — and sometimes it is the most valuable data on the page.
