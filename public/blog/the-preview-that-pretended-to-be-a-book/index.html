The Preview That Pretended to Be a Book

The book ended on page six.
Not with a conclusion. Not with an ending at all. It just stopped — mid-subject, mid-thought, as if someone had quietly closed a door.
I went back to the start and checked. The title was right. The authors were right. The cover was the cover of a real, respected textbook on protecting power systems — the kind of book engineers keep within reach for a whole career.
Everything about the file said book.
Except the book.
What we had was a preview. Six or seven pages of a much larger work, wearing the whole work’s name.

A label is a claim

Files are polite liars. They rarely lie in words. They lie by arriving under the right name, at a plausible size, in the right folder — and we do the rest ourselves. We see the name we were hoping for, and we stop checking.
I know that feeling well. This book mattered. A protection text we wanted in a library I was preparing, page by page, for serious study. When the file turned up — twice, in fact, two copies staged from different corners of my own shelves — it felt like a small win. Found. Done. Next.
It was not done. It was not even found.
Both copies, when we finally measured them instead of admiring them, held a few thousand characters of text each. One held 6,762 characters. The other held 4,747.
To feel what that means, set it beside a real book from the same shelves. The genuine books in that collection run to hundreds of pages and millions of characters. A single rich chapter can carry thousands of characters on one page.
Six thousand characters, total, is not a book. It is barely a long letter.
The preview had done nothing wrong, in its own terms. A preview is an honest thing — a sample, a first taste, made to be small. The dishonesty was never in the file. It would have been in us, if we had filed it as the book it only pointed at.

It was not alone

Once we started measuring instead of trusting, the same shape showed up again.
A handbook of induction machines — a serious, heavy reference by its name — opened to fifteen pages. A partial. Seventeen thousand characters, and then the same quiet stop. Not the handbook. The beginning of the handbook.
And then there was the material that was not small, but silent.
Some files were large and complete in page count, and still carried no readable text at all. They were pictures of pages: scanned images, page after page of them, that a person could read with eyes and a machine could not read at all. One protection book was there in full — 397 pages — as a fax-style scan. Complete, in a sense. Usable as text, not at all.
Three different files. Three different ways to look like knowledge without being usable as knowledge: a sample wearing a book’s name, a beginning wearing a handbook’s name, and a full set of pictures wearing the name of a text.
If we had trusted the names, all three would have counted. Our shelves would have looked richer. Our count of books would have risen. And the first time anyone reached for the knowledge that was supposedly there, they would have found a door painted on a wall.

The gate that caught them

They were caught by a habit so plain it sounds dull, which is often how you know a habit is good.
Before any material was allowed into the work, it had to pass a few simple tests. Is the file real? Does it open? How much readable text does it actually carry — measured, not assumed? Is it the whole thing it claims to be, or a wrapper, a stub, a sample? And can we say exactly where it came from?
No single test is clever. Together, they do one vital thing: they make a file prove it is what its name says, before its name earns it a place.
The preview failed on size of content. Not on suspicion, not on a feeling — on a number. The partial failed the same way. The picture-books failed a different test: they were honestly flagged as image-only, given a named place to wait — a queue for a slower kind of reading that would turn pictures into text — and excluded until that work was done.
Excluded. Not smuggled in. Not quietly counted and hoped for the best.
When the final accounting was written, it carried a sentence I am prouder of than any total in it: the share of scanned, unreadable material in what was actually used was zero.
Zero is a hard number to earn when the shelves are full of almosts. Every almost is a temptation to round up — to tell yourself the preview is most of the book, the pictures will surely be read someday, the count looks better if you include it. Rounding up is easy, and it is exactly how a library — or a dataset, or a report, or a résumé — becomes a place where the labels are confident and the shelves are empty.

Why this matters beyond one library

You do not need my shelves to meet this problem. You have already met it.
It is the report that has a title page, an executive summary, and nothing behind them. The certificate framed on a wall for a course that was only ever sampled. The spreadsheet with a thousand rows of which the last nine hundred are copies of the first ten. The download that says complete manual and delivers a brochure. The result that looks finished because its first page is beautiful.
The world is full of previews wearing books’ names. They are rarely malicious. They are usually just unfinished things that nobody measured — and then the next person, trusting the label, builds something real on top of them. The harm was never the preview. The harm is the building that follows it, standing on six pages and believing it stands on six hundred.
So the lesson is not "distrust everything." Distrust is expensive and, most of the time, wrong — most files on my shelves were exactly what they claimed to be, and the real books were magnificent. The lesson is cheaper and kinder: measure the thing, not the label on the thing. Open it. Count what is inside. Let the first page tell the truth, and let a number confirm it.
A book is what its pages contain. A report is what its evidence supports. A claim is what survives a small, plain test applied without favour — even to the claims we were hoping were true. Especially to those.
That last part is the whole discipline. The preview we caught was one we wanted. Wanting is what lowers the guard. The gate did not care what we wanted, and that indifference — a few dull tests, applied to everything alike — is what saved the work from building on a beautiful lie.
The real book, for the record, is still out there somewhere. We never did find a full copy, and we wrote that down too, in the same document, in plain words: not found. Not "probably somewhere." Not dropped in silence. Not found — a sentence with its own kind of honesty, the same kind that caught the preview.
Because a library you can trust is not the one that claims the most.
It is the one that can tell a book from its preview — and says so.
