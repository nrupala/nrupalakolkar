The Counterexample That Could Not Happen

The proof tool came back with a number that should not exist.
It was checking one of my claims — a simple one. A current in my calculation work, I had written, can never rise above 20,000,001 amps. The tool did not argue. It did something colder. It offered me a case that broke my claim: 20,000,100 amps.
Ninety-nine amps over the line. Not a rounding smudge. A clean, specific counterexample, with the inputs attached.
My first instinct was to fix the claim. That instinct is exactly what this story is about — because this time, the alarm was wrong. And the only way to know that was to investigate the alarm as carefully as I would have investigated the code.

The work underneath

I am building a calculation engine for short circuits — the moment a power system fails and the current spikes far beyond anything normal. This week’s chapter was about converters: the electronic boxes that turn alternating current into direct current for big drives and industrial loads.
A converter looks quiet. It hums, it switches, it minds its own business. But on the day a fault strikes, it stops being a load and becomes a source. It feeds the fault from its own side — from the direct-current machine behind it, through its own valves, in peaks that rise and die inside a single cycle. If you are sizing the gear that has to survive that moment, you need those peaks, and you need them right.
The worked example in the standard is a full-size one. A system that can deliver five hundred million units of fault power, feeding a converter transformer, feeding a six-thousand-horsepower direct-current motor. I rebuilt the whole chain in code — every link, from the system’s strength down to the motor’s own resistance — and checked my answers against the book’s printed answers at every step. All fifty new checks passed. So did every older chapter’s checks: two hundred and ninety in total, none broken by the new work.
Then came the harder test, the one I run on every chapter now. A proof tool takes the claims I write about my own code — this output stays inside these bounds, this function never divides by something too small — and tries to prove them for every possible input, not just the examples I chose. Where it cannot prove a claim, it says so. Where a claim is false, it hands me the case that breaks it.
It proved three hundred and five of three hundred and twenty-six claims. And it handed me my counterexample.

First, the chapter fought back

Before I tell you what I did with that number, you should know what kind of week it had been — because the chapter itself had arrived damaged.
The text I was working from had lost its square-root signs. Somewhere between the printed page and the file on my screen, every root symbol had quietly vanished. A factor that should have read the square root of three, over two arrived as a plain three over two. In some formulas that plain reading was correct — a path through the converter really does add up to three halves. In others it was wrong by most of a factor of two.
There is no search that fixes that. The only way through was arithmetic: take each doubtful formula, work it both ways, and see which reading lands on the chapter’s own printed answers. The book became its own witness. Reading by reading, the roots went back where they belonged — eleven such flags written into the record, each one named, none of them smoothed over.
I tell you this because it set the week’s mood. Nothing in this work was going to be believed just because it was written down — not the book’s damaged text, not my claims, and, as it turned out, not the proof tool’s alarm either.

Investigating the alarm

So: 20,000,100 amps, offered as a case my code could produce.
I did not fix the claim, and I did not dismiss the alarm. I rebuilt the corner by hand, slowly, from the claim’s own terms.
The current in question is a voltage divided by a resistance. The claim’s bound was built from the hardest corner the inputs allow: the highest voltage the function accepts, divided by the smallest resistance it accepts. That corner is two hundred thousand volts over one hundredth of an ohm. Do the division and you get exactly twenty million. Not twenty million and a hundred. Twenty million, flat — comfortably inside my bound, with one amp of headroom to spare.
Then I asked the machine to do what the proof tool could not: actually run the corner. I evaluated the function at that extreme input, and across a fine grid of inputs around it. Every run returned the same flat twenty million. The breaking case could not be produced — not by the corner, not by anything near it. In the arithmetic the code actually uses, the counterexample does not exist.
What had happened is subtler, and worth understanding. The proof tool reasons about perfect arithmetic — the mathematics of real numbers, where a division can land on any value at all. My code runs in the arithmetic of a real machine, where numbers live on a grid and a quotient snaps to the nearest rung. The tool had found a gap between those two worlds, and reported it honestly as unproven, and here is a candidate. The candidate was real in its world. It is unreachable in mine.
The record now says exactly that, in the open: the alarm was investigated, the corner was rebuilt, the grid was run, and the claim stands — with the tool’s limit named beside it, not hidden under it.

Why this matters more than a clean pass

Last week, on the previous chapter, the same kind of alarm caught a real error — a bound I had written by hand that was genuinely, if slightly, wrong. I fixed it and wrote it down. This week the alarm was the thing that was wrong. I investigated it and wrote that down too.
Those two weeks are the same discipline, facing opposite directions.
An alarm is a claim. It deserves the same treatment as any other claim in the building: show me the case, let me rebuild it, let me run it. Obey every alarm without checking and you will spend your life fixing things that are not broken — and quietly teaching yourself that alarms are noise. Dismiss an alarm because the last one was wrong and you have taught yourself something worse.
And the honest count still stands at the end of the week. Twenty-one of the proof tool’s three hundred and twenty-six checks remain unproven — none refuted, none hidden. They sit on a named list of nine items, with a date set to return to them. The values behind every one are checked against the book. But checked is not proved, and I will not let the one word borrow the other’s strength — not for the claims that passed, and not for the alarm that didn’t.

The lesson

When a tool you trust tells you that you are wrong, believe that it is worth your afternoon. Do not yet believe that it is right.
Rebuild the corner. Run the case. Let the evidence — not the alarm, and not your pride — write the verdict. Some weeks the tool catches you. Some weeks you catch the tool. A record that shows both is the only kind worth keeping.
