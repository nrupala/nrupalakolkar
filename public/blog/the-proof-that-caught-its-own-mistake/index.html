The Proof That Caught Its Own Mistake

2026-11-23 · Industrial Intelligence

The number on my screen said 35,805.7.
The book said 35,806.
That is a difference of less than one amp in nearly thirty-six thousand. I should have been pleased. Instead, I sat still for a moment — because the part of the work I trust most had just told me that I, personally, had written a wrong number into the code. Not the book. Not the method. Me.
Let me back up, and tell it in order.

A bank of capacitors, a bolted fault

I am building a calculation engine for short circuits — the violent moment when something fails in a power system and the current spikes far beyond anything normal. Engineers size their equipment for that moment. Get the number wrong and you either waste money on gear you did not need, or worse, you install gear that cannot survive the fault it was bought to face.
This week’s piece was capacitors.
A capacitor bank stores energy. On the day everything goes right, it quietly helps hold the voltage up. On the day a fault strikes nearby, it does something else: it dumps its stored energy into the fault, fast. How fast, and how hard, is a real question with a real answer, and a standard worked example walks through it step by step.
The example is plain enough to state. A bank rated at ten million units of reactive power, sitting on a bus at 13,800 volts. A solid fault — engineers call it bolted, meaning there is nothing merciful in the path — connected through thirty metres of cable. What is the peak current when the bank lets go?
The book works the chain one link at a time. The bank’s reactance from its rating. Its capacitance from that. Its rated current. The cable’s impedance, scaled by its length. The inductance hiding in that impedance. Then the peak discharge current, and the natural frequency at which the discharge rings — a little over sixty times the normal frequency of the system, a high, fast ring riding on a system built for a slow one.
I rebuilt that chain in code, link by link, and checked my code’s answer against the book’s printed answer at every single step. Not just at the end. At every step. That habit sounds slow. It is the opposite. When the final number is wrong, a step-by-step chain tells you exactly which link broke. When you only check the end, a wrong answer is just a mystery with good formatting.
Every link matched. The peak came out at 35,805.7 amps against the book’s 35,806 — and the book itself, in a later section, predicts the same figure from a different direction, so my code had to satisfy two independent answers, not one. Both agreed.
That was the good news. Then I asked the harder question.

The part that refuses

A capacitor is, in the language of this work, a negative reactance. The rest of the system is positive. Put them in parallel and their values combine in a way that has a cliff in it: when the positive and the negative nearly cancel, the combined value does not glide through zero politely. It explodes. Engineers call that condition resonance, and near it, small errors stop being small.
So the code was written with a refusal built in. If the two values come so close to cancelling that the answer would be a guess dressed as a number, the code does not guess. It stops and says so.
I like tools that know where their own ground ends. A calculator that will happily print twelve digits on the far side of a cliff is not being helpful. It is being confident, which is a different and much cheaper thing. The refusal is not a limitation I apologise for. It is the feature I would show a customer first.

Then the proof turned around

Matching a book is one kind of check. It proves the arithmetic follows the method. It does not prove the code can never misbehave on inputs the book never tried.
For that, I use a proof tool. You state, in advance, what must always be true — this output stays within these bounds, this function never divides by something too close to zero — and the tool tries to prove those statements for every possible input, not just the ones you tested. Where it cannot prove a statement, it says so. Where your statement is actually false, it does something ruder and much more useful: it hands you the exact input that breaks it.
It handed me one of mine.
For one function — the bank’s rated current — I had written the upper bound myself, from a corner case I had worked out by hand: the largest bank at the lowest voltage. My bound said the current could never exceed 5,773,501 amps. The proof tool came back, politely and without any doubt, with the breaking case: 5,773,503.
Two amps. In five point seven million. My hand arithmetic for the corner had been slightly off, and float rounding had moved the true corner a breath further. The function itself was right. My claim about the function was wrong — by two amps — and the proof caught the claim, not the code.
I fixed the bound to the true corner, in the open, and the record of the work says plainly that the first proof run failed on my number. I would not trade that moment away. This is what a check is for. A check that only ever agrees with you is not a check. It is a mirror.

What I did not prove, said out loud

Here is the part I insist on, in my own work and in anything I would sell.
The proof run did not prove everything. Of 161 statements, 151 were proved. Ten were not — the tool ran out of road on a family of them involving square roots, and it neither proved them nor found them false. They sit on a named list, five items, with a date set to return to them.
I could have written "formally proven" on the work and left the ten in a drawer. Nobody would have known. That is exactly why the list is written in the record, next to the successes, in the same size of type. The arithmetic behind those ten is checked against the book’s values, every one. But checked is not proved, and I will not let one word borrow the other’s strength.
That discipline costs a little pride and buys the only thing this kind of work runs on: when I say a number, the person reading it does not have to wonder which kind of "checked" I meant.

The lesson I keep relearning

The machine matched the book to a third of an amp, and that was satisfying. The proof catching my own two-amp error in a claim I wrote — that was valuable.
Tests tell you the work agrees with the examples you chose. Proof tells you whether your claims survive the examples you did not choose — including the ones hiding in your own handwriting. Build both. Publish the failures of both. And when your tools catch you, thank them in the record, by name and by number, where everyone can see it.
Two amps in five point seven million. Caught. Fixed. Written down. That is not an embarrassment. That is the whole point.
