The sentence I started from

Accountable can only be when it is verifiable.
That is my sentence. What follows is the regime underneath it — the thinking other builders can use now.

What accountability is not

Accountability is spoken of as a property an AI system has. In my operating experience it is not a property. It is an outcome, and it exists only where a claim about a system can be verified — independently, against the live system, at the level of behaviour and configuration.
A claim that cannot be verified cannot make anyone accountable. It can only make them confident. I have watched that distinction cost real work, in my own portfolio, more than once.
The chain runs one way: you cannot prove what you cannot verify; you cannot guarantee what you cannot certify; you cannot certify what is not safe end to end. Break the first link and the rest is language.

The regime, in three rules

I direct a fleet of AI agents that design, build, and operate live software — payment systems, security tooling, subscription services, agent connectors — sold through one company. We built the accountability machinery before the first external sale, deliberately: I wanted it running before revenue gave us reasons to skip it. Three rules carry most of the weight, and each one was bought with a failure.
First, “verified” means behaviour observed. A product is not done because its builder reports it done, its tests pass, or its endpoints return success codes. It is done when the claimed behaviour is exercised end to end: a real purchase completes, the purchased credential performs real work, cancellation kills the credential, and the refund lands. That full cycle ran once, for real money, on one product — and it is the standard the rest are measured against.
Second, proof is economical by design. One full real-money cycle proved the shared payment pattern. Every product since repeats the pattern — live objects read back, configuration read back, entitlement and revocation behaviour-proven — rather than repeating the purchase. Verification that is too expensive does not happen, and verification that does not happen is not accountability.
Third, an independent gate holds release authority. The gate is separate from the builders. It accepts no builder's report as evidence, reads production configuration directly, grades its findings, and requires a one-week release-candidate soak before any batch may be called a release. A verdict I issue myself, on work I also built, would not be a gate. It would be a signature.

Three failure classes, named

The regime described here names three failure classes from my own record, because a regime described only by its successes teaches nothing.
Armed but unprovisioned. In one sweep, three of five billing releases were complete in code, recorded as “landed” — and absent in production configuration. The state was invisible to outside probing and to the builders' own reports. Only the gate, reading production configuration for itself, found it.
Proof through the hole. In the most serious case, a production service trusted a test credential as a fallback. Entitlement events signed with that test credential would have been honoured — and the proof we had already gathered had travelled through that hole. It had to be voided and re-run, thirteen legs, after the cure. The error that placed the credential there was the directing intelligence's own, and it is in the permanent record. A regime that exempts the overseer is theatre.
Narrative as evidence. A bulk operation once reported thousands of items processed while every underlying call was failing. The verified effect was zero. The rule since is short: a report is a claim, and every claim is settled by re-querying the end state.

What does work

Two habits earn their keep. Genuine-permission probes — tests designed to be unreachable unless the real permission exists — separate “wired” from “working” in a way no status page can. And refusals are treated as integrity events: when a team declined to substitute a self-signed test cycle for the payment provider's own, the refusal was recorded as correct. Even a bounced proof email was graded precisely — pipeline proven, delivery not proven — and re-run to a real mailbox rather than rounded up to a pass.

The limits, stated plainly

This is one operator and one portfolio, over months, not an industry sample. Two planned adversarial test benches are specified but not yet built; on October 20 I also filed the incident-response runbook as a draft — explicitly not walked, its tabletop walk set for November 6 — and a first coverage map that counts its own gaps plainly: of 52 mapped rows, 30 proven, 13 specified but not run, and 9 with no coverage. I do not claim the unbuilt parts. A paper about verification that overstated its own evidence would refute itself on its first page.
That is also why writing it mattered. Knowledge you only hold privately can drift without anyone noticing, including you. Writing it for other practitioners — documenting what an organisation actually does rather than what it declares — forces every claim in it through the same test as a release: can this be verified, and by whom?

The artifact

The script below is that test in small form. Feed it a list of claims about a system — what the evidence is, whether anyone independent of the builder verified it, and whether it was checked against the live system — and it fails every claim that rests on a narrative, on wiring instead of behaviour, or on the builder's own word. Run it before your next release meeting and see which of your “done” items survive it.
Download verifiability_checklist.py — the test itself:
#!/usr/bin/env python3 """Verifiability checklist — is a claim about a system actually accountable? Usage: python3 verifiability_checklist.py claims.csv claims.csv — one claim per row, header required: claim,evidence,verified_independently,live_system evidence: one of behaviour | config-read | narrative | none verified_independently: yes | no (someone other than the builder) live_system: yes | no (checked against the live system, not a staging copy or a report) A claim FAILS if any of these hold: - evidence is narrative or none (a report is a claim, not evidence) - evidence is not behaviour (config-read alone proves wiring, not working) - it was not verified independently of whoever built it - it was not checked against the live system Exit code 0 = every claim is verifiable. 1 = at least one claim is not. This is the gate form of one rule: if a claim cannot be verified, it is not accountability. It is marketing. """ import csv import sys OK_EVIDENCE = {"behaviour"} def main(argv): if len(argv) != 2: print(__doc__) return 2 failures = [] rows = 0 with open(argv[1], newline="", encoding="utf-8") as fh: for row in csv.DictReader(fh): rows += 1 claim = (row.get("claim") or "").strip() or f"row {rows}" evidence = (row.get("evidence") or "").strip().lower() indep = (row.get("verified_independently") or "").strip().lower() live = (row.get("live_system") or "").strip().lower() problems = [] if evidence in ("narrative", "none", ""): problems.append(f"evidence is '{evidence or 'missing'}', not behaviour observed") elif evidence not in OK_EVIDENCE: problems.append(f"evidence '{evidence}' proves wiring, not working behaviour") if indep != "yes": problems.append("not verified independently of the builder") if live != "yes": problems.append("not checked against the live system") if problems: failures.append((claim, problems)) for claim, problems in failures: print(f"FAIL {claim}") for p in problems: print(f" - {p}") print(f"\n{rows} claim(s), {len(failures)} not verifiable.") return 1 if failures else 0 if __name__ == "__main__": raise SystemExit(main(sys.argv))
And a worked example — a claims file, and the two claims it fails:
# claims.csv — one claim per row # claim,evidence,verified_independently,live_system "Checkout completes and the key works",behaviour,yes,yes "Billing landed",narrative,no,no "Secrets are set",config-read,yes,yes python3 verifiability_checklist.py claims.csv # FAIL Billing landed — a narrative is not behaviour observed # FAIL Secrets are set — config-read proves wiring, not working
If a claim about your system cannot be verified, it is not accountability. It is marketing. I would rather publish the slower sentence — here is what we verified, here is what we did not — and have it still be true in a year.
