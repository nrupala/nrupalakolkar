The Missing Door Is Still a Missing Product

We called two products launched. Then someone asked a plain question: where is the browser extension?
There was no good answer. For one product, the thing we had been calling an extension was not an extension at all. For the other, the extension was real, finished, and sitting in a drawer — built, tested, and never sent anywhere. Both products were live. Both products were incomplete. And nobody had noticed, because nobody had ever written down the full list of doors a product owes the people who use it.
That is the uncomfortable part. The work had not failed. The counting had. You cannot miss what you never enumerated.

A door that was not a door

Start with the first product. On paper, it had an extension. The word appeared in our notes, and the word did quiet, reassuring work every time someone read it. Extension: present. Box: ticked. Move on.
When we finally opened the thing itself, it was something else entirely — a small gallery file for a command-line tool, pointing at a service address. Useful, perhaps. Legitimate, certainly. But a person sitting in a browser, looking for the product where their work actually happens, would never find a door there. We had labelled one kind of object with another kind of object’s name, and the label had protected the gap from ever being questioned.
This happens more often than builders like to admit. A name is not a surface. A file that mentions a place is not a way into that place. If you want to know whether a door exists, do not read the sign. Try the handle.

The door that was built and never opened

The second case was stranger, and in some ways worse.
That extension had actually been made. The work was done. It had been prepared for the store and proven on a real machine. Then it stopped — not because anything was wrong with it, but because nothing in our system required the next step. No list said: a built door must be submitted. No check asked: why is finished work still sitting here? So it sat, complete and invisible, while we went on describing the product as launched.
Effort is not delivery. A finished thing in a drawer serves exactly as many people as a thing that was never built. The only difference is that the drawer version costs more, because you paid for it twice — once to make it, and once in the confidence you borrowed against it.

Why missing things stay invisible

There is a simple reason gaps like these survive. We track what we are building, not what a finished thing owes.
A build list is a story told from the maker’s side: features started, features finished, problems solved. It is a good story, and most of it is true. But a customer does not meet your build list. A customer meets surfaces — a website, a phone app, a browser door, a way to reach you, a way to use the thing from the tools they already live in. If one of those surfaces is absent, the customer does not experience a small gap in an otherwise complete product. They experience a product that is not there, in the one place they went looking for it.
And absence has a special talent for hiding. A broken door complains. It throws errors, it draws reports, it gets fixed. A missing door is silent. Nobody writes to tell you about the entrance they never found. They just go somewhere else, and you record it, if you record anything, as a mystery about demand.

The list that changed the meaning of launched

So we stopped trusting memory and built the list the way it should have existed from the start: a plain grid. Down one side, every product. Across the top, every surface a product can owe its users — a website, a service connection, an interface other tools can call, an email door, a phone app for each major platform, a browser extension, a desktop app, a command-line tool.
Then we filled in every cell honestly: present, missing, not applicable — and for the ones that claimed to be present, we checked.
The grid held nineteen missing cells across the whole set. Nineteen. Not nineteen disasters — some were small, some were planned for later, some belonged to products still growing. But before that day, the number was not nineteen. The number was unknown, which is worse. You can plan around a known gap. An unknown gap plans around you.
Out of that grid came a rule I now keep where I cannot avoid it: no product is completely deployed while a surface it owes is missing, or unverified, and I have not consciously set that surface aside. The last part matters. Choosing not to build a door is a legitimate decision. Products do not owe every surface. But the decision has to be a decision — written in the cell, with the reason — not a silence that everyone mistakes for coverage.

A completeness check you can run today

You do not need a large system for this. You need one grid, kept where your launch decisions are made, and the discipline to read it before you use the word launched.
For each product, list every surface it could owe:
website · service connection · tool interface · email phone app (each platform) · browser extension desktop app · command-line tool
For every cell, write exactly one word:
PRESENT - and where a stranger can verify it MISSING - owed, and not yet there SET ASIDE - not owed, with the reason written beside it
Launch rule: no MISSING cells, and no PRESENT you have not personally verified. Anything else is not launched. It is launched-ish.
Run it the hard way at least once. Open each claimed surface the way a stranger would — signed out, from the front, with no shortcuts and no memory of how it was built. The grid will embarrass you the first time. That embarrassment is the product. A list that never embarrasses anyone is not a list. It is a decoration.

The word launched should cost something

We use the word launched the way we use the word done — as a comfort. It closes a chapter. It lets the next thing start. That is exactly why it needs a price.
A product is not the sum of the work you put into it. It is the set of doors a real person can actually walk through. One of those doors missing does not make the work worthless — the other doors are real, and the people using them are real. But it does make the claim incomplete, and incomplete claims have a way of quietly becoming the culture of a house. First one surface is assumed. Then another is labelled instead of built. Then a finished thing sits in a drawer for a season because no list ever asked after it.
The grid ended that, not by making us better people, but by making the gaps visible on an ordinary day, in plain cells, where a decision could reach them.
Write the list before you need it. Try every handle. Count the missing doors while they are still just cells in a grid — because out in the world, a missing door is not a cell at all.
It is a customer who never came in, and never told you why.
