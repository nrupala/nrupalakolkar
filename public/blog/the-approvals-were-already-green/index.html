The Approvals Were Already Green

For a week, my notes said the same calm sentence: three listings are still under review.
It was a tidy sentence. It sounded careful. It let everyone move on to other work, because pending things are not urgent things — you wait for them, the way you wait for weather to change.
Then, on an ordinary records check, I opened the decision messages themselves. They were dated a week earlier. Each one had arrived within minutes of the filing it answered. Approved. Claimed. Done — while my notes were still patiently saying under review, and would have gone on saying it for as long as nobody looked again.
Nothing had gone wrong out in the world. The world had answered quickly and clearly. The only place the answer had never arrived was my own tracker.

Pending is not a place

We talk about pending as if it were a place a thing sits in — a waiting room, with chairs, where good filings rest until someone calls their name.
It is not a place. It is a claim.
When I write pending beside an item, I am claiming something about the world today: that a decision has not yet been made, by people outside this room, on a surface I do not control. That claim was true on the day I filed. Every day after that, it became a little less earned — because the world keeps moving whether I re-check it or not, and my sentence does not.
That is the quiet danger of a status. It does not announce when it stops being true. A broken thing complains. A finished thing, filed under the wrong word, is silent. It sits there being complete, while the people who own it plan around a wait that ended days ago.
In this case the cost was small: a week of wrong briefs, and work sequenced around approvals we already held. But run the same habit in the other direction and the cost stops being small. A tracker that can be a week stale about good news can be a week stale about bad news too — a rejection nobody read, a condition nobody met, a deadline quietly walking past. The direction of the surprise does not matter. The habit is the problem.

Why stale states survive

Stale states survive because they are comfortable, and because three ordinary habits protect them.
The first habit is treating a sent message as a result. I filed, therefore the story is now in someone else’s hands, therefore my record of it is finished until they speak. But sending is an action, not an outcome. The outcome lives on their surface, in their system, under their word — and it can change there long before it changes in mine.
The second habit is letting a brief copy the last brief. Once a status is written down in a respectable document, the next document borrows it, and the next one borrows it from that one. After a few rounds the sentence looks well sourced. It has been repeated in many places. It has been checked in none of them. Repetition starts to feel like verification, and it is not. It is just the same old claim, wearing more clothes.
The third habit is having too many lists. When submissions live scattered across chats, notes, and separate trackers, no single list ever has to answer for the whole truth. Each fragment can be honestly maintained and the whole can still be wrong — because nobody owns the moment where a fragment gets compared with the world again.
I know these habits because they were mine, in my own notes, in the same week. The fix was not to try harder at remembering. Memory is not a system. The fix was to change what a status is allowed to mean.

Green has to be earned in person

So the lists were gathered into one rolling list — one place, one standard template, every submission on it from the day it is made.
Each row carries a status in three colours. Red means not yet live. Yellow means something conditional has come back and there is work still to do. Green is the interesting one, because green got a definition strict enough to hurt a little:
Green means live on the surface itself, and claimed, and seen directly — by my own eyes, on the other side’s own page, on a date written in the row.
Not green because a message was sent. Not green because someone reported it green. Not green because it was green last month. A row earns green the way a traveller earns a stamp: by actually arriving, and being seen to arrive.
And every row, whatever its colour, carries the date it was last verified against the live surface. That date is the honest part of the row. A status without a verification date is a rumour with good formatting. A status with one tells you exactly how much trust it has earned and when that trust starts to expire.
Because statuses do expire. That may be the simplest lesson in this whole story: write the expiry into the habit. If a row says pending and nobody has looked at the real surface in seven days, the row is not telling you the filing is pending. It is telling you that you have not looked in seven days. Those are different sentences, and only one of them is true.

A check you can run today

You do not need special software for this. You need one list, kept where decisions are made, with four honest columns — and the discipline to re-earn your colours on a schedule.
One list for every submission, filing, or application:
item - what was sent, in plain words status - RED / YELLOW / GREEN evidence - where on the live surface you saw it last checked - the date you saw it there yourself
Rules that keep the list honest:
GREEN only when it is live AND claimed, seen directly on their surface — never from a sent message, never from someone’s report.
PENDING older than your check interval is not a status. It is a task: go and look.
One list. A second list is where the truth goes to get lost.
Run it once on the list you already keep. Pick every row that says pending, waiting, or under review, and check each one at its source — the actual page, the actual inbox, the actual register — not in your own notes about it. I would be surprised if every row survives the afternoon unchanged. One of mine had been wrong for a week, in the pleasant direction. The unpleasant direction is the one this habit exists to catch early, while catching it is still cheap.

The answer may already be waiting

There is a strange, hopeful side to this story, and I do not want to lose it under the discipline.
Sometimes the work is further along than you think. Sometimes the door you are patiently waiting beside opened days ago, quietly, on time, exactly as hoped — and the only thing between you and walking through it is a sentence in your own notes that nobody thought to question.
That is worth the small embarrassment of checking. A tracker should be a window, not a painting of a window. Keep it pressed against the real world, date every look, and let green be a word a row has to earn in person.
Your approvals may already be green.
Go and look — at the source, with your own eyes, today.
