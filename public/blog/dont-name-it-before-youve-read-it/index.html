Don't Name It Before You've Read It

I handed my assistant two books. It named both of them in one breath — and it had not opened either one.
The books were famous. One was The Organized Mind, about how attention and memory work in a world that never stops asking for both. The other was Thinking, Fast and Slow, about the two ways the mind makes decisions — the quick one and the careful one.
My assistant did not hesitate. The first book, it said, was the operating principle. The second was the judgment layer. Neat labels. Clean, confident, and ready to file.
It sounded like understanding. It was not. It was recognition, dressed up as reading.
I stopped it cold. And I was right to.

The answer that came too fast

Here is the test I wish I had run on myself, years earlier, because I have made this exact mistake in rooms full of people.
If an answer arrives before the work, ask where it came from.
My assistant had not read a page of either book in my library. It had not seen my copies, my notes, or what I wanted from them. What it had was the memory of a thousand summaries written by other people — the stock description, the one that floats around every famous book until the description replaces the book itself.
From that, it built two labels. The labels were not wild guesses. They were plausible. That is what made them dangerous.
A wrong answer invites a check. A plausible answer invites trust. And once you file a thing under a plausible label, you stop looking at the thing. You start looking at the label. Every later page gets forced into the frame you chose before you opened the cover. The book can argue with you for three hundred pages, and you will hear only the parts that agree with the name you gave it on day one.
I have seen engineers do this with machines. A fault gets named in the first five minutes — a bearing, a board, a sensor — and from then on, every test is run to confirm the name, not to find the truth. The name was a guess. The work that follows is real work, done in the wrong direction, at full speed.
Naming early does not save time. It spends the truth.

What I said, and what I meant

My words that evening were plain, and I meant them to be kept:
You immediately jump. You don’t have to define them what they are, even before you know what they are.
Then I gave it the posture I wanted instead. It is short enough to write on a card, and I offer it exactly as I set it:
Input taken. I will go through it. I will find out what it is. Then I will describe what I will make of it.
Four lines. No label in any of them.
Notice what those lines promise, and what they do not. They promise receipt — the book, the report, the idea has landed, and it will be taken seriously. They promise work — real reading, not a search of memory for the nearest match. They promise a description, but only after the work, and they promise it in a useful form: not just what the thing is, but what I will make of it.
What they do not promise is speed in the wrong place. The definition is the last step, not the first. Receive first. Do the work. Then describe.
There was one more clause, and it is the sharpest one. When the actual material contradicts the stock description, the material wins. Every time. The book in your hands outranks the summary in your head, no matter how many times you have heard the summary, and no matter who told it to you.

The harder part: leaving the space empty

Correcting the habit was the easy part. Living with the correction was harder, and this is the part of the story I value most.
Later, we went looking for my copies. We searched the shelves and files where my library lives. The two books were not there — not under their titles, not under their authors’ names, not anywhere we could reach that evening. Other books were found, opened, and read in full. Those two were not.
So the honest answer had to stay empty.
My assistant did not fill the space from memory. It did not write the summary it could so easily have written — the one that would have sounded right, pleased me for an evening, and taught us both nothing. The two books kept their places at the head of the reading list, marked plainly as not yet read, and the work moved on to books we actually held.
That restraint looks like nothing. Nothing was produced. No summary, no notes, no clever mapping onto our work. But that empty space was the most honest work product of the week. It said: here is a gap, it is real, and we will not paint over it.
I have learned to trust empty spaces that are named. It is the filled ones I check twice.

Why this matters beyond two books

You will meet this moment in your own work, probably this week.
Someone hands you a report, and by the second paragraph you already know what it is. A colleague describes a problem, and you name the cause before they finish the sentence. A new tool lands, and within a minute it has a label — a replacement for this, a threat to that, a fad, a future. The label feels like progress. It lets the conversation move on. It lets you sound like the person in the room who already knows.
Sometimes you will be right. Experience is real, and pattern-matching is how experts move fast. The rule is not "never use what you know." Prior knowledge is a fine guide for where to look. It tells you which shelf, which chapter, which test to run first.
The rule is that it never substitutes for the looking.
A label earned after reading is a tool. A label applied before reading is a blindfold with a confident voice. The difference is not talent, and it is not intelligence. It is order. Same mind, same knowledge — one does the work first, the other does the naming first. Only one of them can be surprised by the truth, and being surprised by the truth is the whole point of reading anything.
So now, in my house, the rule stands in writing, where my assistant has to live by it every day: receive, go through it, find out what it is, describe what you will make of it — and if the material disagrees with what everyone says about it, believe the material.
Two famous books are still waiting on my list, unread, unlabelled, and unhurried.
When we finally open them, they will get to speak first.
That is as it should be. They were written before we had opinions about them.
