The training run that kept dying of heat

My training run died fifteen steps in, cooked by its own heat.
I was teaching an AI model from my own library of engineering books, on the laptop I use every day — no server room, no rented machines in a distant data centre, just this laptop and a full day's work ahead of it.
The fix was not in any guide I had read. And when I finally found it, slowing the machine down cost me nothing at all.

The first run

Training a model is patient work. You show it examples — thousands of them, again and again — and slowly, measurably, it gets better. You track that with a number called the loss: the lower it falls, the more the model has learned. Mine was falling beautifully.
The plan was simple. Let it run for about a day, and come back to a model that had read my whole library.
The temperature had other plans.
Within six minutes of the run reaching full speed, the graphics card — the part of the laptop that does this heavy arithmetic — had climbed from 70 degrees to 86. A safety guard I had set up did exactly what it was told: it paused the run, let the machine cool down, and started it again. The card was hot again in two minutes. The guard paused and resumed once more — and this time the run did not survive the restart. It died quietly, mid-lesson.
Fifteen steps of learning, gone. A run that dies at step fifteen has not trained anything. It has only made heat.

The fix that said yes and did nothing

Every guide on the internet gives the same advice for a hot graphics card: tell it to use less power. There is a standard command for exactly that. I ran it, and the machine answered, politely: All done.
Nothing changed. The card drew the same power as before — about a hundred watts — as if I had never asked.
I checked carefully, because the answer mattered. The command had not exactly failed. It had said yes and quietly done nothing. On a laptop like mine, where the graphics card is shared between the machine's own system and the workspace the training lives in, that power control is simply never connected. The button exists. There is nothing behind it.
It is a strange feeling, being lied to by a success message.
That was the real lesson of the morning, and it is bigger than one laptop: don't trust the receipt — measure the thing itself. A tool that reports success while doing nothing will fool you every time, politely, and with a success code to prove it. The only proof is the number on the meter.

Smaller bites didn't help either

Next, I tried feeding the model smaller bites of work — the same meal, in smaller mouthfuls, so the card would never have to strain. It made no difference at all. In about two and a half minutes the card was climbing the same hill, past 80 degrees, and I stopped the trial myself rather than let it cook.
Smaller bites change how much work the card holds at once. They do not change how hard it works. I had assumed they would. Now I had measured it. The distance between those two sentences is the whole game.

The dial that actually exists

If I could not tell the card how much power to use, maybe I could tell it how fast to run.
That dial exists. It is one line, typed before the run starts, while the machine is still idle and cool:
nvidia-smi -lgc 300,1050
In plain words, all this says is: graphics card, never run faster than this speed. Like cruise control in a car — you set it before the journey, and then you leave it alone.
I will be honest: I expected a compromise. Slower, but safer. The usual bargain.
Instead, I started the run and watched the temperature the way you watch a pot that has boiled over once already. It climbed to 70. Then 72. Then it stopped climbing.
It just sat there — 72 degrees, steady, drawing about 70 watts instead of a hundred — while the training ran at full stretch underneath. It passed step fifteen, the graveyard, without noticing. The loss kept falling. The machine had found its pace. I checked on it the way you check on a sleeping child — quietly, and mostly to reassure myself.
And then came the surprise, the part I still enjoy telling people: it was not slower. Each step had taken about thirty seconds before the lock, and each step took about thirty seconds after it. All that extra speed I had started with had been turning directly into heat. It was buying nothing. I had not traded speed for safety — I had only stopped paying for heat.

The lesson

Control the power, and the temperature will follow. I spent a morning fighting the temperature — pausing it, cooling it, pleading with it. The temperature was never the problem. It was only the messenger.
A few things I do now, offered the way I would offer them to a friend:
Test a control before you trust it. Set the limit, then measure the real power and the real temperature under real work. If the meter doesn't move, the control isn't real. - Watch the first ten minutes of any long run. You are not hoping for a slower climb. You are looking for a curve that stops climbing. - Choose a ceiling you never plan to touch. Mine is 85 degrees. My machine lives at 72. The distance between those two numbers is my peace of mind. - Set your controls before you start, and leave them alone while the work runs.
As I write this, the model is still training on my desk. Seventy-two degrees. Quietly learning my library, one patient step at a time — about a day's work, all told. When it finishes, the real test begins: asking it questions, and finding out what it actually learned. That is a story for another day.
Slow and steady finishes. Fast and dead doesn't.
