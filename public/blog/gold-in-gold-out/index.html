The model was ready to train. The data was not ready to be believed.
That was the morning I stopped the work.
Everything looked finished. A large set of training examples had been built from my own library — thousands of passages, drawn from books and papers I have kept for years. The next step was the easy, exciting one: start training and see what the model could do.
I asked one plain question first.
"How good is the data?"
Not how much of it there was. How good was it — and could we prove it, topic by topic, in writing, before a single claim was made about the model that would learn from it?
We had been ready to judge the student before anyone had checked the textbook.

The law I set that morning

I have spent a working life around machines that do exactly what they are fed. Feed a control system a bad signal and it will not argue. It will obey.
A model is no different. It gives back its content. What it presents is what it has. Nothing more, and — this is the part people forget — nothing better.
So I set a law for the work, and I said it in the plainest words I had:
We can only say that our model is great and working well if, and only if, our data quality is good. If our data quality is not good, we are not doing the right thing.
Garbage in, garbage out. Quality in, quality out. Gold standard in, gold standard out.
It sounds like a slogan. It is not. It is a gate. It means the verdict on the data comes first, and it gates every claim that comes after it. A model verdict without a data verdict is not a report. It is an advertisement. I do not make those, and I was not about to start with my own work.
So the training waited. The judging began — of the data, not the model.

Every example had to earn its place

The judging was not a feeling. It was a set of tests, applied to every single item, one by one.
Was the source a real file, not a label that promised a book and delivered a fragment? Did it open? Did it carry real text a machine could read — measured, page by page — or was it only a picture of pages, honestly flagged as such? Was it a duplicate of something already counted, wearing a second name? And could we say, for every example, exactly where it came from?
That last test matters more than it sounds. "Somewhere on my shelves" is not an answer. Provenance means each example carries its source with it, so anyone can walk back from a claim to the thing the claim rests on.
When the work was done, the set that passed looked like this.
The dataset held 4,519 examples, drawn from 134 sources, with zero gaps in provenance. Every example could name where it came from. Not most of them. Every one.
That sentence — zero gaps — is short. It took the longest to earn.

The drops were counted, not hidden

Here is the part of the story I care about most, because it is the part most reports quietly leave out.
To get to those 4,519 examples, a large amount of material was thrown away — and the throwing away was counted, in public, in the same document as the celebration.
2,177 passages were filtered out. Index pages that are furniture, not knowledge. Tables of contents. Passages too short to teach anything. Copyright notices and boilerplate that had slipped into an earlier version of the work as if they were content. Duplicates that crossed from one pool of material into another.
Each kind of drop was named, and each kind had its number beside it.
Why does that matter? Because a filter you do not count is a filter you cannot trust. If I tell you I cleaned the data and I cannot tell you what I removed, or how much, then "clean" is just a word I am asking you to believe. A count can be checked. A word cannot.
The same rule held for what never entered at all. Material that existed only as scanned images — pictures of pages with no readable text inside — did not get smuggled in as if it were text. It went to a named queue, to wait for a different kind of reading, and it was excluded until then. Scanned share of what was actually used: zero. That is not a loss. That is honesty with a number on it.

Gold in the middle, thin at the edges

Then came the verdict itself, and this is where the discipline was tested — because a verdict that grades everything excellent is worth nothing.
Topic by topic, the data was graded against a simple scale. Gold meant depth, clean text, and enough of it to learn from. Below gold sat the honest lower grades: usable, with a stated limit. Thin, where something was present but light. And gap, where there was simply no usable material at all — said in those words.
Eleven topics earned gold. They were the core of the field — the ground the whole project stands on — and they earned it with evidence: depth, clean extraction, and sufficiency, all three.
And in the same document, on the same page, the weak ground was named just as plainly. Fuel cells: a gap. Marine engineering: a gap. The electrical side of data centres: a gap. Not footnotes. Not fine print. Lines in the verdict, in the same size type as the triumphs.
I remember reading that list and feeling something I did not expect: relief.
A report that names its own gaps can be trusted about its strengths. A report that hides its gaps cannot be trusted about anything, because you have learned what its author does when the news is bad — and the news, in any real body of work, is sometimes bad.
The verdict, in full, was this: good — a gold-standard core, thin edges named, nothing inflated.
Read that sentence again. The middle clause is doing the work. "Thin edges named" is what makes "gold-standard core" a fact instead of a boast, and "nothing inflated" is the promise that lets the other two stand.

What the gate changed

The training went ahead after that — on data that had earned it. But the bigger change was the order of operations, for everything since.
Now quality is a verdict you issue before the celebration, not a feeling you have after it. The weak parts are named in the same breath as the strong ones, or the report is not finished. And no result gets described as great until the material underneath it has been described, honestly, first.
That order feels slower. It is not. It is the fastest route I know to work you do not have to redo in embarrassment later.
Anyone can build on unjudged material. The build will even work, for a while, in the easy cases. The failure arrives later, dressed as a surprise, in a hard case — and by then nobody can say which input taught the system to be wrong, because nobody checked the inputs one by one.

The rule worth stealing

If you take one thing from this story, take the order, not the scale. My library is mine; your material will be different. The order travels.
Judge the inputs before you praise the output. Grade topic by topic, not in one warm blur. Count what you removed and say what it was. Excluded material gets a name and a reason, never a quiet disappearance. And when part of the ground is thin, write "thin" — in the same document, in the same breath, where your reader can see you doing it.
Gold in, gold out is not a promise that good material guarantees a great result. It is a discipline that makes a great result possible — and a poor one explainable, because you know exactly what went in.
The model, in the end, is a mirror of its material.
So polish the mirror last.
First, be honest about the glass.
