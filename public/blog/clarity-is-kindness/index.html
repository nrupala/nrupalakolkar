I sent myself a finished blog post. Every fact in it was right.
I sent it back with one question: where is the kindness?
That morning taught me more about writing than years of reports ever had. A page can be correct and still be unkind. If the reader has to work hard to receive what you made for them, you have not finished the work. You have only moved your work onto them.

The draft that did everything except welcome me in

The post told a true story. A long training run on my own computer kept dying of heat. We found the cause. We fixed it. The run finished. Every number in the draft was real. Every step was in the right order.
And I could not get through it without effort.
It read like a logbook. Times. Settings. Steps. One fact after another, all of them true, none of them alive. I had to read sentences twice. I had to hold the thread in my head while the page explained itself. By the middle, I knew what had happened. I did not feel any of it.
That is a strange place to land. Nothing was wrong, and yet the whole thing was wrong.
I have read that kind of page many times in my career. You have too. A report that is complete and still leaves you tired. A manual that answers every question except the one you came with. A message so packed with detail that the one thing you needed is buried in the middle, wearing the same clothes as everything else.
The writer did the hard part. They got the facts. Then they stopped one step early. They handed the reader a pile of true things and left the reader to build the meaning alone.

Correct is not the same as kind

Here is the sentence that stayed with me that day, and it has stayed since: clarity is kindness.
Simple words are not a style choice. They are a respect choice. Plain words say: your time matters. Your attention matters. I will not spend either one to make myself sound clever. Heavy words do the opposite. They make the reader pay for my comfort.
Short sentences help. One idea at a time helps. But clarity is more than short lines. It is order. Scene before explanation. The moment before the lesson. A reader should always know three things: where they are, why it matters, and what happens next. If they have to stop and ask any of those, the page has failed them, even if every fact on it is right.
I learned this late, and I learned it from being the reader. I sat with that draft as a stranger would. No memory of the day it described. No reason to be patient. Just a busy person with a screen, deciding in seconds whether to stay or leave. From that chair, the draft was not impressive. It was work.
Writing for that chair changed everything.

Three seconds, two seconds, one second

I keep a small rule now. I call it the 3-2-1 rule, because that is how much time a reader really gives you.
About three seconds to understand what you are. About two seconds to judge whether you are worth their time. About one second to decide what to do next.
Three, two, one. That is the whole doorway.
It sounds harsh. It is not. It is honest. Think of your own reading. You open a page. Your eyes take in the title and the first few lines. Before you have decided anything on purpose, you have already decided. Stay or go. Trust or doubt. Read on or close the tab.
So the title and the first three lines carry the whole promise. Not a summary of everything. A promise of one thing, said plainly enough that a smart person who knows nothing about your field gets it on the first read. If those lines need a second read, the post has lost people it will never meet. They did not argue. They just left, quietly, the way all of us leave pages that make us work too soon.
This is why I ask for more story, not less. A story is the fastest way through that doorway. A person in a moment. A problem in front of them. A question in the air. A reader gets that at once, with no effort. Explanation makes a reader climb. Story walks them in.

The rewrite

We wrote the post again. Same facts. Same numbers. Same ending.
This time it opened inside the moment. A run, dying fifteen steps in, cooked by its own heat. No settings yet. No background. Just the problem, alive on the page. And one question a reader could feel: why does a machine kill its own work?
The details came later, one at a time, each one earned by the story in front of it. The lesson was never announced. It arrived the way lessons arrive in real life — at the end of trying things, some of which failed. When the fix finally worked, the reader felt it work, because they had stood next to the failures first.
I read the new version once. Once was enough. Every sentence landed the first time. I approved it the same day, and I set one standing direction with it: even more story next time. The approved version was the floor, not the ceiling.
Nothing about the facts had improved. The facts were already right. What improved was the welcome. The writer was the same writer, an hour later, with a clearer law. This time he did the last step of the work. He carried the meaning across, so the reader did not have to.

A small test I now use

Before anything of mine goes out, it passes a short test. It fits on one screen. A small script runs the counting part. I run the human part out loud:
The first-read test
1. Read the title and the first three lines once. Can a smart stranger say what this is and why it matters — without reading them again?
2. Mark any sentence you had to read twice. Rewrite that one sentence. Do not defend it.
3. Find the first real moment — a person, a problem, a choice. If explanation comes before it, move the moment up.
4. Cut one heavy word in every paragraph where a plain word says the same thing.
If it fails any line, it is not ready — however true it is.
The script that ships with this post does the mechanical part: it counts long sentences, flags heavy words from a plain list you can edit, and checks that a real moment appears early. It cannot judge kindness. No script can. It can only point at the places where a reader is most likely to stumble, so a human can fix them with care.
That division is the honest one. Machines count. People welcome.

What kindness costs, and what it buys

Plain writing costs the writer more than fancy writing does. It is easy to be complete. It is hard to be clear. To write simply about a hard thing, you must understand it deeply. Deep enough to leave most of it out. And to know which small part the reader needs. Every vague sentence is a skipped judgment — billed to the reader.
What it buys is trust. A reader who never has to struggle with your pages learns something about you that no claim can teach: this person respects me. They did the work so I would not have to. Over many pages, that feeling becomes the whole relationship. People return to writers who are kind to them in this quiet way. They recommend them. They act on what they read, because they actually finished reading it.
I still write reports for a living. I still work with machines and numbers. None of that changed. What changed is the last step. Before I call anything finished, I sit in the stranger’s chair. I read it once. Three seconds, then two, then one.
If it welcomes me in, it ships. If it makes me work, it goes back — however true it is.
Correct is the entry fee. Kindness is the gift. A page owes its reader both.
