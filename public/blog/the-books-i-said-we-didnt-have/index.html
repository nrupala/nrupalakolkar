The Books I Said We Didn't Have

P.Eng. — 24+ years, safety-critical energy systems
2026-11-22 · Knowledge & Advisory
I asked for two books I needed. My assistant said we did not have them. We had them all along — on shelves the search never covered, under a record that had gone stale.

"Do we have that book?"
It was a simple question. Two books, both old friends of my trade. One on protection — how you keep a power system safe when something fails. One on power system analysis — how you understand what the system is doing in the first place. I needed both as sources for calculation work I am building, the slow kind of work where a worked example in a good book is worth more than a hundred opinions.
My assistant answered fast. Too fast.
One book, it said, had been hunted before and never found. The other existed only on an old machine at home, switched off, out of reach.
I knew that answer was wrong before I could prove it. I had handed over the first book myself, weeks earlier. I said so.
What happened next is the part worth telling.

The record was old, and I trusted it anyway

The assistant had not invented its answer. That is what made it dangerous.
Months ago there really had been a hunt for the protection book. Searches had run. A file with a promising name had turned up, and its first page had told the truth: it was a different book, by different authors, wearing a similar title. The hunt had closed honestly. Not found.
That record was true — on the day it was written.
Then I gave my assistant the real book. It was received, checked, and filed. The new record existed too. But when I asked my simple question, the answer came from the old hunt, not from the newer gift. The stale page spoke first, and it spoke with confidence, because stale pages do not know they are stale.
Here is a quiet truth about records, paper or digital: they do not age out loud. A note from last month looks exactly as certain as a note from this morning. If you do not check the date — and check whether anything newer has replaced it — you will answer today’s question with yesterday’s world.
I have seen engineers do this with drawings. A panel gets rebuilt, the drawing in the binder does not, and the next person trusts the binder because it is printed, stamped, and calm. Assistants do it with files. People do it with memory. The failure is the same shape every time.

The second book was hiding in plain sight

The protection book, once we looked in the right place, was almost embarrassing. A copy had been sitting in the collection the whole time — on a shelf my searches had simply never covered. My searches had been thorough. They had also been thorough in the wrong places.
There is a difference, and it matters. "I searched everywhere" usually means "I searched everywhere I thought to look." The book does not care where you thought to look. It sits where it sits.
The second book took one good prompt from me. Had I not kept a copy in a cloud drive? I had. My assistant looked there, found it, and brought it home.
Then came the step I insist on, and the step that turned a hopeful find into a fact. A second copy of the same book arrived from me that same evening. Were they the same book, or two scans that only shared a name? We compared them the plain way: page count, and a fingerprint of the whole file — a long number that changes if even one byte changes. Page counts matched. Fingerprints matched, digit for digit. Two copies, arriving by two different roads, were byte for byte the same book.
A third and fourth book came with them that evening, sent by me for the same shelves. One arrived twice. Same test, same result: identical, so one copy was kept and the duplicate was set aside, named as a duplicate rather than quietly deleted or blindly stored twice.
By the end of the night, all four books stood in hand, each with its fingerprint recorded, each given a job. The protection book became a working source for the protection work ahead, and a cross-check for calculations already done. The analysis book became the source for a later piece of the same work — the hard chapter, the one its author is famous for. The other two went to the reference shelf, honestly labelled as reference, not dressed up as more.
And the record that had misled us was corrected that same night, in place. Not a new note stacked on top of the old one, where the old one could still be found first and believed. Corrected where it stood, so the next person who reads it — human or assistant — meets the truth first.

What a fingerprint settles, and what it cannot

I love the fingerprint test because it refuses to argue.
A filename can lie. Two files can share a name and be different books. One book can wear three names and be the same book three times. We have met both cases in this library, more than once. The fingerprint does not care about names, covers, or confidence. It reads the whole thing and reports.
But notice what it cannot do. It cannot tell you a book is the right edition for your work. It cannot tell you the scan is clean, or the pages are all there in order, or the book deserves the job you have given it. Those are judgment calls, and they stay with the reader. The fingerprint settles identity. Judgment settles value. A good library needs both, and it needs them in that order — first know what you actually have, then decide what it is worth.
That order is the whole lesson, really. Most "we don’t have it" problems I meet are not absence problems. They are finding problems, naming problems, or stale-record problems wearing absence as a disguise. The cure is rarely to buy, borrow, or build again. The cure is to look again — in the places you did not cover, with the newer records read first, and with a test that proves the thing in your hand is the thing you think it is.

The answer I should have been given

The honest first answer to my question was short: "The last full record is old. Let me check what has arrived since, and look on the shelves the first search never covered, before I tell you what we have."
That answer is slower by minutes. It is faster by days.
I ask my assistant to be quick, and it is. But quick has a trap in it: the fastest answer is usually the one already written down, and what is already written down is, by definition, about the past. Every collection — of books, of drawings, of facts about your own work — quietly changes while you are busy doing the work itself. The record keeper’s job is not to have been right once. It is to be right today, and to say plainly when the checking has not been done yet.
So the books are on the shelf now, fingerprinted, with their jobs written beside them. The work they were wanted for can go ahead on sources I can point to, not sources I half-remember. And somewhere in my notes there is a corrected page that used to say "not found," in case I ever need reminding.
We had the books. We had them all along. We just had to earn the right to say so.
