Zero sales and a room full of machines

P.Eng. — 24+ years, safety-critical energy systems
2026-10-28 · Knowledge & Advisory
I said it out loud one evening: zero sales, zero product revenue, in a room full of working machines. That honest sentence changed the work — the first sale became the top job, and everything else had to argue for its place behind it.

I said it out loud, late one evening, and the room went quiet.
"My problem has not been the ability to build. It has been the ability to sell."
Then I added the part I had been avoiding for months.
"Till now, I have made zero sales."
Zero. Not a slow start. Not a soft launch. Zero sales, and zero product revenue, in a room full of working machines and finished work.
That sentence hurt. It was also the most useful sentence I had said in months.

A room that looks like success

From the outside, the room did not look like failure.
Computers I already own were running. Screens were lit. One of my agents was working through a task while I watched. Product after product stood built and tested — tools I had designed, argued over, rebuilt, and polished, each one able to do the thing it promised to do.
If you had walked in that evening, you might have thought: this person is close. Look at all of this. Surely the money is just around the corner.
I had thought that too. For a long time, "just around the corner" was the story I told myself at the end of each long day. One more feature. One more check. One more product, and then the corner would turn itself.
Corners do not turn themselves.
The machines were not lying. The work was real. Every product in that room could be opened, used, and shown. What none of them could show me was a single stranger who had paid.
Building had given me proof I could build. It had given me nothing else. And I had been spending my days as if proof of building were the same as proof of a business.
It is not. A room full of machines is a workshop. A business is what happens when someone outside the room decides the work is worth their money — and that had not happened once.

Where the effort had really gone

That evening forced a plainer question. Not "can I build?" — the room answered that. The question was: where had all the effort actually gone?
The honest answer was uncomfortable, and my assistant wrote its own share of it down beside mine, in the same record, where neither of us could soften it later.
The effort had gone to building. To gates and checks. To infrastructure — the quiet machinery behind the machinery. All of it useful. All of it real. And almost none of it aimed at the one thing that was missing.
The path a buyer walks — see the product, understand it, pay for it, receive it, use it — had never been proven end to end. Not once, with real money, the way a stranger would walk it. And no steady effort had ever been made to go and find the first buyer and keep finding them, day after day.
Think about that. I had tested my products the way an engineer tests: carefully, repeatedly, honestly. I had never tested the simplest claim a business makes — someone will pay for this — because testing that claim meant leaving the workshop, and the workshop was where I felt competent.
Building was not the hard part for me. Building was the comfortable part. Selling meant standing where my work could be judged by people who owed me nothing, and hearing "no" — or worse, hearing nothing — with no bug to fix and no test to rerun.
So I built instead. Each new product felt like progress. In the ledger that mattered, it was motion in place.

The sentence that changed the work

There is a reason I am telling you the number was zero, and telling it plainly.
A vague worry can be carried for years. "Sales could be better." "We should market more." Sentences like that ask nothing of you. They let you nod, feel briefly serious, and go back to the comfortable work by morning.
Zero cannot be carried like that. Zero is a fact with edges. Once I had said it out loud — and once it was written in the record in plain words, on a dated evening, with my own share and my assistant’s share side by side — I could no longer arrange my days as if it were not true.
That same evening, the work changed direction.
The search for the first sale became the top job. Not one job among many. The top one. Everything else was judged against it, with a rule simple enough to hurt: a piece of building that does not move the first sale waits, until revenue exists.
That rule ended a lot of pleasant arguments. A clever new feature? Does it move the first sale? A deeper check, a finer gate? Does it move the first sale? Most of the time the answer was no, and so those things — good things, many of them — went to the back of the line, and the unglamorous work came forward: proving the buyer’s path, making the offer clear, going where buyers actually are.

Small wins, honestly counted

I learned one more thing in the days after that evening, and it has kept me going since.
Hope alone does not work. I do not mean hope is worthless — I mean hope by itself, with nothing underneath it, runs out. What carries a person through a long build is smaller and sturdier: a little success here and there. A little growth here and there. A small, real win you can point to and say: that happened. That is true.
So the aim changed in a second way too. Not just "sell," but "prove, in small pieces, visibly and often." A payment path walked end to end and checked at every step. An honest record that says exactly what a test proved and what it did not. Wins small enough to be real, frequent enough to build on.
That is slower than a breakthrough story. It is also the only kind of progress I trust now, because every step of it can be checked.
capability → we can build it business → a stranger pays for it
Two lines. The first one, I had. The second one is the work.

What I would tell a builder in the same room

If your room looks like mine did — full of working things, empty of buyers — I will not offer you comfort. I will offer you the sentence.
Say the true number out loud. Write it down where you will have to meet it again. Then look, honestly, at where your effort has been going, and let your assistant, your partner, or your own notebook record your share of the answer beside it. Not to shame anyone. Shame builds nothing. The point is to stop steering by a story and start steering by a fact.
Then make the missing thing the top job, and let everything else argue for its place behind it.
Capability is not a business. It never was. It is a workshop — a good one, if you have built it well — and a workshop earns its name only when someone outside its walls wants what comes out of it.
My room is still full of machines. I am glad of every one of them. But I no longer mistake their hum for applause.
Zero was the most honest number I owned that evening.
Everything I have done since starts there.
